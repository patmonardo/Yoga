# Minds Made from the Mere I-Am

## A report on Yoga Sutra IV.4 and Fichte

## Purpose

This report tests Yoga Sutra IV.4 alongside the corresponding passage of
Fichte's sixteenth lecture in the 1804 *Science of Knowing*.

IV.2 gave us nature's fullness as the power of transformation. IV.3 limited
the external occasion to opening a barrier. IV.4 now introduces a new
problem: constructed minds. If nature supplies the real power and no external
agent creates it, from what do these apparently produced centers of
consciousness arise?

## The Sutra

`nirmana-cittani-asmita-matra`

The received expression is commonly read with an ablative ending:
`asmita-matrat`, "from mere I-am-ness." The local text's shortened form does
not alter the governing construction.

### A close translation

> Constructed minds arise from mere I-am-ness.

### A translation for the Fichtean commentary

> Minds that are constructed proceed from the bare form, "I am."

This translation preserves the compression of the Sutra:

- `nirmana`: making, fashioning, constructing;
- `cittani`: minds or centers of Citta, in the plural;
- `asmita`: I-am-ness, the form in which consciousness posits itself as an I;
- `matra`: mere, bare, or nothing but.

The word `matra` is decisive. The Sutra does not yet say that the constructed
minds arise from the whole concrete person. It reduces their source to the
minimal form of subjective identity: I am.

```text
                    constructed mind 1
                   /
the bare I-am ---- constructed mind 2
                   \
                    constructed mind 3
```

The one form can be repeated across many constructions without thereby
becoming many independent ultimate selves.

## Fichte's question

Fichte has asserted that if being is constructed, it must construct itself.
But he immediately stops and asks what right consciousness has to make this
assertion. The claim first appears only hypothetically:

> If being is constructed ...

How could anyone prove that being is constructed? The immediate answer seems
to be: through consciousness. Yet Fichte has already denied that a declaration
of consciousness is valid merely because consciousness makes it. The validity
of consciousness is itself what must be established.

This produces a distinction between two constructions:

```text
real self-construction       being is of itself, from itself, through itself

ideal self-construction      consciousness pictures being as self-constructing
```

The first is living `esse`: being is only in its own self-construction. The
second is an ideal or imaginal construction presented by consciousness. It is
a reconstruction in the image of the I.

Fichte refuses to identify them prematurely. That refusal is his commentary
on `matra`, the "mere" in mere I-am-ness.

## Fichte as Bhasya

Read as a Bhasya on IV.4, Fichte distinguishes the genesis of a constructed
mind from the real genesis of being. Consciousness can reproduce the form of
self-construction within itself. It can say "I construct" and can project
this form upon being. But the mere form of the I does not by itself prove that
its image is the real movement of being.

The correspondence is:

| Yoga Sutra IV.4 | Fichte's lecture |
|---|---|
| `nirmana`, construction | the ideal or imaginal construction |
| `cittani`, minds in the plural | factical acts and forms of consciousness |
| `asmita`, I-am-ness | the "we" that appears to perform the construction |
| `matra`, mere or bare | the restriction of this construction to hypothetical validity |

The constructed mind is not false simply because it is constructed. Its
mistake would be to take its own form as the independent ground of reality.
It says:

```text
I construct being;
therefore my construction is the principle of being.
```

Fichte interrupts the inference. The I that constructs philosophically is
not free-standing. If its act is true, it is true only insofar as the I
coincides with living being. The constructed Citta is a vehicle of the real
genesis, not its creator.

## The force of the plural

`Cittani` is plural. Fichte's analysis helps us avoid treating this plurality
as a collection of unrelated substances. Many ideal constructions can arise,
and each can carry the form "I am." Yet their common subjective form does not
make each one an absolute source.

```text
many constructions       one repeated form
Citta 1  --\             /-- I am
Citta 2  ---- asmitā ----+--- I am
Citta 3  --/             \-- I am
```

The plurality belongs to appearance. The bare identity that informs the
plurality is one formal principle. But even that principle remains only
formal until its relation to living being is genetically demonstrated.

This guards against two opposite errors:

1. Treating every constructed mind as an independently absolute creator.
2. Treating the constructed mind as an unreal nothing with no cognitive
   validity.

Fichte holds the middle. Conscious construction is real as an act of
appearance, but its truth depends on whether its principle can be derived
from being rather than merely asserted by the I.

## From Mahat to Asmita

The sequence from IV.2 to IV.4 can now be stated more sharply.

Your formulation of IV.2 was:

> Nature armed with Mahat creates by filling in forms.

That gives nature an intelligent power of articulation without placing a
separate artificer outside it. IV.4 shows what happens when this formative
power assumes the minimal subjective shape of "I am." Citta can now be
constructed as a center in which nature's intelligence appears to belong to
an individual agent.

```text
Prakrti armed with Mahat     formative intelligence
             |
             v
        Asmita               the form "I am"
             |
             v
     Nirmana-Cittani         constructed centers of mind
```

Fichte supplies the necessary warning: do not mistake the center in which the
construction appears for the absolute source of the construction. The "I"
is where genesis is reconstructed for consciousness. Whether this
reconstruction expresses real genesis remains a question of validity.

## The analytic-synthetic principle

Fichte says that the real task is not simply to choose between a real and an
ideal construction. The task is to establish genetically the
analytic-synthetic principle grounding the distinction.

This is important for the Sutra. `Asmita` is analytic because every
constructed consciousness repeats the immediate identity "I am I." It is
synthetic because the I also constructs, differentiates, and relates an
objectified content. The bare I is thus the formal hinge between identity and
construction.

But a formal hinge is not yet a demonstrated ground. Fichte grants the
distinction between real and ideal construction only hypothetically until the
principle generating it has itself been deduced.

IV.4 therefore marks the birth of a problem, not its final solution:

> How can the form "I am" generate a constructed field of consciousness
> without falsely making itself the independent creator of being?

## Does the comparison hold?

The fit is unusually strong at the level of form:

1. The Sutra identifies constructed minds.
2. It derives them from the minimal form of I-am-ness.
3. Fichte distinguishes an ideal, imaginal self-construction performed by
   consciousness.
4. He restricts this construction to hypothetical validity until its ground
   has been genetically demonstrated.

The comparison would become excessive if `asmita` were simply equated with
Fichte's absolute I, or if Yoga's constructed minds were said to be identical
with philosophical representations. In the Yoga setting, the Sutra belongs
to an account of yogically constructed Cittas and their coordination. Fichte
is investigating the truth of consciousness's claim to construct being.

The systematic connection is nevertheless exact enough for commentary:
both distinguish the constructed form of consciousness from the real power
whose genesis that form claims to express.

## Provisional result

The simple translation is:

> Constructed minds arise from mere I-am-ness.

Fichte's Bhasya is:

> Consciousness can construct being in the image of its own "I am," but this
> ideal construction must not yet be confused with being's real
> self-construction. Its truth has to be genetically established.

The resulting Dharma thesis is:

> The I is not initially the creator of form. It is the minimal form in which
> nature's formative power appears as a construction for consciousness.

IV.2 supplied the fullness that forms. IV.3 removed the fantasy of an
external producer. IV.4 now locates the appearance of production within the
bare I. The Doctrine of Essence has begun to explain why the agency of nature
appears as the agency of a constructed mind.
