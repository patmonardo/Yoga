# Error and Reversal in the Kośa

## A companion essay for Yoga Sūtra I.8

Yoga I.8 defines *viparyaya* as *mithyājñānam atadrūpapratiṣṭham*: mistaken cognition established in a form that is not the object's own. The Kośa gives us something further to ask: when a mistake becomes a view, how does it govern perception and consciousness? These are related inquiries, not identical definitions.

## Four determinate reversals

In the *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya*'s account of latent tendencies, a question about reversal (*viparyāsa*) brings out four cases: taking the impermanent as permanent, the painful as pleasant, the impure as pure, and the non-self as self. Each attributes a determinate predicate to an object in opposition to its character. The Kośa connects these four reversals with three kinds of view: an extreme view of permanence, attachment to views concerning pleasure and purity, and *satkāyadṛṣṭi* concerning self. These assignments belong to this argument, not to every use of *viparyaya*. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 283, lines 5–10](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

Why do these count as *viparyāsas* rather than simply as afflictions? The Kośa gives three marks: inversion with respect to the object, decisive judgment (*nitīraṇa*), and superimposition (*samāropa*). The combination matters. A passing mistake may be corrected on looking again; a settled view superimposes a determination on the object and sustains it. The passage excludes some false views from this narrower group because they proceed by negation rather than superimposition. Its *viparyāsa* therefore does more precise work here than a broad English “error.” [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 283, lines 14–21](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

## View, perception, and consciousness

The Bhāṣya next asks about a sūtra that speaks of reversal in *saṃjñā*, *citta*, and *dṛṣṭi*. Its answer makes view the governing reversal: associated perception and consciousness are called reversed through its influence. This is a claim about a specific structure of associated mental factors. The Bhāṣya then discusses disagreement over which reversals persist after stream entry; the account should not be made into an uncontested Kośa doctrine. [Kośabhāṣya, source pp. 283–284, lines 21–16](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

Elsewhere the Kośa records a dispute about whether a certain mistaken cognition counts as a view. The Abhidharmikas call it *mithyājñāna*, not *dṛṣṭi*; the response asks how that distinction has been established. This prevents a simple equation of error, false cognition, and wrong view. The status of the error must be argued in context. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 287, lines 17–20](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

## Returning to I.8

The Kośa helps us ask of Yoga's *viparyaya*: what is the object, which alien form has been attributed to it, and what supports that attribution? Is this a passing misapprehension or an organizing view? If a view governs, which accompanying factors carry the reversal? Yoga I.8 itself says only that mistaken cognition is established in a form other than the object's own. The Kośa differentiates ways such an error can take hold.

I.7 made the grounds of valid cognition explicit. I.8 asks what happens when cognition settles on an alien form. Both remain movements of consciousness under I.5–6. The relation of error, correction, and *nirodha* can be developed later; this comparison establishes the first distinctions.
