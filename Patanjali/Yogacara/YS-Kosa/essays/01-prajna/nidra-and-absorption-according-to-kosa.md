# Nidrā, Absorption, and the Support of Absence

## A Kośa companion essay for Yoga Sūtra I.10

Yoga I.10 says: *abhāvapratyayālambanā vṛttir nidrā* — sleep is a movement of consciousness supported by an absence-presentation. Its force lies in the word *ālambana*. I.9 made us ask whether a word-led image has a corresponding *vastu*. I.10 asks how a movement can be supported when ordinary determinate objects have withdrawn. The inquiry is about **absorption and its support**, not a survey of the physiology or benefits of deep sleep.

The sūtra itself calls sleep a *vṛtti*. Its *abhāva-pratyaya* gives that movement a determinate support; “absence” cannot be read as the nonexistence of all conscious operation. I.11's account of memory supplies a further test: on waking, an absence-supported condition can be retained and taken up in a new cognition. This reading follows the sūtras' sequence without requiring ordinary sleep to be a completed *nirodha*. [Yoga I.10–11](../../../Sources/Yoga.txt).

## Kośa I: contraction has more than one sense

The Kośa does not simply supply a Buddhist synonym for Yoga's *nidrā*. It distinguishes *styāna* and *middha*. In the discussion of associated factors, *styāna* is described through bodily and mental heaviness and lack of workability. Elsewhere *middha* is described as a contraction of consciousness (*cittābhisaṃkṣepa*) that makes bodily maintenance difficult. These are determinate conditions of a psychophysical continuum, not an analysis of I.10's compound *abhāvapratyayālambanā*. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 56, lines 8–10](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt); [source p. 312, lines 17–19](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

The Kośa also refuses to make *middha* uniformly unwholesome. In VAK 2.30 the Bhāṣya says it is compatible with the listed mental factors because it can be wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate. In the discussion of the hindrances, however, *styāna–middha* functions as a pair that draws consciousness toward sinking (*laya*) and impedes the work of contemplative discernment. The ethical and functional descriptions have different scopes. “Sleepiness” is thus too blunt a word to decide in advance what an episode does. [Kośabhāṣya, source pp. 58–59, lines 26–2](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt); [source pp. 318–319, lines 8–12](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

## Kośa II: collectedness is not dulling

A sharper comparison appears when the Kośa distinguishes kinds of contraction. One account calls a wholesome consciousness *saṃkṣipta*, “collected” or “drawn together,” because of the gathering of its object-support (*ālambanābhisaṃkṣepa*). A rival explanation calls consciousness contracted when it is associated with *middha*; the Bhāṣya rejects that explanation in this setting. Its point is exact: **concentration around an object and dull contraction cannot be identified merely because both reduce the spread of activity**. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 396, lines 19–23](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

VAK 8.1 then defines meditative attainment in general as *śubhaikāgrya*, wholesome one-pointedness. The Bhāṣya asks what one-pointedness is and answers *ekālambanatā*: having one object-support. It insists that *samādhi* is a distinct mental factor by which consciousnesses become one-pointed, not merely another name for those consciousnesses. The Kośa therefore gives us a positive criterion of absorption: a gathered relation to an *ālambana*, qualified by its mode and value. [Kośabhāṣya, source pp. 432–433, lines 14–2](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

This Kośa distinction makes I.10 harder and more interesting. Sleep too has a support, but Yoga names it an *abhāva-pratyaya*. The fact of having an *ālambana* is not yet sufficient to make the movement meditative attainment. We must determine **what supports it, how cognition is gathered, and what becomes possible on emerging from it**. In that precise respect, *nidrā* and *samāpatti* are comparable without becoming interchangeable.

## Kośa III: even cessation is a differentiated attainment

The Kośa's two special attainments, *asaṃjñisamāpatti* and *nirodhasamāpatti*, make the comparison still more exact. The Bhāṣya treats both in one discussion of the stopping of *citta* and associated factors, but distinguishes their conditions and purposes. The first is undertaken with an idea of escape; the second with an idea of peaceful abiding. It then disputes whether the latter is wholly without mind and whether these attainments are distinct realities or designations for a conditioned interruption. Their Sanskrit arguments must be read on their own terms; neither attainment is simply ordinary sleep. [Kośabhāṣya, source pp. 68–70, lines 28–8](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt); [source pp. 72–73, lines 17–11](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

This matters because an absence of ordinary cognitive activity does not specify the state by itself. Entry, sustaining condition, purpose, and return all matter. The Kośa will not let “no ordinary object appears” carry the whole philosophical burden.

## Our Bhavacakra hypothesis: a daily gift of return

From our Yoga–Kośa standpoint, nightly sleep can be read as a recurring Bhavacakra moment: the world of selected objects loosens, consciousness is gathered around absence, and on waking a world becomes determinable again. “A daily free *samādhi* reset gift from the clouds” names the felt generosity of that recurring release and return. It is a **project image of the cycle**, not a claim that ordinary sleep fulfills the Kośa's *śubhaikāgrya* or Yoga's *samādhi*.

The illuminating question is whether the release can be known and worked through rather than merely undergone. Yoga I.38 later says *svapnanidrājñānālambanaṃ vā*: knowledge of dream or sleep may itself become an *ālambana* for steadiness. That later sūtra gives the present metaphor a disciplined route. The sleep event is one thing; cognition taking its knowledge as support is another. [Yoga I.38](../../../Sources/Yoga.txt).

For the present I.10 study, this is the governing comparison: **absence-supported movement; dull contraction; wholesome one-pointed absorption; and deliberate cessation are distinct determinations**. The Kośa makes their boundaries visible. Yoga asks what the fourth *vṛtti* is and, later, how even knowledge of it can support a lucid turn toward *samāpatti*.
