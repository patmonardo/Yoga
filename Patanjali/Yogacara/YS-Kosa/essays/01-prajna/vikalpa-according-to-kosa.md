# Vikalpa and the Question of Its Object

## A companion essay for Yoga Sūtra I.9

Yoga I.9 says: *śabdajñānānupātī vastuśūnyo vikalpaḥ* — *vikalpa* follows verbal cognition and lacks a corresponding thing. The Kośa also speaks of *vikalpa*, but gives the word a wider technical range. Reading it first prevents us from treating all differentiation as empty fiction. It also makes Yoga's narrower description more exact: what, precisely, is missing when a verbal construction is *vastuśūnya*?

## Three kinds in the Kośa

The *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya* asks why the five sensory cognitions (*pañca vijñānakāyāḥ*) are called *avikalpaka*, “without vikalpa,” when they are accompanied by *vitarka* and *vicāra*. Its answer reports a threefold classification: *svabhāvavikalpa* (intrinsic differentiation), *abhinirūpaṇavikalpa* (examining or determining differentiation), and *anusmaraṇavikalpa* (recollective differentiation). Sensory cognition has the first but lacks the latter two. Hence “without vikalpa” is relative. The Bhāṣya compares the expression to calling a one-footed horse “footless”: the normal complement is missing, not every foot. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 22, lines 18–22](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

The identifications are precise. *Svabhāvavikalpa* is *vitarka*. *Abhinirūpaṇavikalpa* is mental *prajñā* associated with mental cognition (*manovijñāna*) when unconcentrated (*vyagra*, glossed *asamāhita*). *Anusmaraṇavikalpa* is mental *smṛti*, whether concentrated or unconcentrated. These distinguish kinds and qualifications of cognitive operation; the passage does not arrange them as successive stages. Nor does it call every exercise of *prajñā* the examining kind. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 22, lines 22–26](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

This Kośa account immediately rules out a blanket gloss of *vikalpa* as “fantasy” or “falsehood.” A sensory cognition can possess intrinsic *vikalpa*; mental examination and recollection have their own roles. The question for Yoga is which of these operations, if any, is being characterized by I.9's verbal dependence and lack of a corresponding *vastu*. The common term does not authorize identifying Yoga's whole category with the Kośa's threefold classification.

## Object support is a different question

The very next Kośa verse says that the seven *cittadhātus*—five sensory cognition Principles, the mental cognition Principle, and the mind Principle—are *sālambana*, object-supported, because they apprehend an object (*viṣayagrahaṇāt*). Part of the *dharmadhātu*, the Dharma Principle, is object-supported too, insofar as it consists of mental factors. Here *dhātu* is rendered “Principle”; “domain” and “realm” remain companion terms when context calls for their classificatory or cosmological senses. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 23, lines 1–6](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

That does not refute Yoga's *vastuśūnya*. “Object-supported cognition” in the Kośa and “empty of a corresponding thing” in Yoga answer different questions. In our Yoga–Kośa reconstruction, the object supports **cognition in its act of apprehending**; it is not thereby posited as the ground that supports consciousness as such. Nor does object-support certify the thing posited by a word: the cognitive act can have a presented content while the corresponding *vastu* is absent. **The object-support of cognition and the thing claimed in its content must be distinguished.** This is a comparative determination from the two texts, not a definition stated by either one in those words.

## Our project determination: image, Principle, Rule

Consider the mouse on my desk as I reach for it. Consciousness presents an image; cognition takes the mouse as *ālambana* when that presented content supports an act of apprehension. Its availability as *ālambana* can come and go as attention and action change. The image alone does not yet determine the mouse as the being for this act. In our Organon reading, *dhātu* as **Principle** supplies the determination of what it is, while *indriya* as **Rule** governs the selection and possible response. This is the proposed **Principle : Rule** relation, not an additional proposition in VAK 1.34 or Yoga I.9.

The transition can be written provisionally as **image → object-supported cognition → Principle : Rule determination → actual model for action**. “Image without Principle” means that the image is not yet *principially determined* in this act; it does not erase the conditions of the sensory cognition that presents it. From the first-person standpoint, the actual mouse available to me is the mouse so determined within consciousness. I cannot step outside consciousness to compare it with a second, unconstrued mouse. In that sense the passage to an actual model is also the passage to an actual object *for me*. This is a Kantian project interpretation of empirical actuality, not a claim that the Kośa explicitly teaches Kant's account of objects.

This also sharpens *vastuśūnya*. A word-led image may enter cognition and be selected under a rule without the corresponding being being established. The task is to determine whether the proposed model has the *vastu* it claims, not to infer that being from the mere occurrence of an image or word. We can revise this account as the later sūtras test what Principle and Rule actually accomplish.

## The precise return to Yoga

I.9 picks out a movement led by *śabdajñāna*: the verbal cognition is operative, yet the corresponding *vastu* is absent. Unlike I.8's *viparyaya*, the defining problem is not simply that an existing object has been taken under the wrong form. A verbal construction can articulate a content without supplying the thing it seems to name. Nor does *vastuśūnya* make the movement nothing at all: the words and the cognition occur. The absence concerns the corresponding thing.

The Kośa helps us resist two shortcuts. First, *vikalpa* is not inherently error; differentiation can be intrinsic, examining, or recollective. Second, cognition's having an object-support does not certify the represented thing. The Yoga sūtra gives the stricter case in which word-led cognition outruns that thing. Our later study of I.42 can ask how word, meaning, and cognition become mixed in *savitarkā samāpatti*. The earlier Fichte and Kant discussions can then be brought into the essay layer without being substituted for the Kośa's three distinctions or for I.9's own criterion.
