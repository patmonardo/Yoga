# The Transformations of Citta Are Always Known

## A report on Yoga Sūtra IV.18, Vyāsa, and Fichte

## The Sūtra

`sadā jñātāś citta-vṛttayas tat-prabhoḥ puruṣasyāpariṇāmitvāt`

### Simple translation

> The transformations of Citta are always known because Puruṣa, their
> governing knower, does not transform with them.

`Sadā jñātāḥ` means always known. `Citta-vṛttayaḥ` are the movements,
operations, or determinate transformations of Citta. `Tat-prabhu` calls
Puruṣa their master, governor, or presiding principle. `Apariṇāmitva` is
non-transformation or invariance.

The last term must be handled carefully. The Sūtra need not mean that knowing
is a dead substance incapable of life. It establishes that the knowing of a
Citta-transformation cannot itself vary in exactly the same way as the
transformation known.

```text
Citta-vṛtti 1  →  Citta-vṛtti 2  →  Citta-vṛtti 3
       \               |               /
        \              |              /
             always knowable
        through an invariant knowing
```

The simple point is:

> Because the determinate states of Citta vanish, their knownness requires a
> principle that does not vanish with each state.

## Why this follows IV.17

IV.17 explained how a Thing becomes known: it colors Citta, and Citta assumes
a corresponding determination.

That immediately raises another question. If Citta is transformed by every
known Thing, how is this changing Citta itself known?

```text
Thing
  ↓ colors
Citta-vṛtti
  ↓
what knows this transformation?
```

The object cannot answer the question; it is what colors Citta. Nor can this
particular Vṛtti simply certify itself without remainder, because it is the
changing determination whose knownness is at issue.

IV.18 introduces an invariant pole: Puruṣa does not undergo the succession of
Citta-vṛttis and therefore their transformations are always known.

## Vyāsa's Bhāṣya

Vyāsa gives a concise argument. Suppose Puruṣa transformed just as Citta
does. Then Citta-vṛttis would sometimes be known and sometimes unknown to it,
exactly as external objects are known or unknown to Citta.

But the states of Citta are always known. This constant knownness allows us
to infer that Puruṣa, the governor of mind, is not transformed with them.

```text
if Puruṣa transformed like Citta:

Citta-vṛtti        sometimes known / sometimes unknown


but:

Citta-vṛtti        always known
                         ↓
Puruṣa inferred as invariant knower
```

Vyāsa's inference is technically strong. His metaphysical separation of
Puruṣa from Citta is not yet forced by it. What the argument strictly
requires is an invariant condition of knownness that is not one passing
Vṛtti among others.

## Appearance is vanishing

In the Sphere of Necessity, every appearance is conditioned and therefore
vanishing. Citta itself belongs to this objective movement. It is colored,
transformed, and succeeded by further determinations.

Yet vanishing cannot appear merely to another vanishing state with no
continuity of knowing. Otherwise each Vṛtti would disappear without its
succession ever becoming manifest as a succession.

```text
only isolated Vṛttis:

A        B        C
no experienced transition or unity


Vṛttis within invariant knowing:

A   →    B   →    C
one manifest succession
```

Thus IV.18 does not stop perpetual dependent origination. It states the
condition under which that perpetual arising and vanishing can be known.

## Fichte's Bhāṣya

Fichte returns to the presupposition that Light is an absolute `from`. Its
legitimacy was demonstrated because we ourselves were the knowing in which
the presupposition became manifest.

But this `We` is no longer the empirical We that freely selects a premise.
It is the We that merges into Light and becomes identical with it. Fichte
uses severe language: in the science of knowing, the freely reflecting We
disappears into Light.

```text
empirical We
freely posits and reconstructs premises
              ↓
merges into the actuality of knowing
              ↓
Light knows itself as an absolute “from”
```

This gives Fichte a distinction between lower empirical knowing and higher
genetic knowing. The former reconstructs conditioned relations. The latter
is knowing's insight into its own genesis.

## Qualitative invariance, not dead immobility

Fichte has already said that Light is genesis. He now also isolates its pure,
bare oneness, which he calls inconceivable and categorical.

These claims appear contradictory only if invariance means inactivity:

```text
Light is absolute genesis

Light is qualitatively one
```

For Fichte, they are one truth. Light changes neither into one of its terms
nor into the objective `from` it posits. It remains qualitatively identical
while permeating genesis and all its disjunctions.

This gives a powerful interpretation of `apariṇāmitva`:

> The knower is invariant not because it stands outside genesis, but because
> it remains the same qualitative knowing throughout genesis.

The Citta-vṛttis change as determinate contents and acts. Light does not
become one more Vṛtti alongside them. It is their manifestness as known.

## The hypothetical `should`

Fichte analyzes the form used during the ascent:

> If knowing should be, then ...

The `should` does not assert that the consequent already exists. It demands
that being arise from an adequate principle. It is therefore a postulation
of genesis.

Fichte then detects a disjunction within genesis:

```text
ideal genesis                     real genesis

the “should” postulates           being actually comes forth
that being must arise             from its principle
```

These are not two independent geneses. They are two aspects within one
absolute genesis. Their eventual resolution will show how knowing can be
both the demand for genesis and the actual coming-forth of its object.

## Two aspects of one knowing

Fichte reaches the formulation most relevant to IV.18. Two points of origin
or two knowings can appear:

- the knowing that asks, “If knowing should be ...”;
- the knowing that would be present when its principle is fulfilled.

But they cannot be two fundamentally distinct things. We are the absolute
Light, and this Light is genesis. The disjunction is therefore internal to
one knowing that remains one throughout all its aspects.

```text
reflecting knowing             fulfilled knowing
         \                         /
          \                       /
             one qualitative Light
```

This is the Fichtean alternative to placing an external spectator behind
Citta. The knower does not multiply whenever knowing adopts a different
aspect.

## Yoga and Fichte

| Yoga IV.18 | Fichte |
|---|---|
| changing Citta-vṛttis | disjoined and impermanent forms of knowing |
| Vṛttis are always known | Light permeates every genesis as manifestness |
| Puruṣa does not transform with them | qualitative Light remains one throughout disjunction |
| the governor is not another Vṛtti | the empirical We disappears into higher genetic knowing |

Fichte lets us preserve the force of Yoga's inference without immediately
externalizing Puruṣa:

> Puruṣa names knowing in its qualitative invariance; Citta-vṛttis name its
> determinate transformations within Appearance.

This remains a proposed Yoga-System reading, not Vyāsa's final dualism.

## The second triadic Pariṇāma question

The sequence now displays a possible second triad:

```text
objective side                         knowing side

Guṇa-differentiation                   ideal postulation
one Pariṇāma                           real genesis
Vastu appears                          invariant knownness
```

But we should not yet identify these structures. Here we remain in the
Sphere of Necessity. Citta-vṛttis and their objects arise within immediate
objective Appearance. Jñānapada will repeat the structure in the Sphere of
Freedom, where the truth of transformation is present in the Concept.

For now IV.18 establishes only the invariant condition required for the
objective succession to be known.

## Practical Reason and the Kośa

The practical significance is considerable. If Citta were identical with
each passing Vṛtti, a being would be imprisoned in whatever state presently
colored it:

```text
pain arises       →       I am nothing but pain
aversion arises   →       I am nothing but aversion
confusion arises  →       I am nothing but confusion
```

IV.18 makes discrimination possible. Every Vṛtti is known, and therefore no
Vṛtti exhausts the knowing in which it appears.

Kośa practice can analyze suffering into conditioned Dharmas precisely
because those Dharmas are knowable as arising and vanishing. The
Immeasurables then transform the practical response without requiring an
eternalization of the passing state.

```text
suffering is fully known
          +
suffering is not the whole of knowing
          =
Karuṇā without engulfment
```

This is not escape from All This. It is the stability required to remain
present to All This as it perpetually arises and vanishes.

## Provisional result

IV.17 said that a Thing is known when it colors Citta. IV.18 now explains why
the resulting transformations of Citta do not vanish into unknowability:

> The transformations of Citta are always known because Puruṣa, their
> governing knower, does not transform with them.

Vyāsa infers an invariant knower from the constant knownness of mental states.
Fichte lets us interpret this invariance dynamically rather than as dead
separation:

> One qualitative Light remains itself throughout the genesis and
> disjunction of its determinate forms.

Appearance can therefore vanish perpetually while its vanishing remains
manifest. IV.19 must next ask whether Citta itself supplies this illumination
or whether, as something seen, it lacks self-luminosity.
