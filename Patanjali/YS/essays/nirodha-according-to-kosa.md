# Nirodha According to the Kośa

## A Kośa companion essay for Yoga Sūtra I.12

Yoga I.12, *abhyāsavairāgyābhyāṃ tannirodhaḥ*, gathers the sequence of I.5–11 into a practical question. The five movements have been named and defined. Their *nirodha* is “by practice and dispassion.” The dual instrumental matters: Yoga gives two means for one restraint, not two disconnected remedies. The received Bhāṣya asks what the means of restraining these movements are, then gives the image of *citta* as a river flowing in two directions. The current descending toward non-discernment and saṃsāra is checked by dispassion; the current toward discernment and *kaivalya* is opened by practice of discerning vision. The Bhāṣya concludes that restraint of the movements depends on both. [Yoga I.12 with Bhāṣya](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutra-with-bhasya-sanskrit/d/doc1216809.html).

This already rules out a merely negative reading of restraint. There is an opening as well as a checking in the commentator's explanation. Yet the sūtra itself has not given a program for each of the five movements. I.13–16 will say more about the two means. We should preserve this interval rather than fill it with a later scheme and then attribute that scheme to I.12.

## The Kośa's exact distinction

The Kośa places two *nirodhas* among its unconditioned dharmas. *Pratisaṃkhyānirodha* is disjunction from conditioned, outflowing dharmas attained through *pratisaṃkhyā*, a special discriminative *prajñā* concerning the noble truths. Its disjunctions are particular, not one blanket cancellation of every bond. *Apratisaṃkhyānirodha* is another kind of non-arising: a future dharma fails to arise because the requisite conditions are lacking (*pratyayavaikalya*), without that discriminative attainment. [Kośabhāṣya, source pp. 3–4](../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

The Kośa's distinction is valuable precisely because the two non-arising relations differ. Discriminative understanding and missing conditions cannot be silently exchanged. Nor can the pair be mapped directly onto Yoga's *abhyāsa* and *vairāgya*. In Kośa they classify unconditioned *nirodhas* and are debated as such; Yoga I.12 names two means of restraining *citta-vṛttis*. The comparison is methodological: when we say a movement has ceased to govern, we ask what was discerned and what sustaining condition no longer operated. The Bhāṣya's two currents give the Yoga side of that question, while the Kośa prevents the answer from collapsing into a single vague idea of “stopping.”

## Five movements and the two means

I.6 presents five names; I.7–11 defines them in turn. *Pramāṇa* is valid cognition; *viparyaya* is mistaken cognition established in an alien form; *vikalpa* follows verbal cognition without the corresponding *vastu*; *nidrā* is a movement supported by a presentation of absence; *smṛti* is non-loss of an experienced object. I.5 says the movements can be afflicted or unafflicted. I.12 refers back to their restraint, without assigning a separate practice to each of the five.

The Kośa sharpens each distinction without making the five Yoga kinds into five Kośa *caittas*. Its analysis of warrant tests perception, inference, and testimony; its account of inverted views probes entrenched error; its treatment of *vikalpa* discriminates intrinsic, examining, and recollective operations; its analysis of object-support helps distinguish sleep from blank nonexistence; its account of *smṛti* and memory-producing capacity shows how experience can remain causally effective. The five companion essays develop those comparisons. [Pramāṇa](pramana-according-to-kosa.md) · [Viparyaya](viparyaya-according-to-kosa.md) · [Vikalpa](vikalpa-according-to-kosa.md) · [Nidrā](nidra-and-absorption-according-to-kosa.md) · [Smṛti](smrti-and-samskara-according-to-kosa.md).

The Kośa comparison gives two precise cautions. First, discriminative *prajñā* is consequential for one Kośa *nirodha*, while deficient conditions explain the other. Second, their technical status as unconditioned dharmas is specific to the Kośa argument. The Yoga Bhāṣya's two currents explain why practice and dispassion are both required for restraint; they do not assign either current to one of the Kośa's two cessations.

The next textual step is I.13–16, where Yoga defines practice and dispassion. Those definitions will determine how much further this comparison can go.
