# Smṛti and Saṃskāra: How an Object Is Not Lost

## A Kośa companion essay for Yoga Sūtra I.11

Yoga I.11 closes the fivefold list of movements with *anubhūtaviṣayāsaṃpramoṣaḥ smṛtiḥ*: memory is the non-loss of an experienced object. The Kośa glosses *smṛti* as *ālambanāsaṃpramoṣaḥ*, non-loss of the object-support. The verbal closeness is striking. Yet the two expressions do not simply repeat one another: Yoga names the object **as experienced** (*anubhūta-viṣaya*); the Kośa names it in its role as the cognition's **support** (*ālambana*). Their relation gives our Kośa-first commentary a precise question: how can the object cease to be present and nevertheless remain available to cognition? [Yoga I.11](../../Sources/Yoga.txt); [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 54, lines 17–24](../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

## Retention is an operation

The Kośa places *smṛti* among the mental factors in its account of what occurs with consciousness and immediately defines its work through the *ālambana*. The definition does not say that an object remains physically present or that an image is stored unchanged. It says the support is not lost. In VAK 1.33, the Bhāṣya identifies *anusmaraṇavikalpa*, recollective differentiation, with mental *smṛti*, whether concentrated or unconcentrated. Memory is therefore an operation of mental cognition that can be active within meditative as well as ordinary conditions. It is not merely a warehouse one enters after experience has ended. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 22, lines 23–26](../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

Our Kośa analysis separates three moments: an earlier experience, a capacity formed through that experience, and a later act of recollection. The object is retained **as encountered**, so a later cognition can recover both a content and a way of taking it. This is our reconstruction from Yoga's *anubhūta-viṣaya*, Kośa *ālambana*, and the memory-capacity analogy below; the sūtra does not itself name a complete causal mechanism.

That matters after I.7–10. A remembered content could have been established by a valid cognition, misdetermined, constructed from words, or encountered in an absence-supported movement. This is an inference from the fivefold sequence, not a guarantee that every kind leaves the same trace. Remembering therefore does not certify the earlier determination as true. [Yoga I.6–11](../../Sources/Yoga.txt).

## The seed is a capacity, not a second remembered object

In the Kośa's discussion of latent affliction, the Sautrāntika explanation calls a dormant affliction a continuing seed condition and an active one its manifestation. To explain “seed,” it offers an analogy from memory: cognition born of experience gives rise to a **capacity to produce memory** (*anubhavajñānajā smṛtyutpādanaśaktiḥ*). The argument resists making that capacity into an additional, separately existing entity. This is a claim made in a dispute about *anuśaya*; the memory analogy illuminates causal continuity, but it should not be presented as a universal Kośa theory of all *saṃskāras*. [Kośabhāṣya, source pp. 278–279, lines 19–1](../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

Here we distinguish the operation from its condition: *smṛti* is the recollective non-loss that occurs; a relevant *saṃskāra* is a formed capacity through which an earlier act can shape a later one. The Kośa's memory-capacity analogy supports the distinction, while Yoga I.11 supplies the experienced object to which retention is answerable. Not every occurrence of *saṃskāra* names a mnemonic trace: the formation aggregate and conditioned formations have wider ranges in the Kośa. [Kośabhāṣya, source pp. 54, 278–79](../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt); [Yoga I.11](../../Sources/Yoga.txt).

## The return from sleep, and the purification of memory

I.10 names an absence-supported movement; I.11 names non-loss of what has been experienced. In our Bhavacakra reading, sleep loosens the currently selected world, and memory participates in the return through which a world becomes recognizable again. This is **our reconstruction** of the cycle. The sūtras themselves give the two operations, not a theory of the Bhavacakra. [Yoga I.10–11](../../Sources/Yoga.txt).

The later Yoga sequence reveals why this fifth movement matters to *samāpatti*. I.43 speaks of *smṛtipariśuddhi*, the purification of memory, in *nirvitarkā samāpatti*. Memory need not be erased: the inherited name and form can become transparent rather than dictate what now appears. This is the interpretation developed in the current [I.43 Prajñāpāda report](../../../Docs/Prajnapada/ys-i-43-fichte-report.md), not a replacement for I.11's wording. At I.50, insight gives rise to a *saṃskāra* that inhibits other *saṃskāras*: the issue is no longer simply what one remembers, but what formed capacity governs future cognition. [Yoga I.43 and I.50](../../Sources/Yoga.txt).

The essay's working genetic line is **experience → formed capacity → possible recollection → renewed cognition**. Within that line, *smṛti : saṃskāra* is a reciprocal determination in our project register: recollection discloses a capacity formed by experience, while that capacity makes recollection possible. Memory preserves access; fresh cognition tests what the retained way of access discloses. Yoga and Kośa meet precisely where preservation can either serve the present object or impose the past upon it.

I.12 will ask how these five movements come under *nirodha* through *abhyāsa* and *vairāgya*. The functional synthesis of the set belongs there; the present essay establishes the fifth movement and its *saṃskāra* condition.
