# Smṛti and Saṃskāra: How an Object Is Not Lost

## A Kośa companion essay for Yoga Sūtra I.11

Yoga I.11 closes the fivefold list of movements with *anubhūtaviṣayāsaṃpramoṣaḥ smṛtiḥ*: memory is the non-loss of an experienced object. The Kośa glosses *smṛti* as *ālambanāsaṃpramoṣaḥ*, non-loss of the object-support. The verbal closeness is striking. Yet the two expressions do not simply repeat one another: Yoga names the object **as experienced** (*anubhūta-viṣaya*); the Kośa names it in its role as the cognition's **support** (*ālambana*). Their relation gives our Bhāṣya a precise question: how can the object cease to be present and nevertheless remain available to cognition? [Yoga I.11](../../Sources/Yoga.txt); [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 54, lines 17–24](../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

## Retention is an operation

The Kośa places *smṛti* among the mental factors in its account of what occurs with consciousness and immediately defines its work through the *ālambana*. The definition does not say that an object remains physically present or that an image is stored unchanged. It says the support is not lost. In VAK 1.33, the Bhāṣya identifies *anusmaraṇavikalpa*, recollective differentiation, with mental *smṛti*, whether concentrated or unconcentrated. Memory is therefore an operation of mental cognition that can be active within meditative as well as ordinary conditions. It is not merely a warehouse one enters after experience has ended. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 22, lines 23–26](../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

The received Yoga Bhāṣya makes the problem more exact by asking whether consciousness remembers the earlier cognition (*pratyaya*) or its object (*viṣaya*). It answers that the prior cognition, colored by what was grasped, displays both the form of the grasped (*grāhya*) and the form of grasping (*grahaṇa*). It produces a *saṃskāra* of that kind, and the *saṃskāra* later gives rise to a memory with both aspects. Memory can thus carry forward **what was encountered and how it was taken**. This is the commentator's analysis of I.11, beyond the sūtra's compressed definition. [Yoga I.11 with Bhāṣya](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutra-with-bhasya-sanskrit/d/doc1216808.html).

That dual form matters after I.7–10. A memory may arise from a valid cognition, an error, a word-led construction, or sleep; the Yoga Bhāṣya explicitly says memories arise from experiences of all five movements. Remembering is therefore no automatic certificate that the remembered determination was valid. What persists may be a way of misapprehending or imagining as well as a way of knowing. [Yoga I.11 with Bhāṣya](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutra-with-bhasya-sanskrit/d/doc1216808.html).

## The seed is a capacity, not a second remembered object

In the Kośa's discussion of latent affliction, the Sautrāntika explanation calls a dormant affliction a continuing seed condition and an active one its manifestation. To explain “seed,” it offers an analogy from memory: cognition born of experience gives rise to a **capacity to produce memory** (*anubhavajñānajā smṛtyutpādanaśaktiḥ*). The argument resists making that capacity into an additional, separately existing entity. This is a claim made in a dispute about *anuśaya*; the memory analogy illuminates causal continuity, but it should not be presented as a universal Kośa theory of all *saṃskāras*. [Kośabhāṣya, source pp. 278–279, lines 19–1](../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

Here we can distinguish the operation from its condition: *smṛti* is the recollective non-loss that occurs; the relevant *saṃskāra* is the formation through which an earlier act can shape a later one. The Yoga Bhāṣya explicitly connects them. The Kośa supplies an analogous account of a memory-producing capacity. These sources meet without becoming the same doctrine. Nor does every occurrence of *saṃskāra* name a mnemonic trace: the formation aggregate and conditioned formations have wider ranges in the Kośa. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 54, lines 17–24](../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt); [Yoga I.11 with Bhāṣya](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutra-with-bhasya-sanskrit/d/doc1216808.html).

## The return from sleep, and the purification of memory

I.10's Bhāṣya appealed to recollection on waking to establish that sleep had a determinate character. I.11 now names the operation by which that character can be retained. In our Bhavacakra reading, sleep loosens the currently selected world, and memory participates in the return through which a world becomes recognizable again. This is **our reconstruction** of the cycle. The sūtra itself defines non-loss of an experienced object; it does not assert a theory of the Bhavacakra. [Yoga I.10 with Bhāṣya](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutra-with-bhasya-sanskrit/d/doc1216807.html).

The later Yoga sequence reveals why this fifth movement matters to *samāpatti*. I.43 speaks of *smṛtipariśuddhi*, the purification of memory, in *nirvitarkā samāpatti*. Memory need not be erased: the inherited name and form can become transparent rather than dictate what now appears. This is the interpretation developed in the current [I.43 Prajñāpāda report](../../../Docs/Prajnapada/ys-i-43-fichte-report.md), not a replacement for I.11's wording. At I.50, insight gives rise to a *saṃskāra* that inhibits other *saṃskāras*: the issue is no longer simply what one remembers, but what formed capacity governs future cognition. [Yoga I.50 with Bhāṣya](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutra-with-bhasya-sanskrit/d/doc1216847.html).

The essay's working genetic line is **experience → formed capacity → possible recollection → renewed cognition**. Within that line, *smṛti : saṃskāra* is a reciprocal determination in our project register: recollection discloses a capacity formed by experience, while that capacity makes recollection possible. Memory preserves access; fresh cognition tests what the retained way of access discloses. Yoga and Kośa meet precisely where preservation can either serve the present object or impose the past upon it.

I.12 will ask how these five movements come under *nirodha* through *abhyāsa* and *vairāgya*. The functional synthesis of the set belongs there; the present essay establishes the fifth movement and its *saṃskāra* condition.
