# What Is a *Pramāṇa* According to the Kośa?

## A companion essay for Yoga Sūtra I.7

Yoga I.6 names *pramāṇa* as one of the five movements (*vṛtti*) of consciousness. I.7 will name perception, inference, and *āgama* as its forms: *pratyakṣānumānāgamāḥ pramāṇāni*. Before reading that enumeration through the Kośa, we need to ask what the Kośa actually makes a *pramāṇa* do. The answer emerges in an argument, rather than in an abstract definition of a theory of knowledge.

## The demand for a ground

In the *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya*'s discussion of the characteristics of conditioned things, the Sautrāntika voice challenges the claim that arising, duration, decay, and impermanence exist as distinct real dharmas (*dravyataḥ*). It asks why, and answers: *pramāṇābhāvāt*, “because of the absence of a pramāṇa.” It then sharpens the challenge:

> *na hy eṣāṃ dravyato 'stitve kiñcid api pramāṇam asti pratyakṣam anumānam āptāgamo vā yathā rūpādīnāṃ dharmāṇām iti*
>
> “For their existence as real entities there is no pramāṇa at all—perception, inference, or trustworthy testimony—as there is for dharmas such as visible form.”

This is a local argument about a particular ontological claim. A *pramāṇa* is what could warrant saying that the proposed dharmas exist **as distinct real entities**. The force of the objection depends on the predicate under dispute: one might accept that conditioned things arise and cease while denying that “arising” and “ceasing” must therefore be further substances alongside them. A word in a teaching is not yet proof of the extra entity one reads into it. The three candidates for warrant are *pratyakṣa* (perception), *anumāna* (inference), and *āptāgama* (testimony from a trustworthy source). [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 76, lines 23–27](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

The next exchange shows why testimony requires interpretation. The opponent cites a sūtra that says arising, passing away, and alteration of what endures are discernible in the conditioned. The reply says that knowing the text is not yet knowing its meaning, invokes meaning (*artha*) as the refuge, and reads the teaching as disclosing the conditioned and dependently arisen character of a stream of formations. It does not grant that each characteristic is therefore an independently real dharma. Here the Kośa gives *āgama* genuine evidential force while requiring the claimed meaning to survive scrutiny. [Kośabhāṣya, source pp. 76–77, lines 26–29 and 1–3](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

## Three avenues, distinct operations

The Kośa's list resembles Yoga I.7, but the wording matters. The Kośa passage says *āptāgama*, testimony associated with trustworthiness; Yoga's sūtra says *āgama*. We can compare their functions without silently making the two expressions identical. Nor should we convert one sentence in the Kośa into a claim that Vasubandhu has presented a complete, universally accepted three-part epistemology. Its immediate purpose is to test an existence claim.

Elsewhere the Bhāṣya reports a fourfold account of what is seen, heard, thought, and known. On that account, what the five faculties directly apprehend is “seen”; what comes from another is “heard”; what one accepts through one's own reasoning and inference is “thought”; and what is directly present to the mind and personally experienced is “known.” The passage introduces this with *kecit tāvad āhuḥ*, “some say,” and continues its own debate. It is useful evidence for distinguishing sensory perception, received report, reasoning, and direct mental experience; it is not a second definition to be imposed on I.7. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 246, lines 1–6](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

The standing of testimony is also contested within the Kośa's arguments. Against appeal to a scholastic rule that ten mental factors occur in every mind, a speaker says *sūtrapramāṇakā vayaṃ na śāstrapramāṇakāḥ*: “We take the sūtra as authority, not the treatise.” The issue is which text can establish the rule at hand. Thus textual authority is a discriminating practice, not a license to treat any inherited formulation as equally probative. [Kośabhāṣya, source p. 146, lines 3–5](../../../../Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt).

## What this gives Yoga I.7

Taken together, these passages let us give a careful Kośa-informed answer: a *pramāṇa* is a warranted way of establishing a determinate claim about its object. Perception presents; inference establishes through a reason; trustworthy testimony transmits a teaching whose meaning must be judged. What each warrants depends on what is being claimed. Evidence that a process occurs does not automatically establish a separately existent entity corresponding to the name of that process.

Yoga I.7 supplies its own compact enumeration of *pramāṇas*. Kośa analysis can help us ask of each: **What object is given? What conclusion is drawn? What does the testimony actually mean?** These are questions for the ensuing study of I.7, not additional words hidden in Patañjali's sūtra.

Finally, I.6 has already placed *pramāṇa* within *vṛtti*. A valid cognition can succeed in its proper field and still be a movement of consciousness. Our larger question—why even this successful movement comes under *nirodha*—belongs to the Yoga study and its later development. The Kośa helps us preserve the validity of the cognition while making its grounds and limits explicit; it does not itself settle Yoga's account of sublation.
