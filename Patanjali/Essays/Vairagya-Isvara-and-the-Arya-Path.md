# Vairāgya, Īśvara, and the Ārya Path

The science of consciousness begins with a demand to know what moves in consciousness and what obstructs its truth. *Vairāgya* is indispensable to that work. YS I.12 joins it to practice as a means of *nirodha*; I.15 names freedom from thirst for seen and heard objects; I.16 carries that freedom to the guṇas through knowledge of *puruṣa*. The movement of dispassion is real. It withdraws authority from attraction, aversion, and the possessive identification of consciousness with what passes through it.

We must give the Arhat's attainment its due. The initial movement of *vairāgya* cannot be dismissed as a mistake simply because our Yoga Vidyā takes another direction. It establishes a freedom without which theoretical reason would remain governed by pleasure and pain. Yet the freedom to leave an object behind is not the whole of the science. Once consciousness is no longer captive to its movements, the question becomes what those movements disclose and how their truth is to be known and taught.

YS I.23 opens another determination: *īśvara-praṇidhānād vā*, “or through dedication to Īśvara.” I.24–26 specify Īśvara as untouched by affliction and its consequences, with unsurpassed seed of omniscience, and as teacher of the ancients. I.27–28 then give the practice its form: *praṇava* is his expression; its repetition is joined to meditation on its meaning. Devotion here is no mere ornament of meditation. It directs the practitioner toward a ground of knowledge and instruction that exceeds the self-enclosed achievement of an individual adept. II.45 later connects dedication to Īśvara with the attainment of samādhi.

Our Ārya path is God-centered in this sense. It seeks the truth of **citta : vṛtti** under Īśvara, and it seeks to articulate that truth for others. *Ārya* names this noble intellectual and spiritual undertaking, never ancestry or race. The horror at a “citta-killing” interpretation arises from devotion itself: if consciousness were simply extinguished, its truth could neither shine nor be taught. *Nirodha* must be understood as sublation of what prevents that truth, including the *doṣa-bīja* named in III.50.

The two movements must be studied together. *Vairāgya* frees consciousness from captivity to its objects; *īśvara-praṇidhāna* orders that freedom toward God and toward knowledge. This is our proposed second pass through the Yoga Sūtra. It will need to answer to the precise sequence of I.12–29, to the science of the *vṛttis*, and to the Kośa's account of discrimination and liberation. The essay gives the path's direction; the individual studies must establish its steps.
