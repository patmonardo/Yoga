# VAK_7.13 Bhāṣya — Sixteen Aspects and the Manner of Knowing

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> dravyataḥ ṣoḍaśākārāḥ prajñākāras tayā saha |
> ākārayanti sālambāḥ sarvam ākāryate tu sat || 7.13 ||

> In substantial distinction, the aspects are sixteen. Prajñā is called
> the aspect; with it, all dharmas with a Support apprehend under an
> aspect. Whatever exists, however, can itself be apprehended under an
> aspect.

The verse identifies aspect with prajñā. The prose objects and offers
`ālambanagrahaṇaprakāra`. That reformulation is commentary, not read back
into the kārikā line.

## 3. Continuous Translation

> Are these sixteen aspects sixteen by name or substantially? Some say they
> are seven substantially and sixteen by name: the four aspects of suffering
> are distinct, while the aspects of origination, cessation, and Path are
> each one entity. The account presented here, however, says: “The aspects
> are sixteen substantially.”
>
> [Suffering is] impermanent because it depends on conditions; suffering
> because its nature is oppressive [local `ṣīḍātmakatvāt`]; empty because
> it counters the view of what belongs to a self; and non-self because it
> counters self-view. [Origination is] cause through having the nature of a
> seed; origination through appearing; arising through continuity; and
> condition in the sense of bringing something to completion. Thus a pot
> is completed through the conjunction of a lump of clay, stick, [wheel?
> local `catra`], thread, water, and so forth.
>
> [Cessation is] cessation because the Bases cease [local `akndha-`];
> peaceful through the extinguishing of fire; excellent because free of
> harm; and escape because free of every calamity. [Path is] Path in the
> sense of going, right method because it is joined to what is fitting,
> course of practice in the sense of proceeding correctly, and leading
> out because it passes beyond definitively.
>
> Or [suffering is] impermanent because it is not final; suffering because
> it is a burden [local `abhinyāsabhūtatvāt`]; empty because it lacks an
> inner acting person; and non-self because it does not act at will.
> [Origination is] cause through drawing [the result] here, origination
> through emergence, arising through expansion, and condition as a
> reliance [local `pratisaraṇārghena`]. [Cessation is] cessation, a state
> of nonconnection, because connection has ceased; peaceful because free
> of the three marks of the conditioned; excellent because wholesome;
> and escape because it is the highest security. [Path is] Path because
> it opposes a wrong path, right method because it opposes wrong method,
> course of practice because it proceeds without obstruction toward the
> city of nirvāṇa, and leading out because it counters every existence.
>
> These are several ways of explaining them. We shall state the intended
> account. [Suffering is] impermanent because it arises and passes away;
> suffering because it is adverse; empty because it lacks a self; and
> non-self because it is itself no self. As for cause, origination,
> arising, and condition, the sūtra says: “These five appropriated Bases
> are rooted in desire, have desire as origination, are of desire's kind,
> and arise from desire.” The word “arising” [*prabhava*] alone should
> be read later in the treatise's order. What distinguishes these?
> Desire has four forms.
>
> First is the undifferentiated desire for present self-existence: “I am.”
> Second is the undifferentiated desire for renewed existence: “I shall
> be.” Third is desire for a specifically differentiated renewed
> existence: “I shall be thus.” Fourth is desire that binds the link of
> rebirth, or else desire for the formation of karma. The first is the
> root cause of suffering, like a seed in relation to its fruit. The
> second is origination, because suffering comes together through it,
> as sprouts and the like issue toward fruit. The third is a condition
> for suffering of that particular kind, like field, water, [manure?
> local `pāśy`], and so forth in relation to fruit: according to such
> conditions, fruits differ in fragrance, taste, potency, maturation,
> and efficacy. The fourth is arising, since suffering comes about
> immediately from it, like fruit's emergence from a flower [local
> `puṣpāvasānam` remains uncertain].
>
> Alternatively, the wanderings of craving contain two sets of five and
> two sets of four: these are the four desires. The cited “I am” series
> varies present being as “I am,” “I am thus,” “I am otherwise,” “I exist,”
> and “I do not exist” [local `sāti` is damaged]. The “I shall be” series
> varies future being as “I shall be,” “I shall not be,” “I shall be thus,”
> “[I shall be thus]” and “I shall be otherwise”; the local line repeats
> `evaṃ bhaviṣyāmi` and has damaged `śranyathā bhaviṣyāmi`. The next
> `syām` series varies “May I be” with “thus,” “in this manner,” and
> “otherwise”; its exact fourth member is not secure in the local text.
> The final `api` series gives “May I also be,” “May I also be thus,”
> “May I also be in this manner,” and “May I also be otherwise.”
>
> [Cessation is] cessation because the course has stopped; peaceful
> because it has no suffering, as it is said: “Conditioned things are
> suffering, monks; nirvāṇa is peaceful”; excellent because nothing is
> higher; and escape because there is no return. [Path is] Path because
> it is a path, right method because it proceeds in accordance with
> actuality, and course of practice because it is definite, as it is
> said: “This is the Path; there is no other for purification of Seeing.”
> It leads out because it effects departure once and for all.
>
> Or the aspects impermanent, suffering, empty, and non-self counter,
> respectively, ways of seeing things as permanent, pleasant, belonging
> to a self, and self. The aspects cause, origination, arising, and
> condition counter ways of seeing no cause, one cause, transformation
> [as cause], and prior intelligent design [as cause]. The cessation
> aspect counters the view that there is no liberation; the peaceful
> aspect counters the view that liberation is suffering; the excellent
> aspect counters the view that meditative pleasure is supreme; escape
> counters the view that liberation is not definitive because one may
> decline again and again. Path, right method, course of practice, and
> leading out counter, respectively, the views that there is no Path,
> that this is a wrong Path, that another Path is [the way], and that
> the Path returns one [to existence].
>
> What dharma is this called an aspect? “Prajñā is the aspect.” If so,
> Prajñā would not itself have an aspect, because it is not conjoined
> with another Prajñā. It would be better to say that an aspect is the
> manner in which all Consciousnesses and mental factors take a Support.
>
> Then does Prajñā alone apprehend under an aspect? No. What, then?
> “Together with it, those with a Support apprehend under an aspect.”
> Prajñā and all the other dharmas with a Support apprehend under an
> aspect. “Whatever exists, however, is apprehended under an aspect”:
> anything that exists can be so apprehended. Thus it is established
> that Prajñā is an aspect, apprehends under an aspect, and is itself
> apprehended; Supports are only apprehended. [The local concluding
> `ākāryante` is grammatically irregular.]

## 5. Sixteen, Substantially

The first attributed account counts seven substantially and sixteen by
name: four suffering aspects, with the four aspects of each of the other
three Truths counted as one entity. The presentation that follows takes
the sixteen as substantially distinct aspect determinations, without
making them sixteen substances or sixteen simultaneous factors.

| Truth | Aspects |
|---|---|
| Suffering | anitya, duḥkha, śūnya, anātman |
| Origination | hetu, samudaya, prabhava, pratyaya |
| Cessation | nirodha, śānta, praṇīta, niḥsaraṇa |
| Path | mārga, nyāya, pratipad, nairyāṇika |

The first two explanations, the preferred account with its four desires,
and the final counteractive series remain distinct. The preferred account
itself gives a second, two-five/two-four enumeration from the wanderings
of craving. The local Sanskrit list is damaged; the translation marks its
uncertain members. `Anitya` through conditions is dependence on
*pratyaya* here, with no representational theory implied.

## 6. What an Aspect Is

```text
verse start:  ākāra = prajñā
objection:    no second prajñā → prajñā would not be sākāra
proposal:     ākāra = manner of taking Support by Consciousness and mental factors
```

`Ākārayati` here = apprehend under an aspect.

```text
sālamba dharma  → has a Support and apprehends under an aspect
existent        → can be apprehended under an aspect
```

The second does not give the first. An apprehended dharma need not itself
apprehend. A cognitive dharma can itself become a Support for another
act of knowing. Neither rule establishes same-moment self-knowing or
omniscience.

Prajñā: named as aspect in the verse; active with the other sālamba dharmas;
itself available to be apprehended. `Ālambana` as Support: apprehended.

## 7. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

- `dravyataḥ` answers `nāmataḥ`;
- `tayā saha` goes forward with `sālambāḥ ākārayanti`;
- `sarvaṃ sat` is the passive side, range not event;
- manner-of-taking definition is the prose's correction.

Literal line keeps “Prajñā is the aspect.”

## 8. Interpretation

This section is reconstruction.

Ākāra is how jñāna flows around artha — the *manner* of holding, not a
second inner knower and not the artha itself. That is Vidyā. The sixteen
are the Principle's aspects, now named. Technē still assigns who uses which
slice (7.10–7.12).

Nirodha in the table is Conceptual/Arthic: one of four cessation-aspects.
Meta-nirodha is this inquiry into aspect itself — paramārtha in kāmadhātu,
divine entertainment that is not comedy: the Desire Realm is where the
manner of holding is determined. Sublation parked. Appearance would print
the 4×4 list and call it knowledge.

Inference remains secondary. The series that oppose views are Technē
functions of aspects, not the generation of the Principle.
