# VAK_7.11 Bhāṣya — Knowledge of Another's Consciousness: Aspect, Singularity, and Discernment

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> tathā paramanojñānaṃ nirmalaṃ samalaṃ punaḥ |
> jñeyasvalakṣaṇākāram ekaikadravyagocaram || 7.11 ||

> Uncontaminated Knowledge of another's Consciousness, as Path-knowledge,
> has the four aspects proper to Path. Contaminated Knowledge of another's
> Consciousness takes the own characteristic of the Consciousness or mental
> factor it knows as its aspect.
> Both range over one individual mental entity at a time.

`Tathā` points to 7.10's four-aspect distribution, not the sixteen.
The following prose tests the sūtra's paired descriptions of Consciousness
through a long exchange; the complete exchange is translated below.

Kārikā study: [`VAK_7.11.md`](./VAK_7.11.md).

## 3. Continuous Translation

> Likewise, uncontaminated Knowledge of another's Consciousness is
> four-aspected, because it has the aspects proper to its own truth: it is
> Path-knowledge. Contaminated Knowledge of another's Consciousness, however,
> takes as its aspect the own characteristic of the knowable Consciousness
> or mental factor. For it apprehends an own characteristic.
>
> Both [local `labhayamapi`] range over one individual entity at a time.
> When it apprehends Consciousness, it does not then apprehend [mental
> factors; local `cittānāṃ` is damaged]; when it apprehends feeling, it
> does not then apprehend recognition, and so forth.
>
> But what of the Blessed One's statement that one knows a Consciousness
> with passion as with passion, as it really is, and so forth? The two are
> not apprehended simultaneously, just as cloth and its stain are not
> apprehended simultaneously.
>
> “Consciousness with passion” has two senses of “with passion”:
> conjunction with passion and bondage by passion. A Consciousness
> conjoined with passion is “with passion” in both senses; another
> contaminated Consciousness is so through bondage by passion.
>
> Some say that in this sūtra “with passion” means conjoined with passion,
> while “free from passion” means a counteragent to passion. If mere
> non-conjunction with passion made a Consciousness free from passion,
> one conjoined with another affliction would be so too. But on the
> counteragent reading, an unafflicted but contaminated Consciousness
> that is no counteragent to passion would be neither with passion nor
> free from it. Others therefore insist on conjunction with passion in
> construing “Consciousness with passion” here. The corresponding cases
> extend through “with delusion” and “free from delusion.”

> Contracted Consciousness is wholesome because its Supports are gathered
> together. Distracted Consciousness is afflicted because it is conjoined
> with distraction. The western teachers say instead that contracted
> Consciousness is conjoined with torpor, while distracted Consciousness
> is other afflicted Consciousness. This is not accepted: an afflicted
> Consciousness conjoined with torpor would then be both contracted and
> distracted. It would also conflict with the treatise's statement that
> knowing contracted Consciousness as it is comprises four knowledges:
> dharma-, anvaya-, conventional, and Path-knowledge.
>
> Sluggish Consciousness is afflicted because it is conjoined with
> indolence; raised Consciousness is wholesome because it is conjoined
> with effort. Small Consciousness is afflicted, being cultivated by
> those with little purification; great Consciousness is wholesome, the
> reverse. Or they are small and great by the fewness or abundance of
> roots, worth, retinue, accompanying factors, and power.
>
> Afflicted Consciousness has at most two unwholesome roots; wholesome
> Consciousness has three wholesome roots. The former has little worth,
> since it is attained without effort; the latter has much, being
> attained through extensive preparation. The former has a small
> retinue, since it has no future cultivation of its kind; the latter
> has a large retinue, with future cultivation. The former has three
> accompanying Bases; the latter has four. Afflicted Consciousness has
> little power, since even good roots that have been cut off will resume.
> Wholesome Consciousness has great power: a single acceptance of
> dharma-knowledge concerning suffering cuts off ten underlying
> afflictions permanently. Hence the afflicted is small and the
> wholesome great.
>
> Agitated Consciousness is afflicted through conjunction with
> agitation; the unagitated is wholesome as its counteragent. The same
> applies to uncalmed and calmed Consciousness. Uncollected Consciousness
> is afflicted through conjunction with distraction; collected
> Consciousness is wholesome as its counteragent. Uncultivated
> Consciousness is afflicted because it has neither acquisition nor
> repeated cultivation; cultivated Consciousness is wholesome by both.
> [Unliberated; the local witness reads `vimuktaṃ`] Consciousness is
> afflicted because it is unliberated in nature and continuum; liberated
> Consciousness is wholesome because liberation in those ways is possible.
> So say the Vaibhāṣikas.

> Yet this explanation does not accord with the sūtra, nor does it state
> the distinct meanings of these terms. How does it fail to accord?
> The sūtra says: “How is Consciousness contracted inwardly? When it
> accompanies torpor and sleep, or is inwardly accompanied by calming
> without insight. How is it distracted outwardly? When it wanders and
> flows among the five qualities of desire.”
>
> “Was it not said that the same Consciousness would be both contracted
> and distracted?” That argument was made, but it is unsound: afflicted
> Consciousness accompanied by torpor need not be admitted as distracted.
> “Was conflict with the treatise not also alleged?” Better conflict with
> a treatise than with the sūtra.
>
> “How does the [Vaibhāṣika] account fail to state distinct meanings?”
> Because it gives one undifferentiated characteristic to distracted,
> sluggish, agitated, uncalmed, uncollected, uncultivated, and unliberated
> Consciousnesses, and likewise to contracted, raised, and the others.
> “The terms do have distinct meanings even on my account: the separate
> faults of the afflicted and virtues of the wholesome are shown, so the
> eight names each express a distinct meaning.” But because that account
> has not removed its conflict with the sūtra, its proposed distinctions
> also fail.
>
> Further, if sluggish Consciousness were just agitated Consciousness,
> the sūtra would not say: “When Consciousness is sluggish, or sluggishness
> is feared, that is no time to cultivate the awakening factors of
> tranquility, collectedness, and equanimity; when it is agitated, or
> agitation is feared, that is no time to cultivate investigation of
> dharmas, energy, and joy.”
>
> “Is cultivation of the awakening factors thus divided [by occasion]?”
> Their cultivation here means attending to
> them, not their actual presence; so there is no fault. “Could my
> account not also agree with the sūtra? Although all afflicted
> Consciousness is sluggish and agitated, the sūtra calls it sluggish
> when indolence predominates, agitated when agitation predominates.
> I speak of one nature because the two are constantly conjoined.”
> No intended use of the words is prohibited; the point is that this
> is not the sūtra's intention.

> As for the earlier claim that every Consciousness bound by passion is
> “with passion”: what does this bond mean? If it means accompanying
> possession of passion, even a learner's uncontaminated Consciousness
> would count as “with passion.” If it means taking passion as Support,
> even an arhat's contaminated Consciousness would count so. If that
> Consciousness did not take passion as Support, how would it be
> contaminated? If the answer is that a general affliction takes it as
> Support, the same reasoning would call it “with delusion.” Yet Knowledge
> of another's Consciousness takes neither the possession of passion nor
> the passion that is the observed Consciousness's Support as its own
> Support.
>
> The local text next reads, “Therefore a Consciousness with passion is
> not accepted here through conjunction with passion.” What, then?
> The sūtra's intention appears to be that Consciousness conjoined with
> passion is “with passion,” and unconjoined Consciousness is “free from
> passion.” [The preceding negative wording conflicts with this answer.]
>
> “But another sūtra says that the Consciousness of one free from passion,
> hatred, and delusion will not return to the three existences.” That
> refers to the loss of possession, and so is no fault. “Was it not
> already objected that Consciousness conjoined with another affliction
> would also be ‘free from passion,’ since it is not conjoined with
> passion?” On this intended sense there is no contradiction. Yet one
> does not apprehend it as “free from passion”; rather, as “with hatred,”
> “with delusion,” and so forth. Enough of this digression; let the
> established position be explained.

> Does Knowledge of another's Consciousness apprehend the aspect or the
> Support of that other Consciousness? It does not. Without regard to
> that [other Consciousness's] aspect or Support, it knows “this
> Consciousness is passionate”; it does not know “it is passionate toward
> this form.” Otherwise it would take that form as Support, and in taking
> another Consciousness that has such a Support it would incur the fault
> of apprehending its own nature [as Support].
>
> All Knowledge of another's Consciousness has as its Conditions the
> individual own-characteristics of present Consciousness and mental
> factors in another continuum, connected with the desire or form domain
> or unconnected [with a domain]. It is barred from the Path of Seeing
> and is obtained in the Path of Cultivation. It is dissociated from the
> emptiness and signless concentrations, is not included in exhaustion-
> or non-arising-knowledge, and is barred from the uninterrupted Path.
> Knowledge of another's Consciousness has been explained.

## 5. Aspect of the Knowing ≠ Aspect of the Target

| Form | Aspect of this knowing | Range |
|---|---|---|
| Pure | four Path-aspects | one entity at a time |
| Contaminated | own characteristic of the knowable mental dharma | one entity at a time |

```text
paracitta knows Consciousness C
AND C has Support S and aspect A
    does not entail
paracitta knows S or operates under A
```

“This Consciousness is passionate” ≠ “this Consciousness is passionate
toward that form.” The second would take the form as Support.

`Dravya` here = the individually apprehended dharma, not a substrate.
`Samala` = sāsrava, not automatically akuśala.

Association, possession (`prāpti`), and having-as-Support stay distinct.
The prose rejects possession and mere object-taking as the sense of
“with passion” for this knowing.

## 6. What the Long Debate Is For

Scripture classifies another's Consciousness (passionate / free of passion,
sluggish / agitated, and so on). Classification is not simultaneous grasp
of Consciousness and a factor, and not identity with that Consciousness.
Cultivation instructions differ for līna
and uddhata; a flattened “afflicted” reading loses the work. Hence sūtra
over śāstra when they collide on these words.

The continuous translation follows every pair and the exchanges that test
them. Its three-relation result separates the knowing's aspect, the mental
entity known, and that entity's own aspect and Support.

## 7. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

- `tathā` = four Path-aspects via `mārgajñānatva`;
- `jñeyasvalakṣaṇa` is the contaminated aspect;
- `ekaikadravyagocara` covers both forms (`ubhayam` working);
- later field-limits are commentary, not verse words.

## 8. Interpretation

This section is reconstruction.

Nirodha as a truth is Conceptual/Arthic: cessation as what is held. This
chapter's work is higher — meta-nirodha: determining knowing without taking
the target's artha as one's own. That is paramārtha *in* kāmadhātu: not
flight to ūrdhva, not conventional aparamārtha. Other-mind knowledge that
stayed in Appearance would claim to live inside the other's passion. The
Idea knows the condition and leaves the other's object unstolen.

Yoga Vidyā: three relations kept apart. Yoga Technē: cultivation still
needs līna ≠ uddhata. Philosophical Cognition sees both sides of a pair;
it does not undo the other's citta (Seeing both ≠ undoing both). Sublation
= nirodha remains parked.
