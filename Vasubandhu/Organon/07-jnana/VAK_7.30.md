# VAK_7.30 — Supporting Levels and Unobstructed Buddha-Knowledge

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

```sanskrit
प्राङ्निवासच्युतोत्पादबलं ध्यानेषु शेषितम् ।
सर्वभूमिषु केनास्य बलमव्याहतं यतः ॥ ७.३० ॥
```

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

```text
prāṅnivāsacyutotpādabalaṃ dhyāneṣu śeṣitam |
sarvabhūmiṣu kenāsya balam avyāhataṃ yataḥ || 7.30 ||
```

The Sanskrit follows the local kārikā witness, with compound
hyphens removed from its IAST presentation. The running
Bhāṣya's opening lemma is damaged; its prose identifies
former-abode knowledge and death-and-rebirth knowledge
unambiguously. The introductory sentence announces the
transition: constitution has been stated; level is now stated.

## 3. Padaccheda and Lexical Analysis

```text
prāk-nivāsa-cyuta-utpāda-balam | dhyāneṣu | śeṣitam |
sarva-bhūmiṣu | kena | asya | balam | avyāhatam | yataḥ
```

| Form | Morphology | Contextual sense |
|---|---|---|
| `prāṅnivāsa` | compound member with `prāk` | former abode / previous existence |
| `cyuta` | substantival participial form in compound | passing away, glossed as `cyuti` |
| `utpāda` | compound member | arising; here rebirth |
| `balam` | nominative singular neuter | power, collectively covering the specified pair |
| `dhyāneṣu` | locative plural masculine | in the dhyānas |
| `śeṣitam` | nominative singular neuter adjective used substantivally | the remaining [power-group] |
| `sarvabhūmiṣu` | locative plural feminine compound | in all levels |
| `kena` | instrumental singular interrogative | by what reason? why? |
| `asya` | genitive singular pronoun | of him, in his case |
| `avyāhatam` | nominative singular neuter adjective | unobstructed |
| `yataḥ` | causal relative adverb | because |

`Cyuta + utpāda` yields `cyutotpāda`; `kena + asya`
yields `kenāsya`. The prose's `cyutir eva cyutam`
secures the event-sense of passing away here.

## 4. Grammar

**Two powers, one compressed expression.** The initial
compound groups the former-abode and death/rebirth powers.
The Bhāṣya expands their support as `caturdhyānabhūmika`,
belonging to the four dhyāna levels. It does not introduce
a new eleventh power combining their two fields.

**The remainder.** Supply `balam` with `śeṣitam`. The
remaining eight powers are included in all levels, and
the prose immediately specifies eleven. “All” therefore
has an enumerated technical domain.

**Question and answer within the pāda.** Read `kena asya
balam?` as the question and `avyāhataṃ yataḥ` as its answer:
why is it power in his case? Because it is unobstructed.
The prose resolves `asya` as the Buddha and names knowledge
as what operates without obstruction.

**The breadth of the explanation.** `Sarvatra jñeye` means
throughout the knowable field. The source does not say
merely that the Buddha succeeds within an arbitrarily
narrow selection of objects. It concludes that the Buddha's
mental power is boundless like the knowable.

**Close construe.** The former-abode and death/rebirth
powers are in the dhyānas; the remainder is in all levels.
Why is this knowledge power in his case? Because it is
unobstructed.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> The powers of former abodes and death and rebirth are
> in the dhyānas; the remaining powers are in all levels.
> Why is it power in his case? Because it is unobstructed.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> The powers of former-abode knowledge and death-and-rebirth
> knowledge are supported by the four dhyānas. The other
> eight powers range across eleven levels: the desire domain,
> anāgamya, intermediate dhyāna, the four dhyānas, and the
> four formless levels. All have a human support in Jambūdvīpa,
> because, in this account, Buddhas do not arise elsewhere.
>
> Why are these knowledges called powers for the Buddha,
> while the corresponding knowledge categories in others
> are not? His knowledge operates unobstructed throughout
> the knowable field. Others' knowledge can fail to arise
> somewhere even when they wish to know. Thus the Buddha's
> mental power is boundless, like the knowable.

Source: Pradhan 412|10–413|03. The final characterization
concerns mental power; the next verse turns to bodily strength.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> A power is not fully determined by its knowledge-category
> membership. Its support and its manner of operation must
> also be specified. The Buddha's distinction is unobstructed
> knowing across the knowable field; possession of the same
> category does not establish that distinction in another
> practitioner. The support-range and the freedom from
> obstruction answer different questions.

Unobstructedness is the doctrinal claim stated by this
passage. It does not require replacing the four-dhyāna
support rule with unrestricted support, nor does the
passage assert that every object is cognized simultaneously
in one timeless cognitive event.

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**`Svabhāva` and `bhūmi`.** Constitution and level. The
opening prose explicitly distinguishes the knowledge-category
analysis already completed from the support analysis now
undertaken. `Bhūmi` here is not the object-field known.

**`Caturdhyānabhūmika`.** Supported by the four dhyānas.
This qualification belongs to both conventional powers
identified in VAK 7.29, not to all conventional knowledge.

**`Sarvabhūmisaṃgṛhīta`.** Included in all levels. The
enumerated eleven comprise one desire level, anāgamya,
intermediate dhyāna, four dhyānas, and four formless levels.
This does not make every constituent knowledge-category
available at every one of those levels without its own
previously established support restrictions.

**`Jambūdvīpapuruṣāśraya`.** Having a human support in
Jambūdvīpa. This is the passage's cosmological specification
of the person supporting the powers. It is distinct from
their meditative level and their knowledge-object.

**`Avyāhata`.** Unobstructed, unimpeded. The central reason
for the designation `bala`. Its contrast is not merely
having less information, but knowledge failing to operate
where it is sought.

**`Icchatām apy apravṛtti`.** Failure to occur even for
those wishing [to know]. The prose's concrete account
of cognitive obstruction in others.

**`Jñeyavad anantaṃ mānasaṃ balam`.** Mental power boundless
like the knowable. This concludes the cognitive analysis
and prepares the separate question of bodily strength.

## 8. Logical Determination

| Power-group | Supporting levels |
|---|---|
| Former-abode knowledge; death/rebirth knowledge | Four dhyānas |
| Remaining eight powers | Eleven-level range |

The eleven-level inventory is:

```text
Desire domain                         1
Anagamya                              1
Intermediate dhyana                   1
Four dhyanas                          4
Four formless levels                  4
Total                                11
```

Four coordinates now need separate representation:

```text
Knowledge-category constitution: VAK 7.28–7.29
Meditative support-level: VAK 7.30
Personal support: human in Jambudvipa
Operational distinction: unobstructed knowledge throughout the knowable
```

The last coordinate explains why the first alone does
not suffice for Buddha-power. The source's argument is
contextual: it explains these powers of the Buddha, not
a universal definition licensing arbitrary systems to
claim Buddha-power whenever they report successful operation.

**The Śāriputra examples.** The Bhāṣya cites his rejection
of a person seeking ordination and his lack of knowledge
of the limits of the rebirth history of a bird threatened
by a hawk. The references illustrate obstruction even in
an eminent disciple. This passage does not narrate the
stories in full; details about remote deeds, dates, or
the Buddha's subsequent interventions are not supplied here.

## 9. Interpretive Note

**Support is not reach.** The two powers can have a
restricted meditative support while their Buddha-specific
exercise is unobstructed. There is no contradiction unless
the level on which cognition operates is confused with
the field about which it knows.

**Preserve the source's strong claim.** The explanatory
prose says knowledge operates throughout the knowable,
and its conclusion calls mental power boundless. A gloss
limited to “reliable within a chosen task” would weaken
the doctrine. Conversely, constant simultaneous cognition
of every object is not asserted by this sentence.

**Textual caution.** The running witness contains a damaged
opening lemma, `duddhānutpādāt`, `jñānabhavyāhataṃ`, and
other transcription faults. The kārikā's `avyāhatam`,
the explicit contrast with obstruction, and the concluding
`avyāhatajñānatvāt` secure the central reading. The printed
eleven-level enumeration and the paired power names secure
the support account. No manuscript variation is inferred
merely from these electronic transcription defects.

**Organon reconstruction — Yoga Vidyā and Yoga Technē.**
Yoga Vidyā determines the knowledge field and its internal
categories. Yoga Technē, its kriyā–karma companion, concerns
the supported exercise of knowing and the conditions
under which it operates. This verse shows why the category
inventory alone is insufficient: knowing must be considered
in relation to support, scope, and obstruction.

For our Yoga system, the distinction between possessing
a determination and exercising it is essential. The
Śāriputra examples sharpen that distinction without
reducing his knowledge to absence of attainment. The
Buddha's unshared power receives its positive determination
through the absence of obstruction across the knowable.

This is a source-controlled contact with our project,
not an equation between Buddhahood and a technical system's
performance. An implemented capability, a successful
exercise, and the doctrine's boundless mental power must
retain their respective scopes. Vidyā–Technē reconstruction
can articulate the relations without claiming identity.
