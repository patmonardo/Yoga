# VAK_7.47 — The Three Prātihāryas and the Primacy of Instruction

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

`Avyabhicāritvāt + hita` yields `avyabhicāritvāddhita-`;
`hita + iṣṭa` yields `hiteṣṭa`. The two causal ablatives
provide two reasons for the superiority of instruction.

## 4. Grammar

**The sixfold inventory supplies the ordinals.** The
feminine singular forms refer to understood `abhijñā`.
They select extraordinary efficacy, other-mind knowledge,
and influx-exhaustion knowledge from the six higher
knowledges. They do not select the first, third, and
sixth members of a new list of prātihāryas.

**A plural nominal predicate.** `Prātihāryāṇi` names
the classification of the three selected capacities.
The Bhāṣya's `yathākramam`, “respectively,” assigns them
to extraordinary efficacy, indication, and instruction.
The shift from feminine subjects to a neuter nominal
predicate is not a disagreement requiring emendation.

**Instruction is foremost.** `Śāsanam agryam` is a
nominal sentence. The prose expands the subject as
`anuśāsanaprātihārya`, the wonder of instruction. The
comparison is among the three prātihāryas just named.

**Beneficial and desired fruit.** The prose resolves
the compound with `hitena iṣṭena phalena yogo bhavati`:
there is conjunction with a fruit that is beneficial
and desired. It does not identify two separate fruits,
one beneficial and the other desired. The persons who
are joined to that fruit are supplied by the instructional
context rather than expressed separately in the verse.

**Close construe.** The first, third, and sixth [higher
knowledges] are the prātihāryas. Instruction is foremost
because of its non-deviation and because it joins [those
instructed] to beneficial and desired fruit.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> The first, third, and sixth are the prātihāryas.
> Instruction is foremost because of non-deviation and
> because it connects with beneficial and desired fruit.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> Extraordinary efficacy, other-mind knowledge, and
> influx-exhaustion knowledge are respectively the wonders
> of extraordinary efficacy, indication, and instruction.
>
> Of these, instruction is foremost. The other two can
> be reproduced through special techniques, whereas
> instruction according to things as they are does not
> admit that substitution. The first two principally
> attract minds; instruction connects those instructed
> with beneficial and desired fruit by teaching the means.

Source: Pradhan 424|08–425|03 in the local Bhāṣya
extract, checked against the running source. The wording
about reproduction follows the extract's intelligible
reading and the examples that follow; the corresponding
running-source verb is damaged.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> Drawing another's attention and guiding that person
> to a beneficial result are different accomplishments.
> Instruction has primacy because its efficacy depends
> on fidelity to how things are and on communicating
> the means to the fruit.

The source does not reduce the first two to uselessness:
they can turn the minds of those to be trained. It gives
instruction a further determination through the means
and resulting benefit, rather than merely greater power
to attract or astonish.

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**`Prātihārya`.** A wonder or extraordinary demonstration.
The Bhāṣya offers explanations through drawing the minds
of those to be trained powerfully at the outset, or
turning the minds of the resistant and indifferent.
These are the source's explanatory derivations, not a
modern historical etymology established here.

**`Vineya`.** One to be trained or instructed. The
recipient's mind is part of the source's explanation
of the prātihāryas, not an incidental spectator added
to an otherwise purely private capacity.

**`Ādeśanā`.** Indication or declaration, here associated
with other-mind knowledge. The prātihārya designation
concerns its demonstrative role; it is not simply a
new synonym for every possible cognition of another mind.

**`Anuśāsana`.** Instruction or directive teaching. The
Bhāṣya uses this expansion of `śāsana` and explains its
benefit through teaching the means. The term here does
not mean legislation merely because other contexts can
give `śāsana` a sense of rule or command.

**`Avyabhicāritva`.** Non-deviation. In this argument it
is clarified by the contrast between manifestations
reproducible through special techniques and instruction
according to things as they are. It does not assert that
every utterance of any teacher invariably succeeds.

**`Gāndhārī / Īkṣaṇikā vidyā`.** The named techniques
said in the passage to enable travel through the sky
and knowledge of another mind, respectively. `Vidyā`
here names a special art or technique; it must not be
silently equated with the three vidyās of VAK 7.45–7.46.
These are source-reported examples, not independently
verified claims about such techniques.

**`Yathābhūtānuśāsana`.** Instruction according to things
as they are. This specifies the instruction whose
primacy is defended, rather than treating the mere
appearance of teaching as sufficient.

**`Āvarjana`.** Attraction or turning toward. This is
the principal effect assigned to the first two demonstrations
in the comparison, not a claim that every instance of
extraordinary efficacy has no other function.

**`Upāyopadeśa`.** Teaching or indicating the means.
This supplies the explicit explanation of connection
to the fruit. It should remain visible beside the two
adjectives qualifying the result: beneficial and desired.

## 8. Logical Determination

| Selected higher knowledge | Number in the sixfold list | Prātihārya |
|---|---|---|
| Extraordinary efficacy | First | Extraordinary efficacy, `ṛddhi` |
| Other-mind knowledge | Third | Indication, `ādeśanā` |
| Influx-exhaustion knowledge | Sixth | Instruction, `anuśāsana` |

The third vidyā and the sixth abhijñā refer to the same
influx-exhaustion member under different enumerations.
The present mapping does not assert that every speech
act by someone possessing exhaustion-knowledge is a
prātihārya of instruction.

**Why the demonstrations attract.** The Bhāṣya gives
two alternative explanations of the name: powerful initial
drawing of learners' minds, and turning resistant or
indifferent minds. The latter is not a doctrine that
all learners begin in hostility or that instruction
requires coercion.

**The first reason for primacy.** The source names
Gāndhārī and Īkṣaṇikā as alternative means to manifestations
associated with the first two wonders. Such manifestations
therefore do not uniquely attest the liberative attainment
that a spectator might infer from them. “Imitation” here
need not mean a fraudulent illusion: the passage presents
other means of producing the relevant effects.

For instruction, the qualifying expression is “according
to things as they are.” Its validity cannot be replaced
by the display of another capacity. This is the local
contrast supporting non-deviation, not a proof that
truthful instructions cannot be verbally copied.

**The second reason for primacy.** The first two principally
attract. Instruction connects to beneficial and desired
fruit through teaching the means. The stated causal
relation is therefore:

```text
Instruction of the means
    → connection with beneficial and desired fruit
```

The passage does not supply a complete sequence of
pedagogical stages, guarantee every listener's attainment,
or require the beneficial result to be every desire the
learner happens to have.

```text
Ranking established here:
    Instruction is foremost among the three.

Ranking not supplied here:
    Other-mind indication is superior to extraordinary efficacy.

Two grounds of primacy:
    Non-deviation of instruction according to things as they are
    Connection to beneficial and desired fruit through taught means
```

This preserves a comparison of instruction with the
other two without turning the enumeration into a three-
step ascent from display through detection to teaching.

## 9. Interpretive Note

**Control of the research interpretation.** The research
kārikā develops instruction as communicable freedom and
the learner's self-transformation. That is a fruitful
Organon direction, but the source explicitly gives the
more bounded relation of teaching means and conjunction
with fruit. Claims about abolishing a monopoly of spiritual
power, scientific reproducibility, or the universal
success of teaching belong to further interpretation.

Likewise, the local Bhāṣya analysis proposes a hierarchy
from attention capture through hidden-state knowledge
to reliable guidance. The Sanskrit ranks instruction
first; it does not rank the other two against one another.

**Witness cautions.** The running source prints damaged
forms including `agcryam`, `agcya m`, `ṛddhacyādeśane`,
`vikeiyete`, and `yayāphāśena`. The local Bhāṣya extract
regularizes these to intelligible readings including
`agryam`, `ṛddhyādeśane`, `vikriyete`, and `yayā ākāśena`.
Thus the extract is not a mechanically identical copy
of the running witness. The interpretation uses the
regularized extract with these differences disclosed.

The final phrase after `upāyopadeśāt`, ending in
`ṛddhir ity ucyate`, is compressed and awkward in the
transmission. The relation between instruction, means,
and fruit is clear before it. A stronger paraphrase
that this closing phrase independently defines instruction
as “supreme accomplishment” remains provisional; the
verse itself already establishes instruction's primacy.

**Organon reconstruction — Yoga Vidyā and Yoga Technē.**
Yoga Vidyā articulates the truth of the condition and
the end; Yoga Technē, its kriyā–karma companion, concerns
the means through which that end can be attained.
Instruction relates these determinations to another
practitioner by making the means available in teaching.

For Organon, this supports evaluating instruction through
the relation among learner, means, and beneficial result.
Producing an impressive response or detecting a hidden
state is insufficient to establish that relation. The
source's positive criterion is `upāyopadeśa`: the means
must be taught, and the instruction must bear on the fruit.

The project can develop this into a grammar of instructional
operations and their verification. Such a grammar would
be our reconstruction; the passage does not itself specify
software agents, validation protocols, or a complete
pedagogical architecture.

**Review status.** Provisional, Bhāṣya-controlled study.
The three ordinal referents, respective prātihārya names,
two explanations of the name, two reasons for instruction's
primacy, and means-to-fruit relation have been checked
against the local extract and running source. Corrupted
forms and the compressed closing phrase remain marked.
