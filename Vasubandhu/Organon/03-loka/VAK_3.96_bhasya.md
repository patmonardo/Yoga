# VAK_3.96 Bhāṣya — The Cakravartin’s Reach and Limits

## Kārikā Anchor

```iast
ekadvitricaturdvīpā na ca dvau saha buddhavat /
pratyudyāna-svayaṃyāna-kalahāstrajito 'vadhāḥ // 3.96 //
```

> They rule one, two, three, or four continents; nor do two arise together,
> as with Buddhas. Their conquests are by welcome, personal approach,
> conflict, and weapons; all are without killing.

The Bhāṣya resolves the final word: even when conquest uses weapons, killing
does not occur. Thus `avadhāḥ` means “without killing,” not “boundaries.”

## Continuous Conventional Translation

The four Cakravartins rule one, two, three, or four continents respectively.
The iron-wheel ruler is lord of one continent, the copper-wheel ruler of two,
the silver-wheel ruler of three, and the gold-wheel ruler of four. This is a
conventional designation. In the sūtra, only the golden wheel is mentioned,
because the principal case is selected.

Nor do two arise together, as with Buddhas. A sūtra says that it is
impossible for two Tathāgatas—arhats, fully awakened Buddhas, one appearing
before the other—to arise in the world at the same time; only one Tathāgata
arises. The same applies to Cakravartins. The commentary asks what “world”
means here: one three-thousandfold great-thousand world-system, or all
world-systems.

Some say that Buddhas do not arise elsewhere, lest the Blessed One's power be
obstructed: the Blessed One alone is capable everywhere, and where one Buddha
could not train those to be guided, another could not either. A further sūtra
passage has Śāriputra affirm that no ascetic or brahmin is presently equal to
Gautama in awakening, citing the impossibility of two Buddhas arising in the
world.

What, then, of the Blessed One's statement in the *Brahmasūtra* that the
three-thousandfold great-thousand world proceeds under his control? The
commentary calls this an intentional expression: without deliberate
application, the field observed is only that extent; with deliberate
application, the Buddhas' field of knowledge is without limit.

The Nikāyāntarīyas maintain that Buddhas do arise in other world-systems.
Many are seen simultaneously engaged in the accumulations [for awakening];
there is no barrier to several Buddhas arising at once in different
world-systems. Since world-systems are infinite, even a Buddha who lived for
a kalpa could not engage with all of them as he does here—how much less
within an ordinary human lifespan. Here a Buddha engages beings according
to their faculties, time, and place: approaching a person, removing a fault,
and completing a faculty by a particular means.

The commentary returns to the sūtra's phrase “in one world”: does it refer
to one world-system or all? Cakravartins, too, do not arise simultaneously
in this world-system, just as Buddhas do not. If one accepts this rule for
Buddhas, why not accept the statement that the arising of Buddhas is
beneficial to the world? If many arose in many worlds, there would be no
problem; many worlds would receive both prosperity and the highest good.
Why, then, do not two Tathāgatas arise together even in one world? Because
there is no need, and because of the force of their vows. Bodhisattvas
resolve: “Would that I arise as a Buddha in a blind world without a guide,
as a protector for the unprotected.” Their singular appearance also fosters
respect and urgency: people regard the Buddha all the more, thinking one so
rare is hard to find again, and hasten to practice the teaching lest they
become unprotected when he has departed and entered final nirvāṇa.

How, then, do these Cakravartins conquer the earth with their golden and
other wheels? In the corresponding order: by welcome, personal approach,
conflict, and weapons. When the wheel is golden, subordinate kings come
forth to receive the ruler, saying: “Your Majesty's districts are rich,
prosperous, secure, abundant, and crowded with people. May Your Majesty
govern them; we will follow you.” With a silver wheel, the ruler goes to
them himself, and afterward they submit. With a copper wheel, he goes to
them and a quarrel occurs; afterward they submit. With a weapon-wheel, he
goes to them, they take up weapons against one another, and afterward they
bow down.

Yet all Cakravartins are without killing. Even when they conquer by weapons,
killing does not occur. After conquering beings, they establish them in the
ten wholesome courses of action. For this very reason, they are assured of
rebirth among the gods.

A sūtra says that when a Cakravartin appears in the world, seven treasures
also appear: the wheel, elephant, horse, jewel, woman, householder, and
counselor. How can elephants and the other treasures, which are beings,
arise through another's karma? No one arises through another's karma. Rather,
when a being who has accumulated karma that brings about such a connection
is born, that being's own karmas produce these associated treasures.

Is this the only distinction between Cakravartins and other kings? There is
another: they possess the thirty-two marks of a great person, as Buddhas do.
The sage's marks are distinctive in being better situated, more developed,
and more complete; these are the differences between them and the Buddha's
marks.

## Natural Movement

The Bhāṣya first maps wheel material onto territorial scope, then examines
the rule that two Buddhas—or two Cakravartins—do not arise together in one
world. It records a disagreement about whether this excludes Buddhas from
other world-systems, while distinguishing local singularity from universal
exclusivity. It then returns to royal conduct: the four ranks correspond to
four modes of conquest, but all remain subject to the explicit limit of
non-killing. The seven treasures and the Cakravartin's bodily marks complete
the portrait before the next verse turns to first-generation humanity.

## Organon Contact Point

Light contact: the passage keeps scope, mode of action, and ethical limit
distinct. It also records a debate whose answer depends on the intended range
of “world”—one world-system or all. These are textual determinations and a
comparative observation, not an Organon ontology attributed to Vasubandhu.
