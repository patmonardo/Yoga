# VAK_3.03 Bhāṣya — The Formless Realm Has No Station

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> ārūpyadhāturasthānaḥ upapattyā caturvidhaḥ /
> nikāyaṃ jīvitaṃ cātra niśritā cittasantatiḥ // 3.03 //

> The Formless Realm has no spatial station and is fourfold through rebirth.
> Here, the continuity of consciousness rests upon class and the
> life-Faculty.

“Spatial” and the expanded terms “common class-character” and “life-faculty”
come from the Bhāṣya. “Is said to” is also necessary: the commentary first
gives the Ābhidhārmika interpretation of the verse and then subjects its
support-model to sustained criticism.

## 3. Continuous Translation

The Formless Realm has no station. For formless dharmas have no spatial
station. It is the rule that past and future dharmas, avijñapti, and formless
dharmas are not spatially situated.

Nevertheless, it is fourfold according to rebirth. The Formless Realm is
fourfold through differentiation of rebirth: namely, the Essence of the
infinitude of space, the Essence of the infinitude of Cognition,
the Essence of nothingness, and the Essence of neither perception nor
non-perception. But these are not distinguished as higher and lower by spatial
location. In whatever place those who have attained the corresponding
attainment die, in that very place they are reborn. And when they later die
from that existence, their intermediate existence arises in that same place.

Just as the continuity of consciousness among beings possessing form proceeds
in dependence upon form, upon what does it proceed among beings in the
Formless Realm?

“Here the continuity of consciousness depends upon class and life.” The
Ābhidhārmikas explain this as dependence upon the common class-character and
the life-faculty.

If so, why does the continuity of consciousness among beings possessing form
not proceed in dependence upon those same two? Because of weakness. What
makes that continuity strong in the Formless Realm? Its arising from a
distinctive attainment. For in that attainment, perception of form has
disappeared. If the continuity is strong for precisely that reason, however,
it should proceed by that strength itself; what need is there of a further
support?

Another difficulty must also be stated. Just as the common class-character and
the life-faculty among beings possessing form proceed in dependence upon form,
upon what do they proceed among formless beings? The same two support one
another. Then why do those same two not support one another among beings
possessing form? Because they are weak there. What, then, makes them strong in
the Formless Realm? Their arising from a distinctive attainment. But this
applies equally to the continuity of consciousness, or to consciousness and
the mental factors themselves.

The Sautrāntikas therefore say that there is no such mutual support for the
continuity of consciousness among formless beings. Rather, when the projecting
cause of a continuity of consciousness has not become free from craving for
form, that continuity arises together with form and proceeds in dependence
upon form. But when its cause has become free from craving for form, it
proceeds without requiring form, because its cause has turned away from form.

Why are these called the Desire, Form, and Formless Realms? A domain is so
called because it bears its own characteristic. The domain connected with
desire is the Desire Realm. The domain connected with form is the
Form Realm, with the middle member elided, as in compounds such as
“diamond-child” and “pepper-drink.” Because form does not exist there, it is
formless. The state of being formless is formlessness. Alternatively, what is
capable of being formed is “formable”; what is not formable is “formless,” and
its state is formlessness. The domain connected with that is the
Formless Realm. Or the Desire Realm is the domain of desires, that which
bears desires. The Form and Formless Realms should be understood similarly.

What is this thing called desire? In brief, it is craving associated with
morsel-food and sexual intercourse. For a verse says: “The attractive things
in the world are not desires; a person's desire is craving born of
conception. The attractive things remain just as they are in the world, while
the resolute discipline desire concerning them.”

An Ājīvaka answered the venerable Śāriputra: “If the attractive things in the
world are not desires, and you call conceptual craving desire, then a monk who
conceives unwholesome thoughts will still be an enjoyer of desires.”
Śāriputra replied: “If the attractive things in the world are desires, and
conceptual craving is not desire for you, then your own teacher will be an
enjoyer of desires merely upon seeing delightful forms.”

Are all dharmas that occur in the three Realms connected with their
respective Realms? No. Those in which craving for desire, form, or
formlessness lies latent are connected with that Realm. What, then, are those
three cravings? Those which lie latent in the three respective Realms. Is
this not the circular explanation of the horse and its tether—“Whose tether
is this? The horse's. Whose horse is this? The tether's”—so that neither is
known?

It is not circular in this way, because the locations within the Desire Realm
have already been specified. The craving of one not free from attachment to
those locations is desire-craving, and the dharma in which it lies latent is
connected with the Desire Realm. Craving for form and formlessness should be
understood correspondingly in one who is free from attachment to the lower
Realm. Alternatively, craving belonging to a non-concentrated level is
desire-craving; craving in the dhyānas and formless attainments is craving for
form and formlessness.

How can desire-craving occur in a mind of magical creation? Because one takes
delight in that creation after hearing of it or after falling away from it;
or the creator's mind may itself have craving through the force of the
creation, or because odors and tastes are created. This craving belongs to the
Desire Realm, since a mind in the Form Realm does not create those two.

Is there only one system of three Realms? Systems of three Realms have no
end: there are as many domains as the extent of space. For this very reason
there is no appearance of completely new beings. Although innumerable beings
attain complete nirvāṇa at the appearance of each Buddha, beings are never
exhausted, just as space is not exhausted.

How are the world-systems arranged? The Tiryaksūtra says that, just as when the
rain god Īṣādhāra rains there is no break or interval between the
streams of water falling from the sky, so toward the east there is no break
or interval among world-systems contracting and expanding; and likewise in
the south, west, and north. It does not say “above and below.” Others say that
there are systems above and below too, according to a reading of another
school. Above Akaniṣṭha there is again a Desire Realm, and below a
Desire Realm there are again Akaniṣṭhas. One who is free from attachment to
one Desire Realm is free from attachment to all Desire Realms, and likewise
for the Form and Formless Realms. Someone born in a given world-system who
develops a supernormal power based on the first dhyāna reaches only that
world-system's Brahmā-world, not another.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The passage first poses two related problems:

```text
no spatial station
    yet fourfold differentiation by rebirth

no material support
    yet continuity of consciousness
```

The opening expository voice explains `asthāna`, enumerates the four rebirths,
and frames the question of continuity. The Ābhidhārmikas are explicitly named
as interpreting `nikāya` and `jīvita` as `nikāyasabhāga` and `jīvitendriya`.
A questioning voice then tests this interpretation through repeated symmetry
arguments. The final rejection and causal alternative are explicitly
attributed to the Sautrāntikas.

The continuation asks why these are called Realms, defines desire through
craving, tests the definition of realm connection, and asks how many systems
of three Realms exist. It quotes a verse, an Ājīvaka, and Śāriputra, then
reports another school reading about worlds above and below.

The individual objections need not all be assigned personally to Vasubandhu.
The text marks the two school positions, but it does not separately identify
the speaker of every intervening question and reply.

## 5. Non-stationed Yet Fourfold by Rebirth

`Asthāna` is not merely a statement that the Formless Realm is subtle or
distant. The prose gives it a precise scope: formless dharmas are
`adeśastha`, not situated in a spatial place. It places them in this respect
with past dharmas, future dharmas, and avijñapti.

Fourfoldness nevertheless remains. Its differentiating principle is not
`deśa`, place, but `upapattibheda`, difference of rebirth:

| Formless rebirth | Controlled rendering |
|---|---|
| `ākāśānantyāyatana` | Essence of the infinitude of space |
| `vijñānānantyāyatana` | Essence of the infinitude of Cognition |
| `ākiñcanyāyatana` | Essence of nothingness |
| `naivasaṃjñānāsaṃjñāyatana` | Essence of neither perception nor non-perception |

The second name contains `vijñāna`, rendered Cognition here. The distinct
`cittasantati` below is the continuity of Consciousness.

The Bhāṣya expressly denies that their higher and lower order is produced by
spatial location. Its remarks about death, rebirth, and intermediate existence
do not covertly restore four formless places. They explain why a being's
rebirth in the Formless Realm and subsequent intermediate existence arise in
the place where the relevant transition occurs, although the formless dharmas
themselves are not spatially stationed there.

## 6. The Ābhidhārmika Support-Model

Removal of material station produces a further question. In form-bearing
beings, the continuity of consciousness proceeds in dependence upon form. If
there is no form in ārūpya, what bears the corresponding continuity?

The Ābhidhārmika answer expands the Kārikā's compressed terms:

```text
nikāya  → nikāyasabhāga   common class-character
jīvita  → jīvitendriya    life-faculty

cittasantati
    depends upon both
```

Neither term should be converted into an enduring self or an immaterial
substance. They are conditioned dharmas proposed as supports within this
doctrinal account. In the first exchange, the feminine `tasyāḥ` resumes
`cittasantati`: the question is what makes that continuity strong in the
Formless Realm. The reply cites its arising from a distinctive attainment
in which perception of form has disappeared.

That reply does not settle the matter. If the continuity is strong from
that attainment, the critic asks why it needs the proposed supports at all.

## 7. From Mutual Support to a Genetic Account

The second objection presses more deeply. In embodied existence,
`nikāyasabhāga` and `jīvitendriya` themselves depend upon form. Asked what
supports them without form, the Ābhidhārmika reply makes the two support one
another. This produces a symmetry problem:

```text
if mutual support suffices in the Formless Realm,
why does it not suffice among beings possessing form?
```

In this second exchange, `tayoḥ` explicitly refers to the two proposed
supports: they are said to be weak among beings with form and strong in the
Formless Realm because of a distinctive attainment. The critic replies
that this same explanation can equally be applied to the continuity of
consciousness—or to consciousness and its mental factors.

The Sautrāntika conclusion replaces the reciprocal support-pair with a causal
account:

```text
projecting cause not free from craving for form
    → continuity arises together with form
    → continuity depends upon form

projecting cause free from craving for form
    → continuity arises without form
    → no material support is required
```

`Ākṣepahetu`, the projecting or impelling cause, does not here mean a free
subject projecting an appearance. It is the prior causal determinant of the
continuum. Whether that cause remains oriented toward form explains whether
the resulting continuity arises with form or without it.

## 8. Domain, Connection, and World-System

The initial definition, `svalakṣaṇadhāraṇād dhātuḥ`, makes a domain that which
bears its own characteristic. Yet a dharma's occurrence in a domain is not
enough to make it “connected with” that domain. Connection is determined by
the craving capable of lying latent in it. The apparent circle—domain-craving
defined by domain, domain-connection defined by craving—is checked by the
already established locations and by the alternative distinction between
non-concentrated, dhyāna, and formless levels.

This preserves three different relations:

```text
occurs in a domain
    ≠ necessarily connected with that domain

craving lies latent in a dharma
    → that dharma is connected with the corresponding domain

domain
    bears its characteristic field of connected dharmas
```

The discussion then pluralizes the entire three-Realm structure. There are
indefinitely many world-systems, arranged without gaps horizontally and,
according to another school's reading, also above and below. Yet freedom from
attachment to one domain applies to all corresponding domains. Cosmological
plurality does not multiply the kind of attachment or the liberation from it.

## 9. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

The Bhāṣya determines `asthāna` as absence of spatial station. It does not
deny order, causality, rebirth, or continuity. Hence “without station” must not
be paraphrased as unconditioned, omnipresent, or absolute.

Here *ārūpyadhātu* is conventionally rendered **Formless Realm**. The general
Organon mapping of *dhātu* remains **Principle**; the compound-specific Realm
rendering does not replace it.

`Upapattyā caturvidhaḥ` is resolved as fourfold through differences of
rebirth. The four named Essences specify those rebirth modes; the later
`samāpatti` is the corresponding attainment possessed before death. The
prose blocks an interpretation of the four as vertically arranged places.

The second half of the verse receives an explicitly Ābhidhārmika gloss:
`nikāya` is `nikāyasabhāga`, and `jīvita` is `jīvitendriya`. Because the
commentary subsequently rejects the proposed mutual-support explanation, the
translation gives the verse's dependence claim without treating this
Ābhidhārmika gloss as the uncontested final doctrine.

Finally, the Sautrāntika argument does not deny conditioned continuity. It
denies that continuity without form requires the stated pair of mutually
sustaining supports. Its alternative ground is the causal orientation of the
series that gives rise to the continuity.

## 10. Interpretation

The opening sequence of Lokanirdeśa has now successively loosened the equation
of world with place:

```text
VAK 3.01 — Desire Realm
    sentient orders, locations, and supporting receptacle-world

VAK 3.02 — Form Realm
    stations articulated through dhyāna-levels

VAK 3.03 — Formless Realm
    spatial station negated
    differentiation retained through rebirth
    continuity retained through causal genesis
```

The strong result is that `loka` survives the negation of location. A world in
the Kośa sense is not fundamentally a physical container. The Formless Realm
remains within conditioned existence because it still has rebirth,
consciousness-continuity, and a cause determining the mode of its arising.

The Sautrāntika move is especially important for the Organon reconstruction.
It shifts the question from an inventory of hidden supports to the genesis of
the continuum: not “what immaterial thing holds this series up?” but “what
causal orientation projects or impels this series with or without form?” This
is a textual use of `ākṣepa`, not yet the Organon's more general theory of
Projection or Outflow.

The continuation then determines what makes a Dharma belong to a Realm: mere
occurrence there is insufficient; the relation to the corresponding craving
matters. It also shows that the three-Realm structure can recur across
innumerable world-systems without multiplying the kind of craving or the
scope of dispassion. These determinations belong to the 3.03 inquiry, before
the five destinies are introduced.

Accordingly, ārūpya cannot simply be identified with the Absolute. It is a
maximal abstraction from material station internal to the World System, not
an escape from conditioned genesis. That boundary is an Organon consequence
drawn from the text's retained `upapatti`, `cittasantati`, and `hetu`; it is
not wording placed in Vasubandhu's mouth.
