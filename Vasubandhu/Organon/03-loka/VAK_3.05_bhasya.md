# VAK_3.05 Bhāṣya — Configurations of Body and Perception

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> nānātvakāyasaṃjñāś ca nānākāyaikasaṃjñinaḥ /
> viparyayāc caikakāyasaṃjñāś cārūpiṇas trayaḥ // 3.05 //

> There are those with diverse bodies and diverse perceptions, those with
> diverse bodies and one perception, and, by reversal, those with one body
> and diverse or one perception; together with the three formless classes.

The verse constructs four embodied combinations from two variables, body and
perception, and adds three formless classes. The running exposition immediately
supplies the name completed in the opening words of VAK 3.06: these are the
seven `vijñānasthiti`, stations of consciousness.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Within these same three domains and five destinies, the following should be
understood in order: those with diverse bodies and diverse perceptions; those
with diverse bodies and one perception; by reversal, those with one body and
diverse or one perception; and the three formless classes. These are the seven
stations of consciousness.

The sūtra says: “There are form-possessing beings with diverse bodies and
diverse perceptions, such as humans and certain devas. This is the first
station of consciousness.” Which are those certain devas? Those of
the desire-domain and those belonging to the level of the first dhyāna, apart
from the beings first arisen there.

They have diverse bodies because their bodies differ, having many colors,
sex-characteristics, and configurations. They have diverse perceptions
because they possess perceptions of pleasure, pain, and neither pain nor
pleasure.

The sūtra continues: “There are form-possessing beings with diverse bodies
and one perception, such as the Brahmakāyika devas first arisen there. This is
the second station of consciousness.”

Those newly arisen beings all have the same perception: “We were created by
this Brahmā.” Brahmā likewise thinks, “These were created by me.” They
therefore have one perception because their perceptions have the same form.
But Mahābrahmā's height and breadth, bodily shape, speech, and clothing are of
one kind, while those of his assembly are of another; hence their bodies are
diverse.

The sūtra says that they think: “We saw this being, long-lived and remaining
for a long period, who wished, ‘Oh, that other beings might arise here in my
assembly’; and after this being formed that aspiration, we arose here.” How
did they see him?

Some say they saw him while dwelling among the Ābhāsvaras, from which they
fell. But without having attained the second-dhyāna absorption, how could they
remember a former dwelling belonging to that level? And if they had attained
it, how could they become fixed in a view grasping discipline and observance
with Mahābrahmā as its object?

Others say they saw him while in intermediate existence. But there too they
could not remain for a long period, since nothing obstructed their rebirth.
How, then, could they think that they had seen this being remaining for a long
time? Therefore, while dwelling there with him, they remembered his earlier
history and saw that he had remained for a long time. Later, having seen this,
the thought “we saw him” arose in them.

The sūtra continues: “There are form-possessing beings with one body and
diverse perceptions, such as the Ābhāsvara devas. This is the third station of
consciousness.” Because the terminal class is named here, the
entire second dhyāna must be understood; otherwise, under which station would
the Parīttābhas and Apramāṇābhas be placed? They have one body because color,
sex-characteristics, and bodily configuration do not differ. They have
diverse perceptions because they perceive pleasure and neither pain nor
pleasure.

It is said that, when wearied by the faculty of gladness on the principal
level, they make the faculty of equanimity from the neighboring level
manifest; and when wearied by equanimity on the neighboring level, they again
make gladness from the principal level manifest. This resembles powerful
persons who, wearied by sensual enjoyment, experience delight in Dharma, and,
wearied by delight in Dharma, return to sensual enjoyment.

Would the same consequence not apply to the Śubhakṛtsnas? No, because they do
not become weary of their pleasure. Why? The Sautrāntikas explain that their
pleasure is tranquil, whereas gladness is untranquil because it excites the
mind.

The sūtra gives another explanation of the Ābhāsvaras' diverse perceptions.
Those newly arisen in the Ābhāsvara divine class, not yet knowledgeable about
the contraction and expansion of this world, see the flame and become afraid,
alarmed, and agitated: “This flame, having burned the empty Brahmā palace,
will come down here at once.” Those long established among the Ābhāsvaras,
who understand the world's contraction and expansion, reassure them: “Do not
be afraid, venerable ones. Previously too this flame burned the empty Brahmā
palace and disappeared right there.” Thus they have diverse perceptions
because they recognize the flame as approaching or departing and because
they perceive fear or fearlessness—not because of perceptions of pleasure
and neither pain nor pleasure.

The sūtra continues: “There are form-possessing beings with one body and one
perception, such as the Śubhakṛtsna devas. This is the fourth station of
consciousness.” They have one body because their color,
configuration, and sex-characteristics do not differ. They have one
perception because they perceive pleasure.

Thus, in the first dhyāna, beings have one perception through an afflicted
perception; in the second they have diverse perceptions through wholesome
perception; and in the third they have one perception through a
maturation-born perception. The three formless classes are as stated in the
sūtra. These are the seven stations of consciousness.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The commentary follows the four embodied combinations in order and then adds
the three formless classes:

```text
diverse body + diverse perception
diverse body + one perception
one body     + diverse perception
one body     + one perception
three formless classes
```

The sūtra supplies each embodied formula and exemplary class. The expository
voice defines the relevant bodily and perceptual differences, raises problems
about the Brahmā narrative, and ensures that a named terminal deva-class can
stand for its whole dhyāna. Two unnamed views explain when the newly arisen
Brahmakāyikas saw Brahmā. The Sautrāntikas are explicitly named only in the
explanation contrasting tranquil pleasure with mentally agitating gladness.

The cosmic-fire passage is itself introduced as a sūtra explanation. It
should not be collapsed into the preceding pleasure/equanimity account: the
text presents two different grounds for the Ābhāsvaras' perceptual diversity.

## 5. The Body–Perception Matrix

The verse does not merely list heavenly populations. It varies two features
independently:

| Body | Perception | Exemplary class |
|---|---|---|
| diverse | diverse | humans and certain devas |
| diverse | one | newly arisen Brahmakāyikas |
| one | diverse | second-dhyāna devas, represented by Ābhāsvaras |
| one | one | Śubhakṛtsnas |

Bodily difference is analyzed through color, sex-characteristics, bodily
configuration, and, in the Brahmā case, stature, appearance, speech, and
clothing. “One body” therefore means sameness of the relevant class-features,
not numerical identity of one body shared by many beings.

Perceptual unity likewise means a common determining perception, not one
numerically identical act of perception. Newly arisen Brahmakāyikas share the
creation-belief; Śubhakṛtsnas share the perception of pleasure. The matrix
classifies continuities by common or diverse modes, not by merging them into a
collective consciousness.

## 6. Perception Is More Than Feeling-Tone

The first explanation often differentiates `saṃjñā` through the associated
feeling-tone: pleasure, pain, or neither. The Bhāṣya nevertheless refuses a
simple identity between perception and feeling. In the fire passage, the
Ābhāsvaras are diverse in perception because the same event is recognized
differently:

```text
newly arisen beings
    flame → approaching danger → fear

long-established beings
    flame → known cosmic cycle → reassurance
```

Their body-class and the visible event are the same, but the recognitional
determination differs according to their knowledge of contraction and
expansion. `Saṃjñā` here must therefore retain the force of perception and
recognition; translating it only as sensation or feeling would lose the
argument.

The text also preserves two explanations of this class's diversity. The first
uses alternation between gladness and equanimity; the scriptural explanation
uses recognition of the flame's arrival and departure together with fear and
fearlessness. The closing `na ... iti` explicitly prefers the latter account
over diversity based merely on pleasure and neutral feeling.

## 7. Brahmā, Shared Creation-Belief, and Difference

The second station is especially instructive. Brahmā and the newly arisen
members of his assembly converge upon one structured belief:

```text
Brahmā
    “I created these beings.”

assembly
    “This Brahmā created us.”
```

The sameness of perception is therefore compatible with opposed positions
within the same relation. Creator and created do not entertain the same
sentence, but their cognitions have one form and sustain one creation-account.
Meanwhile their bodies remain diverse through differences of rank and
appearance.

The Bhāṣya then asks how the assembly could remember seeing Brahmā before its
arrival. It rejects or pressures both a memory from the Ābhāsvara state and a
vision during intermediate existence, before explaining the statement through
their later recollection of Brahmā's prior history while dwelling with him.
This is a local explanation of the cited narrative, not a general endorsement
of Brahmā as creator.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

The transmitted `cārūpiṇasrayaḥ` cannot stand without repair. The prose's
`ārūpyās trayaḥ` confirms the reading “and the three formless classes.” The
repair remains disclosed because the working source is non-critical.

`Viparyayāt`, “by reversal,” completes the two one-body combinations after the
two diverse-body combinations. Nothing in this unit requires the stronger
sense of cognitive error familiar from other contexts.

`Kāya` is translated “body,” with “body-configuration” useful in analysis.
The prose gives concrete criteria for unity and difference. `Saṃjñā` is
translated “perception,” while its recognitional force is retained wherever
the beings understand the same flame as danger or as a familiar phase of a
cosmic process.

`Vijñānasthiti` is translated “station of consciousness.” The earlier
rendering “station of differentiated cognition” added a qualifier absent from
the Sanskrit. The distinction between the seven stations is supplied by their
listed conditions, not by an adjective modifying `vijñāna`.

The phrase `vijñānasthitayaḥ sapta` belongs metrically to VAK 3.06, but it is
syntactically and explanatorily required by the prose on 3.05. This Study
includes that naming phrase and stops after the seven classes are completed;
it leaves the subsequent inquiry into what constitutes a cognition-station to
VAK 3.06.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

VAK 3.04 distinguished field, cause, transition, resultant placement, and the
designation of a being. VAK 3.05 adds a configuration internal to that
placement:

```text
domain
    destiny
        body-configuration
        perception-configuration
            station of consciousness
```

The decisive result is a non-entailment: bodily sameness does not entail
perceptual sameness, and bodily difference does not entail perceptual
difference. Embodiment and recognition are related determinations, but neither
can be reduced to the other.

For a later Organon learning pass, the fire example will be particularly
valuable. One event receives different world-meanings according to the
continuity's knowledge and history. Yet the Bhāṣya does not thereby make the
external event a free invention of the perceiver: the flame is common, while
its arrival, danger, and place in the cosmic cycle are differently recognized.

That is enough analysis for the present translation pass. We need not yet turn
the matrix into an Oculus schema or infer a complete theory of embodiment.
The present gain is more restrained and more secure: the Kośa formally
coordinates bodily and perceptual configurations without identifying them.
