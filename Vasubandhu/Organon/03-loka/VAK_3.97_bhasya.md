# VAK_3.97 Bhāṣya — From Earth-Savor to Social Order

## Kārikā Anchor

```iast
deśasthottaptapūrṇatvair lakṣaṇātiśayo muneḥ /
prāg āsan rūpivat sattvāḥ rasarāgāt tataḥ śanaiḥ // 3.97 //
```

> The Muni's marks are superior through their placement, vividness, and
> completeness. Formerly beings were like rūpa-realm beings; then, through
> attachment to savor, [they changed] gradually.

The Bhāṣya first completes the comparison between Buddha and Cakravartin,
then narrates the gradual material and social transformation of first-age
humanity.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

Is the possession of a wheel the only distinction between Cakravartins and
other kings? There is another: they possess the thirty-two marks of a great
person, as Buddhas do. The Muni's marks are superior through their more exact
placement, more vivid manifestation, and greater completeness.

Were first-age humans already governed by kings? No. What were they like?
The text says that beings formerly were “upivāt”—a difficult reading. The
Bhāṣya immediately explains: first-age humans were like beings of the form
realm. A sūtra describes them as embodied and mind-made, complete in all
limbs, unimpaired, possessing unimpaired faculties, beautiful, with stable
complexion, self-luminous, able to move through the sky, feeding on joy and
having joy as food, long-lived and remaining so for a long time.

Then, gradually, through attachment to savor: for beings in that condition,
an earth-savor appeared, sweet like honey. One being of a greedy disposition
smelled it, tasted it, and ate it. Other beings did the same. This was the
beginning of morsel-food. Through habituation to that food, hardness and
heaviness entered their bodies, and their powers disappeared. When darkness
arose, the sun and moon appeared.

As these beings became greedy for its taste, the earth-savor gradually
disappeared, and an earth-crust appeared. They became greedy for that too;
it disappeared, and a forest-creeper appeared. They became greedy for that;
it too disappeared. Unploughed and unsown rice then grew, and they ate it.

Because this food was now coarse, channels for urine and feces appeared in
their bodies to discharge its residue, along with female and male sexual
organs; bodily forms became differentiated. As they looked at one another,
through the force of former habits, with unwise attention and grasping, sexual
passion arose and they became sexually involved. This was the beginning of
sexual passion and the seizure of those overcome by sensuality.

They began taking the rice in the evening for the evening meal and in the
morning for breakfast. Then one being of a lazy disposition made a store;
other beings did the same. When a sense of “mine” arose among them, the rice,
though cut again and again, no longer grew back.

They divided the fields, laid claim to their own plots, and began taking what
belonged to others. This was the beginning of theft. To restrain them, the
people assembled and hired a particular man to guard the fields, paying him
one sixth [of the produce]. He became the ruler over the fields, a
`kṣatriya`; thus the designation “kṣatriya” arose. Because he was approved
by the great body of people and delighted the people, the designation
“King Mahāsammata” arose. This was the beginning of the succession of kings.

Those whose minds had turned outward from the home received the
designation “Brahmins.” Later, out of a king's greed, some did not share
[their produce], and theft became widespread among beings. The king approached
them with weapons. Then others began to speak falsely, saying, “We did not do
that.”

## Natural Movement

The passage moves from first-age human qualities through successive changes
in food, body, and social relations. Greed for savor begins the process;
coarser nourishment accompanies bodily change, while appropriation and
scarcity lead to property claims, theft, and appointed rule. The Bhāṣya
explains the names `kṣatriya` and `Mahāsammata` within this account of
kingship's origin. It ends with false speech, immediately before 3.98
continues the decline in lifespan.

## Organon Contact Point

Light contact: the commentary narrates a sequence in which changing habits
of nourishment and appropriation accompany changes in bodies and social
institutions. This remains Vasubandhu's cosmological account in the source;
any comparison with project concepts is secondary.
