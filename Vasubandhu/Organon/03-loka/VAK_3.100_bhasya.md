# VAK_3.100 Bhāṣya — Three Contractions and Their Limits

## Kārikā Anchor

```iast
saṃvartanyaḥ punas tisro bhavanti agny-ambu-vāyubhiḥ /
dhyānatrayaṃ dvitīyādi śīrṣa tāsāṃ yathākramam // 3.100 //
```

> There are three further contractions, by fire, water, and wind. The three
> dhyānas beginning with the second are their respective upper limits.

The Bhāṣya explains the reach of these destructions and places a long debate
about eternal atoms between the verse's two lines.

## Continuous Sanskrit

This unit includes the contraction types, the intervening atom debate, the
upper limits of destruction, and the question that leads into the next verse.
Punctuation and sandhi are lightly regularized; difficult readings are not
critically reconstructed.

**Textual note.** The witness is corrupt in several technical clauses in the
atom debate and in the description of the second-dhyāna disturbance. The
Sanskrit above regularizes spacing and obvious sandhi but does not claim a
critical edition. The closing phrase about “not refuting” the atom argument
is particularly uncertain; its force is left open below.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

What are the contractions? There are three: by fire, water, and wind. Beings
associated with one dhyāna contract together in that domain; hence it is
called a contraction. Fire-contraction occurs through seven suns,
water-contraction through rainwater, and wind-contraction through violent
agitation of wind. In these destructions, not even a subtle part of the
receptacle-world remains.

Some non-Buddhist teachers maintain that atoms are eternal and survive then.
Why do they hold this? So that gross things will not be without a seed for
their arising. But has not wind, endowed with special efficacy and generated
by beings' karma, already been stated to be a seed? The wind at the upper
limit of a contraction can be a condition for the wind that blows in a new
world. The Mahīśāsakas also recite a sūtra saying that seeds are brought by
wind from other world-systems.

Even so, these teachers do not accept that sprouts and the like arise from
seeds. Rather, each arises from its own constituent parts, those parts from
their own parts, and so on down to atoms. What capacity do seeds have to
produce sprouts? None, except that atoms approach them. Why insist on this
account? They say that a dissimilar kind cannot reasonably arise from a
cause; otherwise there would be no regularity.

The reply is that regularity can instead be secured by determinate powers,
as in the arising of sound-products and heat-products. Qualities have diverse
properties, they say, whereas substances do not: homogeneous substances are
observed to produce homogeneous substances, as a mat from reeds and cloth
from threads.

This argument is challenged. What is unproved in it? The examples offered
to establish it: that the mat is something other than the reeds, and the
cloth something other than the threads. Those very same things, arranged in
this or that way, receive the respective names—like a row of ants.

How is this known? A cloth is not perceived when only one thread is joined.
But what would prevent perception of a cloth that already existed then? If
only part of the cloth were present, it would be a portion, not the cloth;
and if the cloth were just the collection, what cloth-part could be other
than the threads? If perception required contact with many supports, the
cloth should become visible when merely ten were joined—or perhaps never,
since the middle and far portions are not in sensory contact. Nor, if the
parts were contacted successively, could there be cognition of a whole from
eye and touch. Therefore, through successive contact and ascertainment of
the parts, cognition occurs of the parts themselves, like the apparent
circle made by a whirling firebrand.

If threads differ in color, kind, and activity, the cloth cannot possess
those qualities as a separate thing. If the cloth is variegated, its cause
would itself have to be heterogeneous; if it is not variegated, one would
not see a patterned cloth or its different sides. Its activity, too, would
be diverse. The analogy with fire is also problematic: if heat and
brightness differ, the corresponding form and touch at its beginning,
middle, and end cannot be explained.

The atom theorist replies that although atoms are imperceptible, their
aggregate is directly perceived, just as they produce effects; people with
an eye disorder, for example, perceive scattered hairs. [The theorist adds
that] one region of the aggregate, like an atom, remains imperceptible.

The response is that the designation “atom” is assigned precisely to form
and related qualities; when these are destroyed, destruction of the atom is
established. If an atom were a substance separate from form and the other
qualities, their destruction would not establish its destruction. But this
separateness is not justified: no one can determine, apart from the
qualities, “these are substances—earth, water, fire, and wind—and these
are their forms and so on.” These are known through eye and touch, as in
burnt wool, cotton, flowers, saffron, and similar things. Since no cognition
of a separate substance arises, cognition concerns only differences among
form and the other qualities.

Recognition of a pot when a heat-produced effect arises is due to common
configuration, as with a row; without seeing a distinguishing mark, there
is no recognition [of a separate substance]. “Who would pay attention to
childish prattle?” The text then says to leave the matter “not refuted” for
the time being; that closing phrase is difficult, and its argumentative
force is uncertain.

Which upper limit belongs to each contraction? The second, third, and
fourth dhyānas are the three respective upper limits. The second dhyāna is
the limit of fire-contraction; everything below it is burned. The third is
the limit of water-contraction; everything below it is soaked or dissolved.
The fourth is the limit of wind-contraction; everything below it is
scattered. The level above the reach of a contraction is called its
“upper limit.”

Why are the first, second, and third dhyānas destroyed by fire, water, and
wind? Because their internal disturbances are analogous to those forces.
In the first dhyāna, examination and investigation are disturbances; because
they scorch the mind, they are like fire. In the second, joy is a disturbance;
through association with pliancy it softens the support, and is water-like.
For this reason a sūtra says that, when the harshness of the whole body has
subsided in this [dhyāna], the faculty of suffering ceases. In the third
dhyāna are inhalation and exhalation, which are winds themselves. Thus,
where a corresponding internal disturbance occurs in a meditative attainment,
a corresponding external disturbance occurs in the world associated with
that attainment.

Why is there no earth-contraction? The receptacle is itself called earth;
earth is opposed by fire, water, and wind—not by earth itself. Why, then,
is there no contraction in the fourth dhyāna? Because there is no
`āneñja`—no disturbance—there. The Blessed One called the fourth dhyāna
`āneñja` because it is free from internal disturbance. Therefore no
corresponding external disturbance operates there, and there is no
contraction [of that domain]. Others say it is due to the influence of the
Pure Abodes: those beings cannot enter the formless realm, nor go elsewhere.
Would that not mean, then, that the receptacle of the fourth dhyāna is
eternal? The next verse takes up this question.

## Natural Movement

The Bhāṣya first states the three destruction modes and denies that an
eternal atomic residue is needed for renewed formation. It then identifies
the upper limit of each contraction and explains the mapping through
analogous internal disturbances in the first three dhyānas. The fourth
dhyāna's lack of disturbance explains its immunity here, while an alternate
appeal to the Pure Abodes leads to the question of permanence answered in
3.101.

## Organon Contact Point

Light contact: the commentary relates external cosmic disturbances to
internal disturbances associated with meditative levels. This relation is
presented as Vasubandhu's local explanation, not as a generalized Organon
law.
