# VAK_3.57 Bhāṣya — Anavatapta, the Four Rivers, and the Jambū Tree

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
ihottareṇa kīṭādrinavakād dhimavāṃs tataḥ /
pañcāśadvistṛtāyāmaṃ saro 'rvāg gandhamādanāt // 3.57 //
```

> Here in Jambūdvīpa, northward beyond the nine Kīṭa mountains, is Himavān.
> Beyond it and before Gandhamādana lies a lake fifty yojanas in breadth and
> fifty in length.

The Bhāṣya resolves the nine mountains as three successive groups of three,
names the lake Anavatapta, lists its four rivers, describes its water and
accessibility, and connects the nearby Jambū tree—or alternatively its
fruit—with the name Jambūdvīpa.

## 2. Continuous Sanskrit

```iast
Ihottareṇa kīṭādrinavakād dhimavān.

Ihaiva Jambūdvīpe, uttareṇāsya Jambūdvīpasya kṛṣṇaparvatās trayaḥ; tān
atikramyāpare trayaḥ, punaś ca traya iti navabhyaḥ kīṭaparvatebhyaḥ pareṇa
Himavān parvataḥ.

Tataḥ

pañcāśadvistṛtāyāmaṃ saro 'rvāg Gandhamādanāt.

Tasmād Dhimavataḥ pareṇānavataptaṃ nāma saro Gandhamādanād arvāk, yataś
catasro nadyaḥ sravanti: Gaṅgā, Sindhuḥ, Śītā, Vakṣuś ca. Tasya pañcāśad
yojanāni vistāraḥ, pañcāśad āyāmaḥ. Pūrṇam aṣṭāṅgopetasyāmbhasaḥ. Durgamaṃ
ca manuṣyāṇām anṛddhimatām.

Tasyaiva cāntike Jambūr abhinirvṛttā, madhurasvādūni yasyāḥ phalāni. Tasyā
adhikāreṇāyaṃ Jambūdvīpa iti khyātaḥ; tatphalādhikāreṇa vā Jambūdvīpa iti.
```

The running witness contains minor mechanical forms, including `sravnti` and
`manuaṣyāṇām`, and a compressed expression for water endowed with eight
qualities. The continuous text regularizes evident spelling and sandhi while
leaving the exact syntax of the eight-quality phrase provisional.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Here, to the north, beyond the nine Kīṭa mountains, is Himavān. Here within
Jambūdvīpa, to its north, there are three black mountains. After crossing
them there are another three and then again three. Thus, beyond the nine Kīṭa
mountains stands Mount Himavān.

Beyond it lies a lake fifty yojanas in breadth and length, on this side of
Gandhamādana. Beyond Himavān but before Gandhamādana is the lake named
Anavatapta. From it flow four rivers: the Gaṅgā, Sindhu, Śītā, and Vakṣu. Its
breadth is fifty yojanas and its length is fifty. It is filled with water
endowed with eight qualities. It is difficult to reach for human beings who
do not possess extraordinary power.

Near that very lake a Jambū tree has come into formation, whose fruits are
sweet in taste. With reference to that tree, this continent is known as
Jambūdvīpa—or it is called Jambūdvīpa with reference to the tree's fruit.

## 4. Natural Movement and Voices

The commentary develops one directed regional sequence:

```text
northward within Jambūdvīpa
    → three + three + three black mountains
        → Himavān
            → Anavatapta before Gandhamādana
                → four rivers flow outward
                    → nearby Jambū tree and fruit
                        → name Jambūdvīpa
```

Vasubandhu gives the main geographical exposition. The final `vā`, “or,”
marks two naming explanations: immediate reference to the Jambū tree, or
reference specifically to its fruit.

No opposing school is named. The alternatives share the same geographical
feature-complex and differ only in which member is the immediate ground of
the continent's designation.

## 5. The Nine Mountains and Himavān

The compound `kīṭādrinavaka` is unpacked as a traversed series:

```text
first group     three black mountains
second group    another three
third group     another three
                -------------------
total           nine mountains
```

Himavān lies beyond all nine. `Atikramya`, having crossed or passed beyond,
makes this a route relation rather than a merely unordered set of landmarks.

The Bhāṣya calls the barriers `kṛṣṇaparvata`, black mountains, while the
Kārikā uses the compact `Kīṭa` mountain-name. Whether these are simple glosses
of one another or reflect a textual or traditional variant requires witness
comparison.

The word `iha`, here, is also resolved: the route lies within Jambūdvīpa. The
passage has moved from the four-continent system to a regional geography
inside one continent.

## 6. Anavatapta and the Four Rivers

The unnamed lake of the Kārikā is identified as Anavatapta and placed between
two mountains:

```text
Himavān
    → beyond it: Anavatapta
        → before reaching Gandhamādana
```

Its dimensions are squarely stated:

| Axis | Measure |
|---|---:|
| breadth, `vistāra` | 50 yojanas |
| length, `āyāma` | 50 yojanas |

From this bounded source flow four named rivers:

```text
Anavatapta
    ├── Gaṅgā
    ├── Sindhu
    ├── Śītā
    └── Vakṣu
```

The lake is therefore determined not only by location and size but by its
source-function within a larger hydrological network.

Its water possesses eight qualities, recalling the qualified waters already
described for the Śītā seas. The present unit does not enumerate them anew,
so the translation should not insert a fresh list into Vasubandhu's sentence.

## 7. Accessibility and Extraordinary Power

Anavatapta is `durgama`, difficult of access, for humans who are
`anṛddhimat`, without `ṛddhi`:

```text
same geographical locus
    + human without ṛddhi
        → difficult to reach

the stated limitation
    does not include one possessing ṛddhi
```

The passage does not explicitly say that every person possessing ṛddhi will
reach the lake. It removes such persons from the stated incapacity.

Accessibility is therefore relational. It depends on the route's barriers
and the traveler's capability, not upon the target considered in isolation.

`Ṛddhi` is retained as a technical term and glossed as extraordinary power.
Reducing it to ordinary physical fitness or transportation would replace the
source's doctrinal capability with a modern category.

## 8. Tree, Fruit, and the Name Jambūdvīpa

Near the lake grows the Jambū tree, whose fruit is sweet. The Bhāṣya offers
two closely connected naming structures:

```text
principal phrasing
    Jambū tree
        → name of the containing continent

alternative phrasing
    fruit of the Jambū tree
        → name of the containing continent
```

`Adhikāra` is provisionally rendered “with reference to.” It can carry a
sense of predominating association or relevant heading; the exact semantic
route from tree or fruit to place-name deserves philological review.

The whole region is named through a salient internal feature. This is not a
claim that the tree causes the continent to exist. It concerns denomination:
which contained feature grounds the name of the containing whole.

The two explanations should remain visible rather than being compressed into
the vague statement that the continent is “associated with Jambū.”

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

VAK 3.57 changes cosmographical scale. The preceding verses built continents
from external measure and form; this one gives Jambūdvīpa an internal route,
source-node, capability condition, and naming ground.

For the Organon Dataset, the unit separates:

```text
containment        route lies within Jambūdvīpa
ordered traversal  nine mountains before Himavān
relative placement Anavatapta between Himavān and Gandhamādana
flow relation       four rivers issue from one lake
access condition    human capability indexed by ṛddhi
naming relation     tree or fruit designates the whole continent
```

This is an Organon reconstruction, not Vasubandhu's formal vocabulary. It
shows why a place is not adequately modeled by coordinates alone. A region
becomes operationally meaningful through paths, barriers, flows, agents, and
names.

The naming movement is especially relevant to Projection as labeling: a
label for the whole is projected from a selected internal feature. Yet the
Bhāṣya preserves the material relation grounding that label—the nearby tree
and its fruit—so the name is neither arbitrary nor identical with the thing
named.

No hidden transcendental architecture need be imposed on this geography.
Its philosophical force is already present in the explicit network of
relations through which the region becomes intelligible.

## 10. Review Status

This Study restores the complete VAK 3.57 unit at 162.17–162.27: the three
groups of three northern mountains, Himavān, Anavatapta lake before
Gandhamādana, its dimensions and eight-qualified water, the four rivers, the
access condition involving `ṛddhi`, and the tree- and fruit-based explanations
of the name Jambūdvīpa.

The Sanskrit and translation remain provisional. The `Kīṭa`/`kṛṣṇaparvata`
relation, the damaged eight-quality phrase, the precise force of `ṛddhi` in
this travel context, the Jambū botanical designation, and `adhikāra` in the
naming explanations require comparison with critical Sanskrit, Tibetan,
Chinese, and established translations.
