# VAK_3.71 Bhāṣya — Three Modes of Desire and Happiness

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
kāmopapattayas tisraḥ kāmadevāḥ samānuṣāḥ /
sukhopapattayas tisro nava tridhyānabhūmayaḥ // 3.71 //
```

> There are three modes of sensual-desire rebirth among humans and the
> kāma-devas, and three modes of happy rebirth among the nine grounds of the
> first three dhyānas.

The Bhāṣya classifies the three kāmopapattis by how desired objects are
available and enjoyed: already present, produced by oneself, or produced by
others. It then groups the nine grounds of the first three dhyānas into three
sukhopapattis, distinguished by the kind of sustained pleasure. The
intermediate dhyāna is expressly left as a case requiring examination.

## 2. Continuous Sanskrit

```iast
Tad atra kāmadhātau veditavyāḥ:

Kāmopapattayas tisraḥ kāmadevāḥ samānuṣāḥ.

Kathaṃ kṛtvā?

Santi sattvāḥ pratyupasthitakāmāḥ; pratyupasthiteṣu kāmeṣv aiśvaryaṃ vaśe
vartayanti. Tadyathā manuṣyās tadekatyāś ca devāḥ. Te punaś catvāro
devanikāyāḥ.

Santi sattvā nirmitakāmāḥ; nirmāya kāmān aiśvaryaṃ vaśe vartayanti.
Tadyathā devā Nirmāṇaratayaḥ.

Santi sattvāḥ paranirmitakāmāḥ; paranirmiteṣu kāmeṣv aiśvaryaṃ vaśe
vartayanti. Tadyathā devāḥ Paranirmitavaśavartinaḥ.

Tā etā yathotpannaparibhogitvāt, yathecchātmanirmitaparibhogitvāt,
yathecchātmaparanirmitaparibhogitvāc ca tisraḥ kāmopapattaya ity ucyante.

Rūpadhātau tu:

Sukhopapattayas tisro nava tridhyānabhūmayaḥ.

Triṣu dhyāneṣu yā nava bhūmayaḥ, tāḥ tisraḥ sukhopapattayaḥ. Te hi devā
vivekajena, samādhijena ca prītisukhena, niṣprītikena ca sukhena sukhaṃ
viharanto dīrgham adhvānaṃ tiṣṭhanti.

Ata etā nirduḥkha-dīrgha-sukhatvāt sukhā upapattayaḥ, sukhopapattayaḥ.

Dhyānāntarotpattau tu prītisukhābhāvāt sukhopapattitvaṃ vicāryam.
```

The witness has joined compounds and mechanical spacing irregularities. The
three long compounds in the final explanation of kāmopapatti are divided
readably without claiming a critical reconstruction. In the happiness
passage, punctuation makes explicit the three contrasts carried by the
Bhāṣya: seclusion-born joy and pleasure, samādhi-born joy and pleasure, and
pleasure without joy.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Here in the kāmadhātu, the three modes are to be understood.

There are three modes of sensual-desire rebirth among humans and the
kāma-devas.

How so?

There are beings whose desired objects are already present. They exercise
mastery over objects of desire that are present to them. Such are humans and
certain devas, namely four deva classes.

There are beings whose desired objects are produced by themselves. Having
produced objects of desire, they exercise mastery over them. Such are the
Nirmāṇarati devas.

There are beings whose desired objects are produced by others. They exercise
mastery over objects of desire produced by others. Such are the
Paranirmitavaśavartin devas.

These are called the three modes of sensual-desire rebirth because they
respectively enjoy what is already present, what they produce themselves as
they wish, and what others produce as they wish.

In the rūpadhātu, there are three modes of happy rebirth among the nine
grounds of the three dhyānas. The gods in those grounds dwell happily for a
long time: through joy and pleasure born of seclusion, through joy and
pleasure born of samādhi, and through pleasure without joy.

Because these rebirths are free from suffering and have long-lasting
happiness, they are called happy rebirths.

In the case of birth in the intermediate dhyāna, however, whether it should be
called a happy rebirth requires examination, since joy and pleasure are
absent.

## 4. Natural Movement and Voices

The commentary divides the verse into two classifications with different
bases:

1. In kāmadhātu, it distinguishes three relations to desired objects by
   whether the objects are already present, produced by the enjoyer, or
   produced by others.
2. In rūpadhātu, it identifies three happiness-births among the nine grounds
   of the first three dhyānas.
3. It defines the latter by long-lasting happiness without suffering, then
   marks the intermediate dhyāna as a difficult case because joy and pleasure
   are absent there.

No opponent or named school voice appears in this unit. Vasubandhu's final
`vicāryam`, “requires examination,” is a deliberate qualification, not an
accidental gap in the explanation.

## 5. Three Kāmopapattis

The sensual classification concerns the production and availability of the
object-field:

| Mode | Desired objects | Bhāṣya example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Already present | Humans and four deva classes |
| 2 | Produced by oneself at will | Nirmāṇarati devas |
| 3 | Produced by others at will | Paranirmitavaśavartin devas |

All three groups enjoy desired objects and exercise mastery over them. What
differs is how the objects come to be available. The first is characterized
by enjoyment of what has arisen as present; the second by self-production;
the third by production through another.

The Bhāṣya names humans and “certain devas” in the first mode, then specifies
four deva classes. In sequence, these are the Cāturmahārājakāyikas,
Trāyastriṃśas, Yāmas, and Tuṣitas, as established in the surrounding account.
The other two modes correspond to the Nirmāṇaratis and
Paranirmitavaśavartins.

## 6. Production, Mastery, and Enjoyment

The three modes should not be reduced to a simple ownership scale. The prose
coordinates at least three relations:

```text
who produces the desired object?
who exercises mastery over it?
who enjoys it?
```

In the first mode, the object is already present and the relevant beings
exercise mastery and enjoy it. In the second, the enjoying devas also produce
the object. In the third, another produces it, while the Paranirmitavaśavartin
devas exercise mastery over and enjoy it.

Production and control are therefore distinct. The third mode makes this
plain: the enjoyer need not be the producer. The Bhāṣya's phrase
`vaśe vartayanti` expresses mastery over the relevant objects; it does not
make the three object-provenance relations identical.

## 7. Three Sukhopapattis

The second classification applies to the nine grounds of the first three
dhyānas. The Bhāṣya groups them into three modes according to the pleasant
experience through which beings dwell there for a long time:

| Dhyāna grouping | Pleasant mode named |
|---|---|
| First dhyāna | Joy and pleasure born of seclusion |
| Second dhyāna | Joy and pleasure born of samādhi |
| Third dhyāna | Pleasure without joy |

These are called `sukhopapatti` because the rebirths are without suffering and
the happiness lasts a long time. The definition therefore combines an
affective feature, freedom from suffering, and duration.

The nine grounds are grouped into three kinds; the commentary does not treat
each ground as a separate happiness-birth in this classification.

## 8. The Intermediate Dhyāna Qualification

Vasubandhu does not force the intermediate-dhyāna case into the stated
definition. Since joy and pleasure are absent there, its inclusion as a
`sukhopapatti` requires examination.

```text
stated criterion:
    long-lasting happiness without suffering

intermediate dhyāna:
    joy and pleasure absent

result:
    classification remains to be examined
```

This qualification belongs to the Bhāṣya's reasoning. It prevents the
threefold classification from being taken as an exhaustive, exceptionless
assignment of every meditative ground.

## 9. Organon Study

The two triplets share the word `upapatti`, but the Bhāṣya classifies them by
different Features:

```text
kāmopapatti  → object availability, production, control, enjoyment
sukhopapatti → quality and duration of happiness, absence of suffering
```

In the first, the origin of the object differentiates the experiential mode.
In the second, sustained affective quality groups the dhyāna grounds. The
shared numeral three does not make the two taxonomies equivalent.

For the Organon, this provides a useful example of preserving a class's
classification basis and its unresolved boundary case. This is a
project-level reconstruction, not Vasubandhu's terminology.
