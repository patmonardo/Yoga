# VAK_3.72 Bhāṣya — Station Spacing and Upward Perception

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
sthānāt sthānād adho yāvat tāvad ūrdhvaṃ tatas tataḥ /
nordhvaṃ darśanam asty eṣām anyatrarddhiparāśrayāt // 3.72 //
```

> From each station, the next above is as far upward as Jambūdvīpa lies
> downward from it. These beings do not see upward except through supernormal
> power or reliance upon another.

The Bhāṣya derives the spacing of the higher deva stations from their distance
above Jambūdvīpa and identifies Akaniṣṭha as the terminal station. It then
distinguishes travel, seeing a higher-born visitor, and perceiving a genuinely
higher domain. The last distinction is explained through `aviṣayatva`, the
fact that a higher-level body is outside the object-range of lower-level
faculties. A Nikāyāntarīya account proposes that higher beings appear below in
a constructed body suited to the lower level.

The witness has joined words and small spelling irregularities, including
`yiathā` in the comparison, regularized here to `yathā`. The passage
`aghaṃ kila cittasthaṃ rūpaṃ tanmātraniṣṭheti` is terse; its wording is
preserved rather than expanded into a confident etymology. The phrase

form; its syntax is compressed, but the following question and answer make
the contrast with a constructed lower-bhūmi body clear.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Of the twenty-two stations of the devas that have been described, how far is
each higher one from the one below it?

It is not easy to calculate all of this by enumerating yojanas. Rather:

From each station, the next station above is as far upward as the former lies
below.

Beginning from Jambūdvīpa, for any station above it, the distance from that
station down to Jambūdvīpa is equal to the distance from it upward to the next
station.

For example, the fourth terrace is the principal station of the Four Great
Kings, forty thousand yojanas from here. As far as Jambūdvīpa lies below it,
so far above it is the station of the Thirty-Three. From that station, the
distance down to Jambūdvīpa equals the distance upward to the station of the
Yāmas. In the same way, the station of the Tuṣitas lies above the Yāmas by an
interval equal to the Yāmas' distance down to Jambūdvīpa.

Proceeding through the entire sequence in this way, as far as Jambūdvīpa lies
below Sudarśana, so far above it is the station of the Akaniṣṭhas. Above that
there is no further station. For this reason they are called Akaniṣṭhas,
“the highest,” since they are the most senior.

Others say “Aghaniṣṭhas.” They explain: “Agha is, they say, form situated in
the mind; that alone is its limit.” The wording of this alternative
explanation is terse.

Can beings born in a lower station go to the higher celestial residences and
see them?

These beings have no upward vision except through supernormal power or
reliance upon another. Through supernormal power, the Thirty-Three might go to
the Yāmas. Or they might go through reliance upon another, if led by a deva
with greater power or by a deva who lives there. The remaining cases are
similar.

They may see a higher-born being who has come down, but they do not thereby
see a being of a higher dhātu or higher bhūmi as such—just as one would not
touch a tangible object outside the range of touch, because it is not an
object of that faculty. For this reason, such beings do not come in their own
body. What, then, happens? The Nikāyāntarīyas say that, by its intention, the
higher being comes in a constructed body belonging to a lower bhūmi, so that
one here sees it as if it were native to this world.

## 4. Natural Movement and Voices

The commentary first answers a cosmographic question, then turns to a
perceptual one:

1. The twenty-two deva stations are too numerous for a convenient full
   yojana-by-yojana enumeration, so the Bhāṣya gives a recurrence rule.
2. It illustrates the rule from the Four Great Kings through the Thirty-Three,
   Yāmas, Tuṣitas, and finally Akaniṣṭha.
3. It explains Akaniṣṭha as the highest station and reports an alternative
   `Aghaniṣṭha` explanation.
4. It asks whether lower-station beings can ascend and see the higher
   residences, naming supernormal power and reliance upon another as routes.
5. It distinguishes seeing a higher-born visitor from perceiving a higher
   dhātu or bhūmi as an object of lower faculties.
6. It reports the Nikāyāntarīya account of appearance through a constructed
   lower-bhūmi body.

The alternative name for Akaniṣṭha and the constructed-body proposal are
explicitly attributed to others. They are not merged into Vasubandhu's main
explanation.

## 5. Proportional Spacing of Stations

The recurrence uses Jambūdvīpa as its lower reference. If a station is at
height `H` above Jambūdvīpa, the next station is another `H` above it:

```text
distance(station, Jambūdvīpa)
    = distance(station, next station above)
```

The first example begins with the fourth terrace, the principal station of
the Four Great Kings, at 40,000 yojanas. Applying the rule gives:

| Station | Height above Jambūdvīpa | Interval from prior station |
|---|---:|---:|
| Four Great Kings' principal station | 40,000 | 40,000 from Jambūdvīpa |
| Thirty-Three | 80,000 | 40,000 |
| Yāmas | 160,000 | 80,000 |
| Tuṣitas | 320,000 | 160,000 |

In recurrence form, each successive height doubles:

```text
H₀ = 40,000
Hₙ₊₁ = 2Hₙ
```

The Bhāṣya says to continue this procedure through the stations to Akaniṣṭha,
above which there is no further station. The sequence is ordered and
bounded; the recurrence does not posit an infinite series.

## 6. Akaniṣṭha and the Alternative Name

The primary explanation connects `Akaniṣṭha` with being highest or most
senior: no station lies above it. The Bhāṣya then reports that others say
`Aghaniṣṭha` and supplies a compressed gloss involving `agha`, mind-situated
form, and a limit.

Because the alternative gloss is syntactically terse, the translation keeps
its main words visible and marks the relation among them as uncertain. It
does not replace the principal cosmographic explanation or establish a
separate doctrine about mind and form.

## 7. Access and Perception

The second half does not equate travel with seeing. Lower beings may reach a
higher residence through two routes:

```text
own ṛddhi:
    exceptional capacity enables ascent

parāśraya:
    another, more powerful or resident deva, leads them
```

The example is a Trāyastriṃśa going to the Yāmas. Similar cases apply to the
other lower and higher stations. These exceptions qualify the general
statement that lower-station beings lack upward vision; they do not make
ordinary access universal.

The Bhāṣya then narrows the perceptual claim. A lower being may see a
higher-born individual who has come down. It does not follow that the
individual's native higher dhātu or bhūmi has become an object of the lower
being's faculties.

## 8. Faculty-Object Range and Constructed Appearance

The comparison with touch expresses a categorical limit: a tangible object
outside the range of touch is not touched because it is not an object of that
faculty. Similarly, a genuinely higher-level body is not directly available
to lower-level perception merely because its bearer has arrived below.

The Nikāyāntarīya account explains how appearance can nevertheless occur:

```text
higher being's native body
    → outside lower bhūmi's object-range

higher being's intention
    → constructed body belonging to lower bhūmi
        → seen here as if native to this world
```

This is an attributed explanation of cross-level presentation. It distinguishes
the visitor's origin from the level of the body through which it appears.

## 9. Doctrinal and Organon Study

The passage keeps several relations distinct:

```text
station → vertical position
ṛddhi or assistance → route of travel
faculty-domain → what can be perceived
constructed body → level-appropriate presentation
```

Movement between stations does not automatically change the perceiver's
object-range. Nor does seeing a visitor establish perception of the visitor's
native higher bhūmi. The Nikāyāntarīya proposal introduces a mediating form
that is adequate to the lower field.

For the Organon, this offers a precise distinction among location, access,
perceptual object, and mode of presentation. It is a project-level
reconstruction of the Bhāṣya's reasoning, not its own terminology.
