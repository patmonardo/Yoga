# VAK_3.43 Bhāṣya — The Final Mind and the Locus of Death

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> naikāgrācittayor etau nirvāty avyākṛtadvaye /
> kramacyutau pādanābhihṛdayeṣu manaścyutiḥ // 3.43 //

> These two occur neither in one-pointed consciousness nor in a mindless
> state. The arhat enters final extinction in one of two indeterminate
> [states]; in gradual death, Mind ceases at the feet, navel, or heart.

`Etau`, “these two,” carries forward death and arising from VAK 3.42. The
Bhāṣya supplies the arhat as the subject of `nirvāti`, identifies the two
indeterminate minds, distinguishes sudden from gradual death, and correlates
the three bodily loci with different destinations.
After the account of vital points, it distinguishes fallible advance signs
from five signs said to accompany a deva's death unfailingly.

The arhat as supplied subject, the two indeterminate minds, the distinction
between sudden and gradual death, and the destination–locus mapping are Bhāṣya
determinations.

## 3. Continuous Translation
Even there—that is, even in mental differentiated cognition—these two do not
occur in concentrated consciousness or in a mindless state. “These two” means
death and arising. A person does not die or arise in rebirth while the mind is
concentrated, because such a mind belongs to a dissimilar level, is
deliberately produced, and is supportive. Nor do these events occur in a
mindless state. One could not set upon a mindless person in that state. When
that person's support begins to undergo fatal alteration, a mind bound to
that support must necessarily become manifest and then cease; it cannot be
otherwise. Mindlessness at rebirth is also inadmissible: there is then no
cause for an interruption of mind, and rebirth does not occur without
affliction.

It has been said that the existence at death is threefold. An arhat, however,
becomes extinguished in one of two indeterminate minds: either a mind
associated with bodily deportment or a maturation-result mind. If a
maturation-result equanimity exists in the desire realm, final extinction may
occur in it; if it does not, extinction occurs only in a deportment-associated
mind. Why does it occur only in an indeterminate mind and not in another?
Because an indeterminate mind, being weak, is favorable to the cutting off of
the mental series.

Now, in what region of the body does differentiated cognition cease in one
who is dying? In sudden death, the bodily faculty together with mind
disappears abruptly. But if one dies gradually, the cessation of mind occurs
at the feet, navel, or heart—for those going respectively downward, to human
birth, and to the devas.

Those who go downward are those bound for a lower destiny. Those who go to
humans are bound for human birth, and those who go to the devas are bound for
deva birth. For these three, in the stated order, differentiated cognition
ceases at the feet, at the navel, and at the heart. Arhats are “non-goers to
birth,” because they are not born again. In their case too, differentiated
cognition ceases at the heart. Others say that it ceases at the crown of the
head.

This occurs because the bodily faculty ceases in those places. In one who is
dying, the bodily faculty contracts and disappears from the feet and the
other regions, like water cast upon a heated stone. In this way death takes
place gradually.

Among living beings, gradual death commonly occurs when they are struck down
by the impairment of vital points and by painful feeling. Certain regions of
the body bring about death when they are damaged; these are therefore called
vital points. They appear to be cut when one of the water, fire, or wind
Principles becomes excessively predominant, bringing intense pains like the
impact of a sharp weapon. They are not literally cut as wood is cut. They are
called “cut” because afterward they no longer function, as though cut.

Why is the Earth Principle not included? Because there is no fourth bodily
disorder: the disorders are the three—wind, bile, and phlegm—and, as
appropriate, these are principally associated with the wind, fire, and water
domains. Others explain the matter by analogy with the forces that dissolve
the container-world. Devas do not suffer impairment of vital points.


A deva does not undergo severing of vital points. Instead, five preliminary
signs appear for a deva about to pass away: a pleasant sound comes from clothes
and ornaments; bodily radiance dims; drops of water remain on the body after
bathing; even an ordinarily restless mind settles on a single object; and the
eyes begin to blink. These signs can occur without death.

Five further signs do not fail to accompany death: clothes become soiled, a
garland withers, sweat comes from the armpits, bodily odor develops, and the
deva takes no pleasure in the usual seat. The Bhāṣya thus distinguishes
fallible advance signs from signs said to be unfailing. These are claims of
the transmitted account, not independent observations.

## 4. Argument and Voices

The unit unfolds through five questions:

```text
Can death and arising occur in concentration or mindlessness?
    → neither is an admissible terminal condition

In what mind does an arhat become finally extinguished?
    → in one of two indeterminate minds

Where does cognition cease in the dying body?
    → suddenly everywhere, or gradually at a destination-specific locus

Why are these loci relevant?
    → the bodily faculty progressively contracts and ceases there

What commonly occasions gradual death?
    → painful impairment of vital points
```

Vasubandhu supplies the principal explanations throughout. Two explicitly
marked alternative opinions must remain visible:

- `mūrdhnīty apare`: some place the arhat's final cessation at the crown,
  rather than the heart;
- `bhājanalokasaṃvartanīsādharmyeṇety apare`: some explain the Elemental
  pattern of vital-point injury by analogy with the dissolution of the
  container-world.

The line `adhonṛsuragājānām` continues the explanation of the verse. It is
necessary for resolving the destination-to-locus mapping even though it is
not part of the numbered VAK 3.43 stanza as transmitted here.

## 5. Neither Concentrated nor Mindless

The first prohibition completes the determination begun in 3.42:

```text
death and arising
    occur in mental differentiated cognition
    but not in every condition of mental cognition
```

Concentrated mind is excluded for three reasons:

| Bhāṣya term | Force in the argument |
|---|---|
| `visabhāgabhūmika` | it belongs to a level dissimilar to that of the transition |
| `abhisaṃskārika` | it is intentionally produced or constructed |
| `anugrāhaka` | it supports and sustains rather than terminates the series |

The source's compact wording does not fully specify the referent of
`visabhāga`, so “dissimilar level” should remain provisional rather than be
expanded into an unargued cosmological claim.

Mindlessness is excluded differently. When bodily support begins its fatal
alteration, support-bound mind must first manifest and only then cease:

```text
fatal alteration of support
    → manifestation of support-bound mind
        → cessation of that mind
```

Thus the Bhāṣya does not allow the absence of manifest mind to pass directly
into death. At rebirth, continuous mindlessness is also excluded because
there is no cause there for a break in mind, while afflicted continuity is a
condition of renewed existence.

## 6. The Arhat's Final Indeterminate Mind

The two possible final minds are:

```text
airyāpathika-citta
    consciousness associated with bodily deportment

vipāka-citta
    maturation-result consciousness
```

Both are `avyākṛta`, ethically indeterminate. The qualification concerning
maturation-result equanimity in the desire realm determines whether the
second alternative is available; if it is unavailable, the
deportment-associated mind alone remains.

The stated reason is operational rather than evaluative. An indeterminate
mind is `durbala`, weak, and for that reason `cittacchedānukūla`, suitable for
the cutting off of the mental series. The Bhāṣya is not saying that
indeterminate minds are generally superior. It is specifying the kind of
cognitive event compatible with terminal cessation.

Nor should the arhat's `nirvāṇa` be assimilated to ordinary `cyuti`. The arhat
has a final cognitive event, but no ensuing rebirth-linking. This is why the
commentary can classify the arhat as `aja`, one who does not proceed to
another birth.

## 7. Sudden and Gradual Death

The Bhāṣya distinguishes two modes of bodily termination:

```text
sakṛnmaraṇa — sudden death
    bodily faculty and mind disappear abruptly

kramacyuti — gradual death
    bodily faculty contracts progressively
    final cognition ceases at a determinate bodily locus
```

For gradual death, the mapping is:

| Destination | Class | Locus of cessation |
|---|---|---|
| lower destiny | `adhoga` | feet |
| human birth | `nṛga` | navel |
| deva birth | `suraga` | heart |
| no further birth | `aja`, the arhat | heart; crown according to others |

Feet, navel, and heart are therefore not successive stations traversed by
every dying consciousness. They are alternative final loci correlated with
different destinations. The text does not present a universal ascent through
subtle bodily centers.

The comparison with water on a hot stone describes contraction of the bodily
faculty, not the travel of an enduring self. What withdraws is `kāyendriya`,
and the terminal event is the cessation of differentiated cognition.

## 8. Vital Points and Elemental Disorder

A `marman` is defined functionally: it is a bodily region whose severe injury
brings about death. Accordingly, `marmaccheda` is not necessarily literal
cutting. The commentary expressly denies that vital points are severed like
wood. “Cutting” names such a loss of function that they behave as though
severed.

The principal explanation coordinates three disorders and three predominant
domains:

```text
wind disorder     → wind Principle
bile disorder     → fire Principle
phlegm disorder   → water Principle
```

Because this medical scheme recognizes three disorders and no fourth, earth
does not provide a fourth corresponding agent. The alternative explanation
appeals instead to resemblance with the dissolution of the container-world.
The two explanations should not be silently combined: the first is
physiological, the second cosmological by analogy.

The final restriction—devas do not undergo `marmaccheda`—applies to this
specific mode of painful vital-point impairment. It does not overturn the
earlier statement that the final cessation of one destined for deva birth is
at the heart.

## 9. Interpretation

VAK 3.43 analyzes death as a constrained reconfiguration, not as an
unqualified departure of “mind from body.” At least four axes remain
distinct:

```text
cognitive condition
    concentrated, mindless, or manifest

ethical type of the final mind
    indeterminate in the arhat's case

mode of bodily cessation
    sudden or gradual

destination and terminal locus
    lower / human / deva / no rebirth
    correlated with feet / navel / heart / disputed crown
```

This separation is valuable for the Organon Dataset. A single label such as
“death” cannot carry the whole determination. Transition type, cognitive
state, bodily process, destination, and doctrinal status are separate
dimensions whose admissible combinations are fixed by relations among them.
That is an Organon reconstruction of the source's structured distinctions,
not terminology attributed to Vasubandhu.

The unit also sharpens the meaning of projection or Outflow. Rebirth is not
licensed by mere appearance, nor is death the disappearance of an isolated
substance. Each transition requires a particular configuration of support,
manifest cognition, affliction, and destination. The source describes the
conditioned series; it does not posit a projector standing outside that
series.

Most importantly, the apparent bodily sequence must not counterfeit the
actual logical form. The three loci look syntactically like a series, but the
Bhāṣya determines them semantically as a mapping across destination classes.
Preserving that difference is essential both to the translation and to any
later Ocular representation.

## 10. Review Status

This Study carries VAK 3.43 and its post-verse explanation from 156.03
through 157.11. The prior unit at 156.03–157.06 covers: the
exclusion of concentrated and mindless states, the arhat's two possible final
minds, sudden and gradual death, destination-specific loci, the disputed
arhat locus, contraction of the bodily faculty, and the account of
vital-point impairment.

The Sanskrit and translation remain provisional. In particular, the damaged
sharp-weapon comparison, the precise force of `upakramitum`, the referent of
`visabhāgabhūmika`, and the relation between Desire Realm maturation and
equanimity require comparison with critical Sanskrit, Tibetan, Chinese, and
established translations. The medical correspondences should likewise be
read as the Bhāṣya's doctrinal physiology rather than modern anatomy.

The continuation preserves both groups of five deva death signs and their
different reliability. VAK 3.44 begins with the three-group transition.
