# VAK_2.55 Bhāṣya — Times and Disconnection

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> sarvatragaḥ sabhāgaś ca dvayadhvagau tryadhvagās trayaḥ /
>
> saṃskṛtaṃ savisaṃyogaṃ phalaṃ nāsaṃskṛtasya te // 2.55 //

> The pervasive and homogeneous causes occur in past and present; the
> co-arisen, associated, and maturation causes occur in all three times.
> Conditioned dharmas and disconnection count as fruits; an unconditioned
> dharma has neither cause nor fruit.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

What is the temporal range of these causes? It was explained in
meaning but not yet formulated in a verse. The pervasive and
homogeneous causes belong to two times, past and present, not future;
the reason has already been given. Three causes belong to all three
times: co-arisen, associated, and maturation. Since no fixed time
was stated for the conditioning cause, it must be understood as
occurring in all times or as dissociated from time. The causes have
been explained.

What is the fruit for which these are causes? Conditioned dharmas
and disconnection are fruits. Which dharmas count as fruits? A
treatise says conditioned dharmas and cessation through
discrimination. If the unconditioned is a fruit, must it not have a
cause whose fruit it is? And if it is a cause, must it not have a
fruit? Cause and fruit belong to a conditioned dharma alone: the
unconditioned has neither of these. It cannot have any of the six
causes or five fruits [as its own causal relations]. Why is the Path
not accepted as a conditioning cause of disconnection? That cause
was defined by non-obstruction of production, whereas the
unconditioned is not produced.

Whose fruit, then, is disconnection, and how is it the Path's fruit?
It is obtained through the Path's power. Would that make only its
acquisition the Path's fruit? The Path is effective in that
acquisition, not in producing disconnection. Its powers differ:
it produces acquisition but brings one to disconnection. Thus the
Path is in no way a productive cause of disconnection, though
disconnection is its fruit. If the unconditioned has no dominant
fruit, how can it be a conditioning cause? By not obstructing
production. It has no fruit of its own, however, since it is
outside time and cannot take or give a fruit.

One speaker says the Blessed One nowhere calls the unconditioned a
cause. The Sautrāntikas answer that he does so indirectly. A sūtra
says the causes and Conditions for the production of Form are
impermanent; Form arising dependent on impermanent causes and
Conditions cannot be permanent. The same is said through Cognition.
Would this preclude an unconditioned Support as a Condition for
Cognition? The restriction “for production” matters: the sūtra
does not say every Condition of Cognition is impermanent. But, the
reply presses, the same restriction then fails to disprove the
unconditioned as a non-obstructing conditioning cause. The sūtra
mentions Support as a Condition, not the non-obstructing cause; it
does not establish the latter. Nor does it forbid it. Many sūtras
have disappeared, so absence of an extant statement cannot settle
what was never said.

What is disconnection? Calling it “cessation through discrimination”
only repeats the earlier definition, which had defined that
cessation as disconnection. Such a circular explanation does not
show its nature. One answer says its nature is directly known only
by the noble ones: at most one may say that an eternal, wholesome,
distinct entity exists and is called both disconnection and cessation
through discrimination. The Sautrāntikas reply that all
unconditioned things lack distinct substance. Space is the mere
absence of a tangible obstruction, as one says “space” when nothing
blocks passage in the dark. Cessation through discrimination is the
non-arising of further underlying tendencies after the destruction
of arisen ones through discrimination. Non-arising through absence
of Conditions without discrimination is cessation without
discrimination, as when a remainder of shared-class life cannot
arise after death in the interval.

Others, identified as another school, locate discrimination's force
in the cessation of underlying tendencies and take the later
non-arising of suffering, due simply to the absence of its causes,
as cessation without discrimination. An objection replies that this
non-arising too depends on discrimination and so belongs to
cessation through discrimination. Another view calls the later
absence of something already arisen cessation without
discrimination by natural cessation. On that account it would be
impermanent, since the absence does not obtain before the thing
has perished.

Would cessation through discrimination likewise become impermanent
because it follows discrimination? No: its non-arising is not first
created by a prior act of discrimination. It already holds that
those dharmas do not arise; what discrimination does is prevent the
arising of dharmas that otherwise would arise. This is its efficacy.
But if nirvāṇa is merely non-arising, how can a sūtra say that five
Faculties cultivated and practiced conduce to abandoning suffering
of the past, future, and present? Abandonment is nirvāṇa, and only
future suffering can fail to arise. The reply accepts the question's
point about time. The Blessed One speaks of abandoning suffering
through abandoning craving for its object: abandon desire and
attachment to Form and that Form is said to be abandoned and fully
known; the same extends through Cognition. This accounts for
abandonment of suffering in all three times.

If the sūtra instead speaks of afflictions in all three times, the
same explanation applies. Or its past and present afflictions may
refer to former and current lives. Those afflictions deposited a
seed in the continuum for future arising; when that seed is
abandoned, those afflictions too are called abandoned, as action
is called exhausted when its maturation is exhausted. Future
suffering or affliction is abandoned by its final non-arising
through absence of a seed. Otherwise, what could be abandoned in
a past thing already ceased or in a present thing about to cease?
Effort directed at their cessation would have no point.

If the unconditioned has no distinct entity, how can the Blessed
One call detachment foremost among conditioned and unconditioned
dharmas? The reply does not deny the unconditioned altogether; it
understands it as the absence just described. One says a sound's
prior and later absence exist without turning absence into a
positive entity. The complete absence of distress may rightly be
praised as foremost to attract those to be taught. Would mere
absence cease to be the noble truth of cessation? Truth means what
is seen without distortion by noble ones: suffering is suffering
and its absence is absence. The objection asks how an absence can
be the third noble truth. The reply refers to its being seen and
set out after the second [the wording is damaged].

If the unconditioned is absence alone, would Cognition with space
or nirvāṇa as its Support have a nonexistent Support? This is
reserved for the later discussion of past and future existence.
What if the unconditioned is accepted as an entity instead? It
would preserve the Vaibhāṣika position, the critic replies, but
would posit what is not established: it has no apprehensible own
nature like Form or Feeling, nor an activity like the eye Faculty.
Nor is the genitive “cessation of this thing” easily explained by
a distinct entity with no causal or other relation to that thing;
“absence of this” is a straightforward negation. The defender
answers that cessation is conventionally called the cessation of
the particular affliction whose acquisition is cut off. The critic
asks what fixes that acquisition to just this cessation.

A sūtra calls a monk one who has attained nirvāṇa in this life.
How can an absence be attained? The answer is that, on gaining the
antidote, the monk gains a Basis permanently incompatible with the
renewed arising of affliction and rebirth; hence nirvāṇa is said
to be attained. Another scriptural description speaks of exhausting
and relinquishing present suffering and of future suffering's
non-arising, and calls this relinquishment, craving's destruction,
detachment, cessation, and nirvāṇa. A defender proposes that
“non-arising” means suffering does not arise *in* nirvāṇa. The
critic rejects this locative: if it means “while nirvāṇa exists,”
non-arising would always hold because nirvāṇa is eternal; if it
means “when nirvāṇa is attained,” the proposed attainment already
presupposes the non-arising it was meant to explain.

The critic compares liberation of the Blessed One's mind to the
extinguishing of a lamp: the lamp's extinction is absence. A cited
Abhidharma passage calls unconditioned dharmas “without a thing.”
The critic takes this to mean without body or own nature. The
defender disputes that meaning: *vastu* has five uses—own nature,
Support, association, cause, and possession—each illustrated by a
cited expression. Here “thing” means causal basis. Thus, say the
Vaibhāṣikas, the unconditioned exists as a distinct entity even
though it has neither cause nor fruit. This dispute is set aside;
the next question asks which fruit belongs to each cause.

## 4. Reading Control

A fruit need not be a product: the Path produces acquisition and
brings one to disconnection without producing disconnection. The
phrase *nāsaṃskṛtasya te* denies cause and fruit as relations
belonging to an unconditioned dharma, while *savisaṃyogaṃ phalam*
includes disconnection among fruits. The Sautrāntika absence account,
other proposals, and the Vaibhāṣika entity account remain distinct
attributed positions. The final fivefold use of *vastu* is part of
the Vaibhāṣika reply, not a shared definition established by the
earlier critic.
