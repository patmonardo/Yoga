# VAK_2.16 Bhāṣya — Faculties Through Which the Fruits Are Attained

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> kramamṛtyau tu catvāri śubhe sarvatra pañca ca /
> navāptir antyaphalayoḥ saptāṣṭanavabhir dvayoḥ // 2.16 //

> In gradual death, four; with a wholesome mind, everywhere five
> in addition. The two fruits at the extremes are attained through
> nine faculties; the two middle fruits through seven, eight, or nine.

## 3. Scientific English Rendering

In the section on faculties, all matters concerning faculties are
examined. Now, which fruit of the ascetic path is attained through
how many faculties? “Attainment of the two extreme fruits is through
nine”: the two extreme fruits are attained through nine faculties.
Which are the extremes? The fruit of stream-entry and arhatship.
Which are the middle ones? The fruits of once-returning and non-returning.

Stream-entry is attained through nine: those beginning with confidence,
excluding the faculty of one who has known, together with mind and
neutral feeling. The faculty “I shall know” is to be understood
on the uninterrupted path, and the knowledge-faculty on the path
of liberation. For attainment is through both, since they respectively
bring about and provide the supporting basis for the acquisition
of disconnection. Arhatship, in turn, is attained through nine:
those beginning with confidence, excluding the faculty “I shall know,”
together with mind and any one of pleasure, gladness, and neutral
feeling.

“The two through seven, eight, or nine”: each of the fruits of
once-returning and non-returning is attained through seven, eight,
or nine faculties. How? If one proceeding in sequence attains
once-returning through a mundane path, attainment is through seven:
the five beginning with confidence, neutral feeling, and mind. If through
a supramundane path, attainment is through eight, with the
knowledge-faculty as the eighth. If one already more extensively
free from desire attains it, attainment is through nine, in the
same manner as stream-entry.

If one proceeding in sequence attains non-returning through a
mundane path, attainment is through seven, as for once-returning.
If through a supramundane path, it is through eight in the same
way. If one already free from desire attains it, attainment is
through nine, as for stream-entry. There is this difference:
any one of pleasure, gladness, and neutral feeling occurs, according
to the difference in the supporting basis.

Even when one proceeding in sequence enters dhyāna on the ninth
path of liberation, because of sharp faculties, by a mundane path,
one attains non-returning through eight faculties. For there, on
the ninth path of liberation, gladness is the eighth, whereas on
the uninterrupted path neutral feeling alone is invariably present;
and attainment is through both. If one enters through a supramundane
path, attainment is through nine, with the knowledge-faculty as
the ninth.

But the Abhidharma says: “Through how many faculties does one attain
arhatship?” and answers, “Through eleven.” How, then, is it said
to be through nine? One attains it through nine alone. “Arhatship
is nevertheless said to be attained through eleven because that
is possible for one person.” It is possible that one person,
having fallen away repeatedly, should attain arhatship with pleasure,
with gladness, and with neutral feeling. Therefore eleven are
stated. It is not possible, however, for pleasure and the other
feelings to occur at one and the same time.

Why does the same consequence not apply to a non-returner? Having
fallen away, that person never attains the fruit with the
pleasure-faculty; and one who had previously become free from
desire does not fall away, because that dispassion has been attained
through both paths.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The commentary first identifies the extreme and middle fruits,
then explains each faculty count through its route of attainment.
The special non-returning case qualifies a simple equation of
“mundane” with seven and “supramundane” with eight.

An objection then cites the Abhidharma's eleven-faculty arhatship
count. The reply distinguishes one attainment from the possibilities
across repeated attainments by one person. A final question tests
whether the same reasoning applies to non-returning. No school
is explicitly named in this local exchange; the doctrine of falling
away is presented as part of the reported argument.

## 5. The Two Extreme Fruits

“Extreme” means first and last in the four-fruit sequence: stream-entry
and arhatship. It does not mean the last two fruits. For stream-entry,
the nine are the five beginning with confidence, mind, neutral feeling,
and the first two realization faculties. For arhatship, they are
the five, mind, one appropriate feeling, and the last two realization
faculties.

The stream-entry explanation expressly distributes its two realization
faculties between the uninterrupted path and the path of liberation.
Their roles differ: bringing about the acquisition of disconnection
and providing its supporting basis. The count is therefore not an
inventory of nine independent factors asserted to coexist in one
moment. It includes different path-designations across the attainment
process, as well as constituent faculty-types.

The arhatship account replaces the first realization faculty with
the last and allows one of three feelings. The text says “any one,”
not all three together. This prepares the later objection about eleven.

## 6. The Middle Fruits and the Additional Case

For one proceeding sequentially, the basic mundane count is seven:
confidence, vigor, recollection, concentration, prajñā, mind, and neutral
feeling. The supramundane case adds the knowledge-faculty. Prior
dispassion permits the nine-faculty pattern compared with stream-entry;
the non-returning account additionally varies the feeling according
to the supporting basis.

The source then adds a case absent from the research 2.16 excerpt.
A sharp-facultied sequential practitioner enters dhyāna on the ninth
path of liberation. Neutral feeling belongs to the uninterrupted
path, and gladness to the liberation path. Because both path-moments
contribute to attainment, both feeling-types enter the count.
The mundane count is consequently eight, and the supramundane count
nine with the knowledge-faculty added.

Thus the same total can arise for different reasons. Eight may
include the knowledge-faculty in one case, or an additional feeling
across the path-moments in another. The number alone does not identify
the composition or route.

## 7. Nine and Eleven for Arhatship

Nine applies to an attainment using one of the three permissible
feelings. Eleven counts what may occur across repeated attainments
by one person: the eight other faculty-types, together with pleasure,
gladness, and neutral feeling at different attainments.

The reply explicitly rules out simultaneous occurrence of those
three feelings. It therefore distinguishes aggregation across a
person's history from a single attainment count. Even the latter,
as the earlier path analysis shows, need not mean simultaneous
presence of every counted faculty-designation.

The final non-returner reply states two restrictions: a fallen
non-returner does not regain the fruit with pleasure, and one who
was already free from desire does not fall away because that
dispassion has been attained through both paths. These claims belong
to the local doctrinal argument. The paragraph does not demonstrate
a general theory of relapse or every case of attainment security.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for Translation

*Āpti* and *prāpti* are “attainment” in the fruit question;
*visaṃyogaprāpti* is “acquisition of disconnection.” The instrumentals
name the faculties through which attainment occurs. They do not
identify the fruit itself with the counted set.

*Ānantaryamārga* is “uninterrupted path,” and *vimuktimārga* “path
of liberation.” Their distinction must remain visible wherever a
count includes the contributions of both. *Niśrayaviśeṣa* is
“difference in the supporting basis”; the present unit does not
enumerate those bases, so none are supplied in the translation.

*Bhūyovītarāga* and *vītarāga* distinguish prior degrees of dispassion
in the two middle-fruit accounts. “More extensively free from desire”
preserves the former without adding a numerical degree of abandonment
absent from this passage.

“Through nine alone” in the arhatship reply restricts an attainment
count; it is not a denial of the same person's possible elevenfold
range across attainments. The word *ekasya*, of one person, matters
as much as the numerical contrast.

## 9. Interpretation

The commentary distinguishes the attained result from the route and
Faculty-configurations through which it is attained. It also
distinguishes a single attainment count from possibilities aggregated
across one person's repeated attainments. The eleven-Faculty
arhatship statement includes different feeling Faculties across
attainments; it does not assert that pleasure, gladness, and neutral
feeling occur simultaneously. In the path analysis, counted Faculties
may also be assigned to distinct path-moments. The count therefore
must not be treated as an inventory of simultaneous factors.

The return to Dhātu 1.48 is a project crosswalk: it relates the
Faculty-classification here to the Base–Essence–Principle architecture
without replacing the Dhātu map. Cessation at death, path-mediated
attainment, and abandonment remain distinct predicates.

The Kośa's governing synthesis: Vijñāna is Discriminative Cognition joining
and governing Perception and Conception. Their unity is Inconceivable as a
homogeneous operation; its Idea is disclosed in the Cognition Base. Vijñāna
governs Mind and guides the reading of Dharma Base. *Avijñapti* is the
bridge, one Dharma classified in both Form Base and Dharma Base; Vijñāna
bears a *prati* relation to it.

In a Kant-informed Techne reading, the result, path-moment, and
Faculty-relations are kept analytically distinct, with the source's
conditions governing their application. This is a project-level
interpretation, not a universal theory of practical planning.
