# VAK_2.47 Bhāṣya — Designation, Sentence, and Phonetic Form

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> nāmakāyādayaḥ saṃjñāvākyākṣarasamuktayaḥ /
> kāmarūpāptasattvākhyā niḥṣyandāvyākṛtās tathā // 2.47 //

> Groups of names and the rest are collections of designations,
> sentences, and phonetic elements. They belong to the desire and
> form realms, are associated with sentient beings, are homogeneous
> outflows, and are indeterminate; likewise [commonality, continued
> in the next verse].

The continuous unit follows the completed discussion of conditioned
marks and covers the linguistic groups at 80.12–82.06. The final
“likewise” connects to the next verse; its prose is reserved for VAK 2.48.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

What are the groups of names and the rest?

“Groups of names and the rest are collections of designations, sentences,
and phonetic elements.”

“And the rest” includes groups of sentences and phonetic elements. A name
is the making of a designation, as in “form,” “sound,” and so forth. A
sentence is a statement extending as far as the completion of a meaning,
as in “Impermanent indeed are conditioned formations,” and the like;
through it particular relations of action, quality, time, and so forth
are understood. A phonetic element is an *akṣara*, such as *a*, *ā*, and
so forth.

But are not *akṣaras* names for the components of writing? No: phonetic
elements were not devised to make the components of writing understood.
Rather, the components of writing were devised to make phonetic elements
understood, with the intention, “How might these be understood through
writing when they are not heard?” Therefore phonetic elements are not
names for those written components.

The collections of these designations and the rest are the groups of names
and the other groups. The root *uc* is taught in the sense of coming
together; *samukti* is a form derived from it. What “aggregation” means,
“collection” means here. A name-group is, for example, “form, sound, smell,
taste, and tangible.” A sentence-group is, for example, “All conditioned
formations are impermanent; all dharmas are without self; nirvāṇa is
peaceful.” A group of phonetic elements is, for example, “ka, kha, ga, gha,
ña,” and so forth [the last element is the transmitted reading].

But since these have speech as their nature and thus consist of sound,
they would have material form as their nature. Why are they called
formations dissociated from mind? They do not have speech as their nature.
For speech is vocal sound, and meanings are not understood from mere
vocal sound. How, then? Speech operates upon a name, and the name reveals
a meaning.

[The critique replies:] Speech is precisely not mere vocal sound. That
vocal sound through which a meaning is understood is speech. Through
what vocal sound is a meaning understood? Through sound whose application
to meanings has been fixed by speakers. Thus the word *go* has a fixed
application to nine meanings:

> The discerning person should recognize the word *go* in nine meanings:
> speech, direction, earth, ray, thunderbolt, cattle, eye, heaven, and water.

Even one who thinks that a name reveals a meaning must necessarily admit
this, if it is to have an understood meaning. But this is accomplished by
sound itself whose meaning is understood. What purpose is served by
positing another name [as a distinct entity]?

Nor is it understood how speech operates upon a name. Does it produce it,
or does it manifest it? If it produces it, then, since speech has vocal
sound as its nature, any mere vocal sound will produce a name. Or whatever
specific vocal sound is accepted as producing the name will itself reveal
the meaning. If speech manifests the name, then, since speech has vocal
sound as its nature, any mere vocal sound will manifest the name. Or
whatever specific vocal sound is accepted as manifesting the name will
itself reveal the meaning.

Moreover, sounds do not have a totality in which they meet for a single
moment. Nor is the production of one entity part by part reasonable. How,
then, could speech produce a name? [The defender asks:] How, in that case,
does the final moment of manifest action produce unmanifest action in
dependence upon what has passed? [The critique replies:] On that account,
since the name is produced only at the final [sound], even someone who
hears that one alone should understand the meaning.

Suppose instead that speech produces a phonetic element, and the phonetic
element produces a name. Here too the same consequence follows, because
the phonetic elements do not form a simultaneous totality. The same
consequence follows if speech manifests a name. Even people of exceptional
discernment in speech, with their minds attentive, are unable to delimit
the phonetic elements by their characteristics. Thus speech cannot
reasonably be either the producer or the manifester of those elements
[the transmitted sentence is damaged].

Suppose instead that a name is held to arise together with its referent,
like arising and the other conditioned marks. In that case there could
be no present name of a past or future referent. Parents assign names to
children as they wish: which name would arise together with the child?
And with what would a name of an unconditioned dharma arise together?
This proposal is therefore untenable.

As for the Blessed One's statement, “A verse rests upon a name; the poet
is the support of verses”: here a name is sound whose application to
meanings has been fixed. A verse is a particular arrangement of names,
and in this way rests upon names. But a particular arrangement cannot
reasonably be a distinct substance, just as a row or an ordered succession
of minds is not [a distinct substance]. Or grant the supposition that a
phonetic element itself is a distinct substance: the groups of names and
the rest will simply be collections of those elements, so positing those
[as further entities] serves no purpose.

The Vaibhāṣikas nevertheless say: “Groups of names do exist substantially
as formations dissociated from mind. For not all dharmas are accessible
to reasoning.”

It must now be stated to which realms these groups of names and the rest
belong; whether they are associated with sentient beings or not; whether
they arise from maturation, from increase, or as homogeneous outflows;
and whether they are wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate. The answer is:

“They belong to the desire and form realms, are associated with sentient
beings, are homogeneous outflows, and are indeterminate.”

They belong to the desire realm and the form realm. Others say that there
are also such entities belonging to the formless realm, but that these
are inexpressible. They are associated with sentient beings. The one who
signifies is endowed with them, not the one who is signified. They are
homogeneous outflows and unobscured-indeterminate.

## 4. The Three Linguistic Determinations

The definitions distinguish functions, not merely three sizes of written
expression. *Nāma* makes a designation. *Pada* is explicitly explained as
*vākya*, a sentence or statement whose extent is measured by completion
of meaning. *Vyañjana* is explained as *akṣara*, exemplified by audible
articulations such as *a* and *ā*. “Word” for *pada* would obscure the
present definition; “written letter” for *akṣara* would reverse the
account's explanatory direction.

The sentence makes specific relations of action, quality, and time
understood. This is more determinate than collecting labels. Conversely,
the definition does not supply a complete theory of syntax or an inventory
of all possible sentence relations. *Saṃjñā* here means designation;
its use does not automatically identify this linguistic function with
every determination of the perception aggregate elsewhere.

*Kāya* means a group, and *samukti* is explained as collection or coming
together. The initial definition names these collections without itself
settling the subsequent dispute over their substantial status.

## 5. Sound, Convention, and the Alleged Name-Entity

The first defense distinguishes vocal sound from the name that reveals
meaning. Its premise is sound: mere noise does not disclose a determinate
meaning. The critique challenges the inference that an additional entity
must therefore intervene. Sound with a conventionally fixed application
already differs from undetermined noise.

The nine meanings of *go* make the role of established application
explicit. They are a cited lexical list, not nine genera, nine cognitive
faculties, or a proof that each occurrence means all nine things at once.
The passage does not derive reference simply from an acoustic resemblance
between a sound and its referent.

The production/manifestation dilemma asks what speech contributes to the
alleged entity. If sound as such suffices, any noise should suffice. If
only a determinate kind of sound suffices, the critic asks why that sound
cannot itself disclose the meaning. This is an argument about the need
for an additional explanatory entity; it does not erase the distinction
between sound, convention, and understood meaning.

## 6. Succession, Reference, and Arrangement

The temporal argument concerns the absence of a single moment at which
all the successive sounds are assembled. The defender invokes the final
moment of manifest action producing unmanifest action in dependence upon
preceding moments. The critic presses the resulting difficulty: if the
completed name is produced at the last sound, why does hearing that sound
alone not suffice? Inserting a phonetic entity between speech and name
moves the same problem to another stage.

The argument against co-arising is different. A present expression can
refer to what is past or future; parents can assign different names;
and unconditioned dharmas do not arise together with names. Reference
therefore cannot be explained generally by simultaneous origination of
name and referent. These objections continue the discussion of conditioned
marks without assuming that the ontology of those marks has been accepted
by all participants.

The verse quotation then occasions a distinction between an arrangement
and an additional substance. A row and a succession of minds illustrate
order without supplying a further entity over and above what is ordered.
The critique even grants distinct phonetic entities for argument's sake:
that concession would still not establish further substantial entities
for their collections.

## 7. Doctrinal Voices and Classification

The dialogue's short objections and replies are not all labeled by school
in the Sanskrit. The English marks the functional voices where needed;
it does not assign every line to a named historical interlocutor. The
closing defense is explicitly attributed to the Vaibhāṣikas. Their appeal
to the limits of reasoning follows the critique and must remain visible.
The subsequent classifications report the entities under discussion;
they do not announce that the preceding objections have been withdrawn.

Four axes remain distinct: realm affiliation, association with a sentient
being, causal-result classification, and ethical status. The principal
account places the linguistic groups in the desire and form realms.
The formless-realm alternative is attributed only to “others.” Its
qualification, “inexpressible,” should not be silently converted into
an established account of silent thought or a universal mental language.

Possession belongs to the signifier, not the signified. A person referred
to by a name does not thereby possess the speaker's linguistic formation.
This is a determination of *samanvāgama*, not merely a distinction between
grammatical subject and object. The groups are classified as homogeneous
outflows and unobscured-indeterminate. That classification does not make
every intention or act of speaking ethically neutral.

## 8. Textual and Translation Decisions

The Sanskrit below the anchor preserves the running transcription at
80.12–82.06, including damaged spellings; only source labels and surrounding
spacing have been removed. The anchor follows the existing Organon kārikā.
Its final *tathā*, “likewise,” looks forward: the prose beginning at
82.06–07 carries these classifications into the commonality discussion
of VAK 2.48. That connecting prose and its predicate are reserved together
for the next study, rather than ending this translation with an unfinished
sentence.

The witness gives *ka kha ga gha ñe-* in the phonetic series. The expected
velar-series member would be *ṅa*, as printed in the research translation;
this study retains *ña* in the continuous translation and flags it rather
than silently correcting the witness. Spellings such as *akṣārāṇi*,
*ghoṣāmātra*, *sāmargyam*, and *parakāśaka* remain in the Sanskrit.

Several sentences require more than mechanical spelling normalization.
At 81.04–06, *pratīpadārthakaṃ*, *taccaita cchabdamātrādeva*, and
*kimartharaṃ* occur in a damaged sequence. The English follows the
contextual contrast between understood meaningful sound and the positing
of an additional name; it is not a critically established reconstruction
of every word. At 81.13–14, *paścima eva de* is rendered “at the final
[sound]” from the argument, with the supplied noun marked.

At 81.16–18, *vyañjanāṃ pi vāgviśiṣṭaprajñā* and *vyañjayāpi* leave the
precise wording of the discernment argument uncertain. The provisional
translation retains its local conclusion about production and
manifestation without claiming a general impossibility of phonetic
analysis. The closing *viprayuktasaṃskārabhāvanā* is likewise rendered
contextually as “as formations dissociated from mind.” Collation is needed
before these readings can be finalized.

The research translation omits the nine-meaning verse; it is restored
here. Its writing passage also makes “written signs” the subject of the
initial denial, whereas the Sanskrit makes phonetic elements the subject.
The present translation preserves that contrast. “Name” alternates with
“designation” only where the functional explanation requires it;
“referent” is used for *artha* in the co-arising argument, while “meaning”
is retained in the account of understanding.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The following is Organon interpretation, distinct from the reported
ontology and its critique. The passage gives grounds for distinguishing
an audible occurrence, its established semantic application, the ordered
expression, and completion of meaning. An inventory of names cannot by
itself account for the relations completed in a sentence. Yet the
recognition of those relations does not by itself establish another
substantial dharma corresponding to their arrangement.

The temporal argument also poses a positive question: how is a succession
understood as an expression when its sounds do not coexist? Retention
and synthesis are reasonable directions for further Organon inquiry.
They are not an explicit worked-out cognitive mechanism in this local
passage. The earlier research and kārikā studies develop that reconstruction
more strongly; the continuous Bhāṣya study keeps the distinction visible.

Our comparative discussion of genus membership and mediation can therefore
remain an open inquiry. This passage neither establishes a Sāṃkhya theory
of supersensible knowledge nor resolves the Kant–Hegel question. Its own
contribution is more specific: designation, relational completion,
sequence, and substantial existence cannot be treated as interchangeable
determinations.
