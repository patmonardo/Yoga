# VAK_2.64 Bhāṣya — Condition Counts and the Single World-Cause

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> dvābhyām anye tu jāyante neśvarādeḥ kramādibhiḥ // 2.64 //

> Mind and mental factors indeed arise through four conditions;
> the pair of attainments through three; the others through two.
> They do not arise from Īśvara and the like, on grounds of succession

The natural unit runs from the question at 101.07 through the conclusion
at 102.18. It includes both the condition counts and the ensuing argument

to material causality, beginning the next study. The anchor follows
the polished kārikā; the Sanskrit below retains the running witness's

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Now, which dharma arises through how many conditions? “Mind and mental
factors indeed through four.” For them, cause-condition comprises all
five causes. Immediately antecedent condition is the preceding mind
and mental factors, uninterrupted by others. Object-condition consists,
as appropriate, of the five objects and all dharmas. Predominant
condition consists of all dharmas other than the dharma itself.

“The pair of attainments through three.” Cessation-attainment and
non-percipient attainment have no object-condition, for they do not
have objects. Their cause-condition consists of two causes: co-arisen
cause, namely arising and the other characteristics; and homogeneous
cause, namely previously arisen wholesome dharmas belonging to the
same level. Their immediately antecedent condition is the mind
[entering] the attainment, together with its associated factors.
Predominant condition is as before. These two attainments are immediately
successive to mind because they are produced through mental preparation.
They are not immediately antecedent conditions, because they obstruct
the arising of mind.

“The others, however, arise through two.” The other dissociated
formations and material dharmas arise through cause-condition and
predominant condition, precisely as already specified.

But someone says: “You maintain that things arise from conditions,
and that Īśvara, Puruṣa, Pradhāna, or the like is not the cause of
the entire world. What is the reason for this?” If you admit that
establishment [of a claim] is brought about by a reason, does not
that very admission exclude the thesis that one cause, Īśvara or
the like, is the cause of everything? Moreover: “Not from Īśvara and
the like, on grounds of succession and the like.”

For if there were only one cause, Īśvara or anything else, the whole
world would have to come into being simultaneously. Yet things are
observed to arise in succession. “Then that succession may depend
on Īśvara's will: let this arise now, let this cease, and let that
arise later.” In that case, through differences of will, a plurality
of causes has been established. Moreover, those differences of will
too would be simultaneous, since their cause, Īśvara, is undifferentiated.
Or, if they depend upon differences in other causes, Īśvara alone
would not be the cause. If those causes in turn arise successively
by depending upon differences in further causes, an infinite regress
would follow. Thus, by admitting the beginninglessness of a succession
of causes differentiated without end, the adherent of Īśvara as cause
would not have gone beyond the account of the followers of Śākya.

“Even if Īśvara's intentions are simultaneous, the world need not
be simultaneous, since he produces it in accordance with the intentions.”
No: there is no subsequent difference in those intentions [to account
for the later production]. And what purpose does Īśvara have in so
much effort of creation? If it is pleasure, then, being unable to
produce that pleasure without a means, he would not be sovereign
with respect to it; and likewise with respect to anything else.
If, having created creatures afflicted by many calamities in hell
and elsewhere, Īśvara takes pleasure in that, homage to such an Īśvara!
A verse concerning him is indeed aptly sung:

> Because he burns, because he is sharp, fierce, and scorching,
> because he eats flesh, blood, and marrow, he is called Rudra.

Moreover, someone who accepts a single cause of the world would
thereby deny the directly evident efficacy of other things. Someone
who imagines Īśvara as an agent together with other causes would be
making merely a declaration of devotion, since no activity of anything
other than those causes is observed in that production. And when
other causes cooperate, Īśvara would not be sovereign.

“Then the first creation has Īśvara as its cause.” Since that too
would depend on nothing else, it would follow that, like Īśvara,
it is beginningless. The same should be said, as appropriate, concerning
Pradhāna. Therefore the world has no single cause. It is beings' own
actions that generate them in their respective births. Yet those
unfortunate people whose understanding is undeveloped, while experiencing
their own maturation-fruits, falsely imagine some other Īśvara.

## 4. The Three Condition Profiles

| Dharma class | Conditions | Count |
|---|---|---:|
| Mind and mental factors | Cause, immediately antecedent, object, predominant | 4 |
| Cessation and non-percipient attainments | Cause, immediately antecedent, predominant | 3 |
| Material dharmas and remaining dissociated formations | Cause, predominant | 2 |

The two attainments are themselves dissociated formations. “Other”
therefore excludes those already separately enumerated. Their lack
of object-condition means that the attainments do not apprehend an
object; it does not mean that they cannot become objects of cognition.

“All five causes” gives the membership of cause-condition in the
mental account. It does not override 2.60's particular exclusions,
such as the absence of homogeneous cause for the first uncontaminated
mind. Counts of conditions and counts of causes within cause-condition
remain distinct.

The attainment account makes the direction of the relation explicit.
Mental preparation produces the attainment, so the entering mental
complex is its immediately antecedent condition. The attainment itself
blocks mental arising and is not the immediately antecedent condition
of the emerging mind. This continues 2.62's distinction between being
immediately successive to mind and serving as an immediately antecedent
condition. The preceding mind's relation to the emergence-mind is not
replaced by an attainment-to-mind relation of the same kind.

## 5. The Argument from Succession

The final instrumental *kramādibhiḥ* gives reasons for rejecting the
single-cause explanation. It does not make succession a fifth condition
or translate as “produced by sequence instead.” The commentary tests
whether one undifferentiated sufficient cause can explain differentiated
timing. Appeals to will must then explain the differences in intentions
and their efficacy; merely naming those intentions does not answer
the question.

The replies distinguish several proposals: differing intentions,
simultaneous intentions whose results occur later, an agent cooperating
with other causes, and an agent responsible only for the first creation.
The final-creation argument is not simply another version of the first:
it asks why a creation depending on nothing besides a beginningless
cause would itself have a beginning. The text applies its reasoning
to Pradhāna as appropriate rather than treating every proposed principle
as a personal deity with intentions.

The purpose and suffering objections are also part of the source's
argument. The ironic salutation and Rudra verse are retained as the
commentary's polemical voice, not presented as the Organon study's
address to religious adherents. The excerpt does not identify the
quoted verse's source; no attribution is supplied here.

The closing karma statement concerns beings' respective births and
the experience of maturation-fruits. It must not be expanded into
a claim that every physical event, or every experience whatsoever,
is exclusively the result of past karma. The passage has just retained
a plurality of causal and conditional relations.

## 6. Textual, Voice, and Translation Decisions

The initial *āha tu* introduces the challenge to the conditions account.
The subsequent *yadi khalu hetukṛtāṃ siddhiṃ manyase ... vyudāsaḥ*
is read as the reply: if the challenger accepts establishment through
a reason, the exclusive single-cause thesis is already compromised.
*Hetu* here permits the sense “reason” in an argument, while retaining
the causal play of the reply. The research translation instead makes
this establish Īśvara and misses the explicit *vyudāsa*, exclusion.
The local rhetorical argument should not be substituted for the more
fully developed succession argument that follows.

Later quoted objections are identified through their interrogative
and conditional constructions; these editorial quotation marks make
the dialogue readable without inventing named speakers. The source
expressly names Īśvara, Puruṣa, and Pradhāna, but it does not identify
a historical interlocutor for each objection.

The Sanskrit retains such irregular forms as *cattacaittā*,
*svabhāvarjyāḥ*, *cchandarbhedo*, *anavasthāprasaṅga*, *śākyayūrvīyameva*,
*cetibhirūpasṛṣṭāṃ*, *nihgṛhṇtaḥ*, and *kāraka mīśvaraṃ*.
“Followers of Śākya,” “calamities,” and “deny” are contextual construals
of damaged wording in otherwise identifiable arguments. They do not
constitute a silently corrected Sanskrit edition. The verse anchor
regularizes *cattacaittā* to *cittacaittā* in accordance with the verse
witness and surrounding explanation.

*Akṛtabuddhayaḥ* is rendered “whose understanding is undeveloped.”
The first pass's “failing to recognize what they themselves have done”
adds an explanation not expressed by that compound. *Puruṣakāra* in
the efficacy objection is the effective operation of other things;
it should not be confused with the capitalized *Puruṣa* named earlier.

## 7. Philosophical and Organon Study

As Organon interpretation, the condition profiles and the succession
argument share a demand for determinate explanation. A general name
for a cause does not yet explain why this kind of event has these
relations, or why one result occurs before another. The exposition
makes those differences explicit and asks whether the proposed cause
accounts for them.

For our inquiry into grounds and conditions, the attainments provide
a particularly precise case. They arise through mental preparation
while interrupting mental occurrence. Their dependence, their lack
of an object, and their obstruction of mind are different determinations
that must be held together. Calling them simply a break in cognition
would omit what the commentary has explained.

A comparison with mediation or synthesis can build on these articulated
relations. It remains a further Organon inquiry, rather than a replacement
for the source's condition counts or an attribution of Samādhi as their
philosophical unity to Vasubandhu.
