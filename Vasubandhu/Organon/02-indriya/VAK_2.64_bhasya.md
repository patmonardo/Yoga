# VAK_2.64 Bhāṣya — Through How Many Conditions

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> caturbhiś cittacaittā hi samāpattidvayaṃ tribhiḥ /
>
> dvābhyām anye tu jāyante neśvarādeḥ kramādibhiḥ // 2.64 //

> Mind and mental factors arise through four Conditions, the pair
> of attainments through three, and the rest through two. The world
> does not arise from Īśvara and the like as one cause, given
> observed succession.

## 3. Continuous Translation

Which dharmas arise through how many Conditions? Mind and mental
factors arise through four. Their cause-condition consists of all
five causes specified for it. Their immediately antecedent
Condition is earlier mind and mental factors with no others
intervening. Their Support-condition consists, as appropriate,
of the five sensory Conditions and of all dharmas. Their
predominant Condition is every dharma other than their own nature.

The two attainments arise through three Conditions. Cessation
attainment and non-perception attainment have no Support-condition,
since they do not take an object. Their cause-condition has two
kinds of cause: their marks, such as arising, as co-arisen causes,
and earlier wholesome dharmas on the same level as homogeneous
causes. Their immediately antecedent Condition is the mind of
attainment with its associated factors; their predominant
Condition is as before. Because these attainments are produced
by mental preparation, they follow mind immediately. Because
they obstruct the arising of mind, they are not themselves
immediately antecedent Conditions for mind.

Other dharmas, namely material dharmas and formations dissociated
from mind, arise through two Conditions: cause and predominance,
as already set out. The discussion then states that things arise
from Conditions, not from Īśvara, Puruṣa, Pradhāna, or another
single cause of the whole world. An interlocutor asks what grounds
that denial; the wording of the exchange at 101.20–22 is compressed.
The verse appeals first to succession. If a single Īśvara or
other single cause sufficed, the whole world would arise at once.
Things are observed to arise in sequence.

Suppose Īśvara wills that one thing arise now, another perish,
and a third arise later. Different acts of will would already be
multiple causes. If those differences depend only on an unchanged
Īśvara, they too should occur together. If they require different
additional causes, Īśvara is not the sole cause. If each additional
cause in turn requires another, the account faces regress. Accepting
an unbeginning causal sequence would leave the proponent of
Īśvara within the very reasoning being challenged. Even if
Īśvara's wishes were simultaneous, the world is not; saying that
production follows each wish does not explain a later difference
when the wishes themselves have not changed.

What does Īśvara gain from the labor of creating? If pleasure,
then inability to obtain it without a means would limit his
sovereignty; likewise for any other aim. If he delights in
creating beings subject to hells and many sufferings, the text
answers with bitter praise and cites a verse describing Rudra
as burning, fierce, powerful, and eating flesh, blood, and marrow.

Accepting one cause for the world would also suppress the evident
activity of other agents. Positing Īśvara alongside other causes
would be a declaration of devotion alone, since no separate
productive operation by him is observed. With cooperating causes,
he would not be sole sovereign. If the first creation is said to
have Īśvara as its cause without depending on anything else, it
would, like Īśvara, be beginningless. The same objections apply,
as appropriate, to Pradhāna. Thus, the passage concludes, the
world has no single cause. Beings' own actions generate their
respective births. Those lacking understanding, while experiencing
the fruits of their own actions, wrongly imagine another Īśvara.
The digression ends, returning to the claim that other dharmas
arise through two Conditions.

## 4. Reading Control

The three counts specify which Conditions apply to each class;
they do not rank those Conditions. The attainment pair is
conditioned by a preceding mind yet blocks the next mind, so the
two directions of the immediately antecedent relation must be
kept distinct. The subsequent single-cause dispute uses observed
succession, differentiated wills, other causes, and agency as
separate objections. Īśvara, Puruṣa, and Pradhāna are named
proposals in that dispute, not parts of the Condition inventory.
