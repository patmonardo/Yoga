# VAK_2.46 Bhāṣya — Primary and Secondary Marks

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> jātijātyādayas teṣāṃ te 'ṣṭadharmaikavṛttayaḥ /
>
> janyasya janikā jātir na hetupratyayair vinā // 2.46 //

> Arising-of-arising and the rest belong to those [primary marks]. The
> primary marks operate on eight dharmas, the secondary on one. Arising
> generates what is to be generated only with causes and Conditions.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Since arising and the other marks are conditioned, must they not
have further arising and other marks? They do: arising-of-arising
and the rest belong to them. There are four secondary marks:
arising-of-arising, persistence-of-persistence, aging-of-aging,
and impermanence-of-impermanence. Would not each require four
marks, and an endless regress follow through further marks?
No, because the primary and secondary marks operate on eight
dharmas and one respectively. Operation means activity, efficacy.
A dharma arises as the ninth member, counting itself, together
with eight primary and secondary marks. Arising generates the
other eight, excluding itself. Arising-of-arising generates that
arising alone. It is like hens, some producing many offspring,
others few. Persistence likewise maintains the other eight,
while persistence-of-persistence maintains persistence alone.
Apply this appropriately to aging and impermanence too.
Thus no endless regress follows.

“This is splitting the sky,” say the Sautrāntikas. Arising and
the other dharmas do not exist as distinct entities in the way
being expounded. Why? There is no evidence: neither perception,
inference, nor authoritative transmission establishes their
distinct existence as it does for Form and other dharmas.
What, then, of the sūtra: “Of the conditioned, production is
discerned, passing away is discerned, and alteration of
persistence is discerned”? The worthy interlocutor knows the
wording, not its meaning; the Blessed One said to rely on
meaning. What is the meaning?

Blinded by ignorance, the immature cling to the stream of
formations as self and belonging to self. To overturn that
mistaken commitment, the Blessed One wished to disclose the
stream's conditioned character and dependent origination.
He therefore spoke of three marks of the conditioned—not
of a moment, whose production and so forth are not discerned
and cannot serve as discernible marks. Repeating “conditioned”
shows that these mark conditionedness, not mere existence,
as water is indicated by cranes, or auspiciousness as in
the marks ascribed to a girl.

The beginning of the stream is production, its cessation is
passing away, the stream continuing is persistence, and the
difference between its earlier and later phases is alteration
of persistence. Thus Nanda's feelings are said to arise,
remain, and pass into exhaustion with his knowledge. The
verses explain: arising is the stream's beginning, passing
away its interruption, persistence the stream itself, and
alteration its earlier/later difference. Arising is previously
nonexistent occurrence, persistence continuity, passing away
its severance, and alteration the difference within continuity.
For a momentary dharma, passing away would occur without
persistence, and it would not escape passing away [provisional
contextual reading of the problematic second clause];
thus positing that persistence is pointless. Persistence therefore concerns
the stream. This also fits the Abhidharma definition,
“non-destruction of arisen formations”: an arisen moment
has no such non-destruction. The *Jñānaprasthāna* passage
asking about production, passing away, and alteration “in
one consciousness” can likewise concern consciousness of
one group same-class.

The marks also fit moment by moment without positing
distinct entities. Occurrence after not occurring is
production; non-occurrence after occurring is passing
away. Each preceding moment's connection with a subsequent
moment is persistence; difference within that continuation
is alteration [contextual reading of the damaged phrase].
What if similar moments arise? They are not without
difference. How is that known? Differences in times of
falling of thrown and unthrown objects, with strong and
weak projection [comparison damaged], establish differences
in transformation of their Great Elements. Formations
with small differences appear similar despite alteration.

But the last moment of a sound or flame, and the six
Essences at final nirvāṇa, have no following moment.
Would the marks then fail to apply, since persistence
and alteration are absent [damaged compound contextually
read]? Persistence alone is not called the mark; rather,
alteration of persistence is. Wherever persistence occurs
there is necessarily alteration, so the classification
does not break down. In summary, the sūtra indicates
that the conditioned occurs after not occurring and
then no longer occurs, while whatever continuity is
called persistence becomes otherwise and otherwise
[the summary's first phrase is defective]. What need
is there for distinct arising-entities?

How can the same dharma be both what is marked and
its mark? The marks of a great person are not other
than that person; dewlap, tail, hump, hoof, and horn
are not separate from the cow they mark. Hardness
and the other characteristics of the Earth Principle
and the other Principles are not separate from them.
Smoke is recognized at a distance through its upward
motion, which is not another entity apart from it.
The same reasoning applies here. Merely apprehending
the nature of Form and other dharmas does not yet
disclose conditionedness until prior absence, later
absence, and differences of the continuum are known.
Thus it is not recognized by that apprehension alone,
yet arising and the other marks need not be distinct
entities apart from those dharmas.

Suppose the marks are distinct entities. What would
be wrong? A dharma would be arisen, persisting, aged,
and destroyed at the same time, since they co-arise.
“Their activity occurs at different times: future
arising acts, since what is already arisen is not
generated; once the dharma has been generated, present
persistence and the others act.” First determine
whether the future exists as a distinct entity;
only then can its generation be established. Even
if it exists, how is arising future while active,
or present once its activity has ceased? The temporal
criteria must be explained.

Persistence, aging, and impermanence acting together
would still make one dharma persist, age, and perish
in one moment. Which does it do? If their activity
is successive, momentariness is contradicted. “Our
moment is just the interval in which all this is
completed.” Why does persistence first maintain
while co-arisen aging and impermanence do not act?
“Persistence is stronger.” Why is it later weaker,
when impermanence destroys it together with the
dharma? “Having done its work, like arising, it
cannot act again.” Arising cannot bring what is
already present into presence again; persistence,
however, could continue maintaining what it maintains.
What prevents it? “Aging and impermanence.” If they
are stronger, they should have acted earlier.

Once persistence's activity has ceased, neither
those marks nor the dharma remain: where and upon
what could aging and impermanence act, and what
is left to do? Persistence had prevented immediate
destruction; once it lets go, the dharma necessarily
fails to remain, and that is its destruction.
Even if one allows non-destruction as persistence
and destruction as impermanence, aging is untenable
for a single unchanged entity. If it changes, it
is another. Hence the cited verse: unchanged,
aging is not established; changed, it is another;
aging of one entity is therefore untenable.

Another school says impermanence destroys on
encountering a cause of destruction. That is like
saying a deity causes purgation on encountering
harītakī: why posit the deity? Let destruction
follow from the destructive cause itself. Since
consciousness and mental factors are accepted as
momentary, their impermanence does not await
another destructive cause; persistence and
impermanence would then act together, making
one dharma both persisting and destroyed.
Thus the sūtra is well interpreted as speaking
of marks with reference to the stream.

Moreover, if future arising generates what is
to be generated, why does not everything future
arise together? Because arising generates only
with causes and Conditions: it is not a generator
without their conjunction. Then it is the power
of causes and Conditions alone that we observe:
occurrence when the conjunction exists, non-occurrence
when it does not—not power of a separate arising.

“Is everything that exists perceived? Subtle
dharmic natures exist too. Without arising,
there could be no expression ‘arisen,’ nor the
genitive ‘production of Form’—it would be like
saying ‘Form of Form.’ Apply this through
impermanence.” Then non-selfhood too would
need to be posited to explain the cognition
of non-self, along with number, magnitude,
separateness, conjunction, separation, priority,
posteriority, existence, and other categories
imagined by other theorists, merely to explain
corresponding cognitions and genitives. How
would one then explain “Form's own nature”?

“Arisen” is a designation communicating occurrence
after not occurring. Such production has many
varieties; the genitive in “production of Form”
specifies the production called Form, not another.
Compare “the fragrance of sandalwood” and “the
body of a stone figurine.” Understand persistence
and the other marks similarly.

If things arise without an arising-entity,
why does not unconditioned space and the rest
arise too? Arising means occurring after not
occurring. The unconditioned always exists
and therefore does not arise. Just as not
everything is accepted as possessing an
arising-entity, accept that not everything
arises. Even where all possess arising,
the Conditions of one cannot produce another;
likewise all Conditions may be incapable
of producing the unconditioned.

“Arising and the other marks are established
as distinct entities,” maintain the Vaibhāṣikas.
Authoritative teachings are not abandoned
because critics exist, any more than barley
is left unsown because deer exist, or sweets
uneaten because flies land on them. Objections
must be answered and the established doctrine
maintained. The marks have been explained.

## 4. Nine Members and the Eight/One Reply

The group contains one base dharma, four primary marks,
and four secondaries. Every primary has eight targets,
all group members except itself. Each secondary has
one target, its corresponding primary. Those are thirty-six
operation relations, not thirty-six dharmas. The hen
comparison illustrates unequal ranges of operation;
the Sautrāntika challenge then asks for evidence of
the entities, rather than merely another regress reply.

## 5. Stream and Moment Accounts

The Sautrāntika reading first concerns the stream's
beginning, continuation, alteration, and interruption.
It then explains how moment-by-moment descriptions
are possible without distinct mark-entities. These
are connected stages, not a denial followed by an
unnoticed restoration of those entities.

The last-moment objection is answered conditionally:
where there is persistence, there is alteration.
It is not answered by inventing a subsequent moment
after the last sound, flame, or final six-Essence
moment. Nor does the mark/marked argument simply
equate every designation with the thing designated.

## 6. Activity, Restriction, and Grammatical Inference

The temporal objections test future activity, present
inactivity, simultaneous effects, successive effects,
relative strength, and aging of a single entity.
The destruction-cause analogy is a separate
redundancy challenge. The final verse restriction
then addresses why all future dharmas do not arise
together; the critic asks what work remains for
an arising-entity once the conjunction accounts
for the observed difference.

The genitive argument concerns whether “production
of Form” establishes two entities. The designation
reply and the unconditioned counterexample follow
in that order. The Vaibhāṣika close maintains
doctrine; it is not a concession to the critic.

## 7. Interpretation

In the Organon reading, Cognition joins and governs
Perception and Conception and guides the Dharma
Base, remaining distinct from consciousness and
Mind. Internal closure of an operation scheme
does not establish its ontological commitments.
Likewise, an explanatory restriction and a
grammatical relation must be distinguished from
evidence for a separate productive entity. The
presentation develops these tests through
opposed voices rather than one unqualified verdict.
