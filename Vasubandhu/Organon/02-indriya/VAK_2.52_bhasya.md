# VAK_2.52 Bhāṣya — Homogeneous Cause, Prior Arising, and the Path

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> sabhāgahetuḥ sadṛśāḥ svanikāyabhuvo 'grajāḥ /
> anyo 'nyaṃ navabhūmis tu mārgaḥ samaviśiṣṭayoḥ // 2.52 //

> Homogeneous causes are similar dharmas, of their own class and level,
> that have arisen earlier. But the path on nine levels is reciprocally
> a homogeneous cause, of what is equal or superior.

The continuous unit follows the close of co-arisen causality and covers
the definition, temporal debate, and special range of the path. The
extension to worldly application-produced qualities begins VAK 2.53.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

What is the homogeneous cause? “Similar [dharmas] are the homogeneous
cause.” Similar dharmas are homogeneous causes of similar dharmas:
for example, the five wholesome aggregates of wholesome aggregates,
afflicted aggregates of afflicted aggregates, and indeterminate aggregates
of indeterminate aggregates, reciprocally [within the order specified
below]. Indeterminate material form is a cause of all five. Others say
that the other four are not causes of material form, because they are
inferior [in the relevant respect].

Within one commonality of existence, the embryonic *kalala* stage is a
cause of the ten stages beginning with *kalala*; the *arbuda* stage and
the rest are causes of the stages beginning with themselves, with one
stage successively subtracted. In other instances of the same kind,
all ten stages are causes of all ten. The account should also be applied
in detail to external things: barley to barley, rice to rice, and so
forth. Against those who do not accept material form as a homogeneous
cause of material form stands this text: “Past great elements are a
cause and a predominant condition of future great elements.”

Are all [dharmas] homogeneous causes of similar ones? No. Which, then?
“Those belonging to their own class and level.” Their class and level
are their own. There are five classes, from what is abandoned through
seeing suffering through what is abandoned through cultivation. There
are nine levels: the desire realm and the eight concentrations and
formless levels. What is abandoned through seeing suffering is a
homogeneous cause of what is abandoned through seeing suffering, not
of the others; and so on through what is abandoned through cultivation.
Further, desire-realm dharmas are causes of desire-realm dharmas,
first-concentration dharmas of first-concentration dharmas, and so on
through the summit of existence: of their own level, not of others.

Even among these, not all qualify. Which do? “Those born earlier.”
Previously arisen dharmas are homogeneous causes of later ones, whether
the latter have arisen or have not arisen. Future dharmas are not
homogeneous causes. How is this known? From the treatise:

“What is the homogeneous cause? Previously arisen wholesome roots are
causes, as homogeneous causes, of subsequently arisen wholesome roots
and associated dharmas within their own realm. Likewise, past ones are
causes of past and present ones; past and present ones are causes of
future ones. Thus it should be stated.”

But the treatise also says: “If a dharma is a cause of another dharma,
is there any time when it is not its cause?” It answers, “No.” There
is no fault, because “never” is said with reference to the co-arisen,
associated, and maturation causes.

Someone instead thinks: even while future, a dharma becomes fixed as
a homogeneous cause in the state of arising; “never not a cause” is
said with reference to that final state. This does not resolve the
difficulty, since before its state of arising that dharma was not a
homogeneous cause and afterward becomes one. Moreover, in the question,
“If a dharma is immediately antecedent to another, is there any time
when it is not immediately antecedent?”, that same interpretation could
answer, “Never.” Why does the text instead say, “If that dharma has not
arisen”? [A reply proposes:] To show both approaches: what is done there
should be done here, and what is done here should be done there. What
advantage would that secure? It would only suggest lack of skill on
the treatise author's part. Therefore the former resolution is preferable.

What, then, of the statement concerning future dharmas: “Excepting
identity-view and the truth of suffering associated with it, the other
afflicted truth of suffering has identity-view as a cause but is not
a cause of identity-view; what was excepted both has identity-view as
a cause and is a cause of identity-view”? This should be formulated,
“Excepting the truth of suffering associated with future identity-view,”
and should at least be understood in that sense.

How, then, is this statement of the Prajñapti commentary interpreted:
“All dharmas are fixed in four respects: cause, result, support, and
object”? Here “cause” is the associated and co-arisen cause; “result”
is the result of efficacy and the predominant result; “support” is the
eye and so forth; and “object” is visible form and so forth.

But then a homogeneous cause becomes a cause after not having been one.
That is indeed accepted with respect to its state, not its substance.
The causal assemblage is a result of the state, not a result of the
substance [provisional construal].

What would follow if a future dharma too were a homogeneous cause, as
it can be a maturation cause? It would have been included in the treatise.
[Reply:] Only the one capable of taking and giving a result is included,
so there is no fault in mentioning only that one. [Rejoinder:] That is
not so. A homogeneous cause has a homogeneous-outflow result. This is
inappropriate for a future cause, because there is no earlier-and-later
order [within the future]. Nor can an already arisen dharma be the
outflow of a future one, just as a past dharma cannot be the outflow of
a present one: the result must not precede its cause. There is therefore
no future homogeneous cause.

Then there should likewise be no future maturation cause: a maturation
result cannot occur earlier than or together with its cause, and within
the future there is no earlier-and-later order. That does not follow.
Without temporal order, calling a similar dharma the homogeneous cause
of a similar one would entail their being causes of one another and
thus outflows of one another; mutual homogeneous outflow is unreasonable.
But without temporal order a maturation cause does not thereby entail
reciprocal cause-and-result status, because cause and result have different
characteristics. Thus the homogeneous cause is established by state,
whereas the maturation cause is established by characteristic; the
latter is not excluded even when future.

The homogeneous cause was said to belong to its own level. Does that
restriction apply to every case? It applies to the contaminated.
“But the path of nine levels is reciprocally [a homogeneous cause].”
“Homogeneous cause” remains the governing topic. The truth of the path
in the access concentration, the intermediate concentration, the four
concentrations, and the three formless levels is a homogeneous cause
across these levels. Why? It is adventitious in those levels and does
not fall within their realm, because it has not been appropriated by
the cravings belonging to those levels. It is therefore a homogeneous
cause of what is of the same kind even on another level.

It is, however, a cause “of what is equal or superior,” [not] of what
is inferior. For example, receptivity to dharma-knowledge concerning
suffering is a homogeneous cause of its own future counterpart and
of what is superior, up to knowledge of non-arising. Knowledge of
non-arising is a cause only of knowledge of non-arising, since nothing
else is superior. The paths of seeing, cultivation, and no-more-learning
are causes of three, two, and one [path divisions], respectively. Within
these too, the path of weak faculties is a cause of paths of weak and
sharp faculties; the path of sharp faculties is a cause only of a path
of sharp faculties. Thus the paths of the faith-follower, the one liberated
through faith, and the one liberated at a suitable time are causes of
six, four, and two [path-types]; the paths of the Dharma-follower, the
one attained to view, and the one liberated without dependence on a
suitable time are causes of three, two, and one.

How can a path on a lower level be equal or superior to one on a higher
level? Through its faculties and accumulation of causes. Among the paths
beginning with seeing, and among the grades beginning with weak-weak,
each successive member is more enriched by causes. Even where the two
[path-types in the damaged example] cannot occur in one continuum,
the arisen one is a cause of the future one.

## 4. Similarity Is Restricted Causal Continuity

The definition joins similarity to class, level, and prior arising.
Similarity alone does not establish the relation. The five classes are
the four divisions abandoned through seeing the respective truths and
the division abandoned through cultivation. These are not five aggregates.
The nine levels here are the desire realm, four concentrations, and four
formless levels.

The embryonic examples specify ordered development within one existence
and distinguish it from relations to other instances of the same kind.
They do not authorize a later stage to cause an earlier stage already
past. The explicit *agraja* restriction controls that reading. The text
also preserves differing accounts of material form's causal range.

## 5. Why Prior Arising Matters

The extended debate distinguishes a dharma's existence from its having
this particular causal status. The reply accepts a change of state
without a change of substance. This belongs to the account being defended;
it is not a neutral premise shared by every Buddhist theory of time.

“Future” here is a technical temporal status. A homogeneous cause must
already have arisen, though its result may still be future. The argument
also presupposes the absence of established earlier-and-later ordering
within the future. It should not be paraphrased as a simple denial that
people can anticipate ordered future events.

The maturation comparison is decisive: its cause and result differ in
characteristic, preventing the reciprocal inference that would arise
from similarity without temporal order. The passage does not abolish
the eventual succession of maturation cause and result.

## 6. Scriptural Interpretation and Doctrinal Voices

The objections test the proposed temporal restriction against inherited
statements about invariant causal relations. The replies restrict the
scope of those statements to particular causes, reject a proposed
alternative explanation, and propose a revised construal of the future
identity-view passage. These are visible interpretive operations, not
uncontested readings silently supplied by this edition.

The Prajñapti passage also distinguishes cause, result, support, and
object. Eye as support and visible form as object retain distinct roles;
the word “cause” does not absorb all four determinations into one.

## 7. The Path's Different Range

The path's nine levels are not the ordinary nine levels just listed.
They are access concentration, intermediate concentration, four
concentrations, and the first three formless levels. Its cross-level
causality is explained through non-appropriation by the relevant cravings.
Being present at a level and belonging to its contaminated realm-order
are distinct relations.

“Reciprocally” allows causal relations across levels, subject to earlier
arising and the equal-or-superior restriction. It does not restore the
simultaneous mutual causality discussed under *sahabhūhetu*. Meditative
height alone does not determine superiority: faculties and causal
accumulation matter. Nor is equal-or-superior established here as an
unrestricted rule for every homogeneous cause.

## 8. Textual and Translation Decisions

The unit runs from 85.08 through 87.18–19. The question at 87.20 extends
the equal-or-superior rule to worldly qualities produced through application
and introduces VAK 2.53; it is reserved for that study. The research
2.52 file omits most of the earlier temporal debate and includes this
later material. The continuous edition restores the source sequence.

The running transcription remains unchanged apart from source labels
and surrounding spacing. Its isolated *evam atīte* at 85.27 appears
displaced beside the subsequent treatise quotation; the English preserves
the past/present/future distinctions in that quotation without pretending
the transmitted placement is secure. The sentence *avasthāphalaṃ hi
sāmargyaṃ na dravyaphalam* (with the witness's additional spelling defect)
is rendered provisionally in its state/substance context.

At 87.11, the witness reads *nyūnasya heturbhavati*, “is a cause of the
inferior,” despite the governing “equal or superior” and the following
examples. The translation supplies “[not]” explicitly. At 87.12–13,
*anyaviśiṣṭabhāvāt* is contextually read as the absence of anything superior;
this too needs collation. Forms such as *caturṣuraneṣu*,
*tadbhami kābhiḥ*, and *mānajātīyasya* are damaged but contextually
intelligible in the level discussion.

The last line's *śraddhārnusārimārgayor* fails to name both members of
its dual construction clearly. The translation marks the missing
identification rather than inventing a secure pair. The claim about
an arisen cause and a future path is retained without turning it into
an assertion that both paths must actually manifest in that continuum.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

As Organon interpretation, this passage shows precisely why membership
can begin causal inquiry without completing it. Similarity and shared
classification need temporal and functional determination before they
explain a homogeneous result. The objections make those additional
requirements explicit.

The path also demonstrates why one ordering cannot replace another.
A higher meditative level is not automatically a superior path; grade,
faculties, and causal accumulation contribute different determinations.
Learning the causal relation therefore includes learning which comparison
is relevant. This is a local basis for our inquiry into mediation, not
a textual identification of the account with Kant, Hegel, or Sāṃkhya.
