# VAK_2.52 Bhāṣya — Homogeneous Cause

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> sabhāgahetuḥ sadṛśāḥ svanikāyabhuvo 'grajāḥ /
>
> anyo 'nyaṃ navabhūmis tu mārgaḥ samaviśiṣṭayoḥ // 2.52 //

> A homogeneous cause is similar, of its own class and level, and
> prior-born. The Path crosses the nine levels as cause for the equal or
> the superior.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

What is a homogeneous cause? Similar dharmas are homogeneous causes
of similar dharmas. Thus the five wholesome Bases are causes of
wholesome Bases, afflicted of afflicted, and indeterminate of
indeterminate. Indeterminate Form can be a cause of all five
indeterminate Bases. Others say the four non-Form Bases are not causes
of Form, because they are inferior [the force of *nyūnatvāt* in this
example requires further study]. Within one shared class of beings,
the embryo's first stage is a cause for its ten stages, the next for
that and later stages, and so on with one fewer at each step. Among
beings of the same kind in different classes, each of the ten stages
can cause the corresponding stages. For external things, barley is
a cause of barley and rice of rice; extend the account accordingly.
Against those who deny Form as a homogeneous cause of Form, a cited
treatise says past Great Elements are a cause and dominant condition
of future Great Elements.

Are all similar dharmas homogeneous causes of one another? No: they
must belong to their own class and level. The five classes run from
what is abandoned by seeing suffering through what is abandoned by
cultivation. There are nine levels: the Desire Realm and the eight
meditative and Formless levels. A dharma abandoned by seeing
suffering is a homogeneous cause of another of that class, not of
the other classes; likewise through the cultivation class. Each
Realm or meditative level is similarly restricted to its own level.
Even then, not all qualify: the cause must have arisen earlier.
Earlier arisen dharmas are homogeneous causes of later dharmas,
whether those later dharmas have already arisen or remain future.
A future dharma is not such a cause.

Why? A cited treatise defines the cause using wholesome roots
arisen earlier in relation to later roots and their associated
dharmas within the same Realm. It extends the relation from past
to past and present, and from past and present to future dharmas.
Another treatise asks whether a dharma that is cause of another
ever ceases to be its cause, and answers no. The reply says that
answer concerns co-arisen, associated, and maturation causes, so
there is no fault. One might instead claim that a future dharma
becomes fixed as a homogeneous cause just as it is about to arise,
and read the second answer with that last stage in view. But then
it was not a homogeneous cause before that stage and became one
afterward. A parallel question about an immediately preceding
condition would, on that device, also admit “never,” whereas the
treatise gives a qualified answer for a dharma that has not arisen.
Applying opposite readings in the two cases would leave the
author's method inexplicable. The earlier reply is preferable.

What, then, of the cited passage on future identity-view, its
associated dharmas, and afflicted suffering-truth that is caused
by identity-view but does not cause it? The qualification about
future identity-view should attach to what is associated with
identity-view; this is also how its sense should be understood.
Another treatise says that all dharmas are fixed in four respects:
cause, result, Basis, and Support. Here “cause” means associated
and co-arisen causes; “result” means the result of human effort
and the dominant result; Basis is such a faculty as the eye, and
Support such an object as visible Form.

Does this mean a homogeneous cause changes from being no cause to
being a cause? Yes, with respect to its state, not its entity:
the conjunction is the result of a state, not of an entity.
Why not make a future dharma a homogeneous cause, as with a
maturation cause? It would then have been included in the treatise.
The objection replies that only a cause able to give or take a
result is included. The response denies that escape: a homogeneous
cause has a homogeneous-outflow result, and for a future cause
such a result is untenable because there is no earlier and later
within the future. Nor can what has already arisen be the outflow
of what is still future, for the result would precede the cause.

Would this also rule out future maturation causes? Their maturation
result likewise cannot be earlier than or simultaneous with its
cause, and the future has no earlier or later. The reply distinguishes
them: without temporal priority, similar dharmas would cause one
another and be one another's homogeneous outflows, which is
untenable. Maturation cause and result differ in character, so
reciprocal causation does not follow in that way. Homogeneous cause
is determined by temporal state; maturation cause by the character
of its cause and result. A future maturation cause is therefore
not ruled out by this argument.

Does the own-level rule apply to every contaminated dharma? Yes.
The Path, however, is a homogeneous cause across the nine levels:
the preparatory level, intermediate meditation, four meditations,
and three Formless levels. It is adventitious in them and is not
appropriated by cravings belonging to those levels. Thus a Path
at one level may cause a Path at another level of the same kind.
The verse restricts the result to an equal or superior Path. The
next printed sentence says instead “it is a cause of the inferior”
[*nyūnasya hetur bhavati*, 87.11]; that reading conflicts with
the examples that immediately follow and remains unresolved.

Receptivity to Dharma Knowledge concerning suffering is a cause
of a future instance of itself and of superior stages up to
Knowledge of non-arising. Knowledge of non-arising causes only
Knowledge of non-arising, since nothing surpasses it. The paths
of seeing, cultivation, and beyond training serve as causes for
three, two, and one corresponding ranks. A Path with weak Faculties
causes Paths with weak or sharp Faculties; a sharp-Faculty Path
causes only sharp-Faculty Paths. The text then distributes the
paths of faith-follower, faith-liberated, and time-liberated among
six, four, and two possibilities, and the corresponding paths of
Dharma-follower, one who has attained through view, and one not
dependent on time among three, two, and one. How can a Path on a
lower level be equal or superior to one on a higher level? By
the Faculties and the accumulation of causes. Among paths of
seeing and the later stages, and among successive grades of weak
Faculties, each later stage has accumulated more causes. Even
where two faith-follower Paths cannot occur in one continuum,
one that has arisen can cause one that remains future. The next
question asks whether only the Path is a homogeneous cause of
equal and superior results; its answer opens 2.53.

## 4. Interpretation

The defining relation joins similarity with temporal priority. The own-class/own-level restriction is scoped to contaminated dharmas; the Path is its cross-level exception, not an exception to priority. The equal/superior direction is the kārikā's wording. The conflict in the Bhāṣya witness is unresolved and should not be repaired by silently adding a negative.
