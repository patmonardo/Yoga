# VAK_2.27 Bhāṣya — The Restricted Ground of Afflictions

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> krodhopanāhaśāṭhyerṣyāpradāsamrakṣamatsarāḥ /
> māyāmadavihiṃsāś ca parīttakleśabhūmikāḥ // 2.27 //

> Anger, resentment, deceitful crookedness, envy, spite, concealment,
> stinginess, deception, intoxication, and harmfulness belong to
> the ground of limited afflictions.

The commentary explains their restricted ground but expressly
defers their individual definitions. It then closes the fivefold
classification and acknowledges further factors not fixed in it.

## 2. Continuous Sanskrit

> krodhopanāhaśāṭhyerṣyāpradāsamrakṣamatsarāḥ /
> māyāmadavihiṃsāś ca parīttakleśabhūmikāḥ // 2.27 //
> parīttakleśā bhūmir eṣāṃ ta ime parīttakleśabhūmikā
> avidyāmātreṇa bhāvanāheyena manobhūmikenaiva ca saṃprayogāt /
> eṣāṃ tu nirdeśa upakleśeṣu kariṣyate /
> uktā ime pañcaprakārāś caittāḥ /
> anye 'pi cāniyatāḥ santi vitarkavicārakaukṛtyamiddhādayaḥ /

The unit occupies printed 57.04–57.09 in
`Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt`, ending before the question
*tatra vaktavyaṃ kasmiṃścitte kati caittā avaśyaṃ bhavantīti*.
That question begins the next study's inquiry into necessary
mental-factor counts.

Word division is editorial. The verse's defective
*parīttakleśbhūmikāḥ* is normalized to *parīttakleśabhūmikāḥ*,
as supported by the immediately following prose; *nirdekśa* is
read as *nirdeśa*. No independent collation is claimed. This local
class-name lacks the *mahā* present in the fifth name transmitted
in the introductory classification at VAK 2.23; the studies retain
the respective local readings.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“Anger, resentment, deceitful crookedness, envy, spite, concealment,
stinginess, deception, intoxication, and harmfulness belong to
the ground of limited afflictions.” Limited afflictions are their
ground; hence they are called factors belonging to the ground
of limited afflictions, because they are associated only with
ignorance that is abandonable through cultivation and belongs
to the mental sphere. Their exposition, however, will be given
in the discussion of the secondary afflictions. These five classes
of mental factors have been stated. There are also other, unfixed
factors, such as initial examination, sustained examination,
regret, and torpor.

The restriction “only with ignorance” is read within the affliction
classification, not as denying accompaniment by consciousness
and the universal mental factors. The instrumental qualifiers
*bhāvanāheyena* and *manobhūmikena* qualify the associated ignorance
in the compressed Sanskrit construction. Their effect is to
restrict the range of the present class.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The kārikā lists ten members of the fifth class. The prose explains
the class-name by association, abandonment category, and mental
sphere, postpones individual exposition, and concludes the fivefold
division introduced at VAK 2.23.

The final sentence prevents that conclusion from being mistaken
for an exhaustive inventory of all mental factors. It names further
unfixed factors and ends with “and so forth.” No objection, named
school, or alternative view appears in this short unit.

## 5. The Ten Names and the Deferred Definitions

The ten names distinguish anger from resentment, crookedness
from deception, and spite from harmfulness. The English renderings
are provisional lexical guides; the Bhāṣya does not yet explain
how those distinctions are to be determined.

Likewise, “concealment” for *mrakṣa* and “intoxication” for *mada*
should not be treated as complete definitions. The latter gloss
does not establish substance-induced intoxication as the subject
of the factor. Its technical determination belongs to the deferred
exposition, alongside the others.

The class predicate applies to each listed factor. Nothing here
states that all ten occur together in every afflicted consciousness.
The statement “always in the afflicted,” which governed the six
in VAK 2.26, must not be silently carried over to this different
class.

## 6. What Restricts the Ground?

The compressed association clause joins ignorance with two
qualifications: abandonable through cultivation and belonging
to the mental sphere. The first distinguishes the relevant
abandonment category from abandonment through seeing; the second
restricts the sphere to mental consciousness rather than the
five sensory consciousnesses.

“Only” requires a controlled scope. VAK 2.23 has already established
that mental factors do not occur without consciousness, and
VAK 2.24 has stated the universal accompanying factors. Reading
*avidyāmātreṇa* as “ignorance is the sole accompanying dharma of
any kind” would contradict that context. It restricts the
association being discussed among afflictions.

*Parītta*, limited or restricted, thus concerns range. It does
not assess the harm caused by an occurrence as negligible.
Nor does a shared restricted range entail that each listed factor
has the same definition or must co-occur with the other nine.

## 7. The Further Unfixed Factors

The closing examples are *vitarka*, *vicāra*, *kaukṛtya*, and
*middha*: initial examination, sustained examination, regret,
and torpor. The prose calls them *aniyata*, unfixed, and leaves
the list open with *ādi*.

Here “unfixed” is not the ethical classification *avyākṛta*,
indeterminate. The sentence does not declare all these factors
ethically neutral. It introduces further factors beyond the five
classes just expounded; their particular distributions await
the ensuing count of factors in different consciousnesses.

This also qualifies how the earlier *pañcadhā*, fivefold, should
be understood. It announces the five classes treated in that
sequence, but the explicit acknowledgment of other factors prevents
us from treating those classes as an exhaustive partition of
every possible mental factor.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for Translation

The conventional translation preserves the grammatical dependence
of the instrumental qualifiers on the associated ignorance.
The analytical account can then explain their restriction of
the class's range without turning the compact sentence into
three unrelated assertions.

“Ground” continues to mean the sphere of operation established
in VAK 2.23. “Limited afflictions” and “secondary afflictions”
render different expressions here: *parīttakleśa* names the present
restricted class, while *upakleśa* names the discussion to which
the author defers their definitions. Their relation should not
be simplified into an unsupported identity of every member of
the two ranges.

The next question, at 57.09, is placed with VAK 2.28. It introduces
how many mental factors necessarily occur in each consciousness,
followed immediately by the desire-realm classification and the
first numerical case. This boundary keeps that inquiry continuous.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The fifth class demonstrates a distinction between common range
conditions and necessary co-presence. Its members belong together
under the stated restrictions, but that classificatory unity is
not a claim that they form one simultaneously required set.

For Organon interpretation, a ground must therefore be read with
its actual quantifiers and qualifications. The universal class,
the wholesome class, and this restricted class cannot all be
represented simply as lists that must accompany every occurrence
of any member. Association, domain, and abandonment each determine
a different aspect of the present classification.

The acknowledgment of unfixed factors also makes the transition
substantive. Naming classes has not yet answered the question
of which factors, or how many, occur in a particular consciousness.
The following inquiry must combine the classifications with
more particular conditions of occurrence. This passage prepares
that work rather than supplying its results in advance.
