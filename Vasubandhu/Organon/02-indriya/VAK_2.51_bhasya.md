# VAK_2.51 Bhāṣya — Followers of Consciousness

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> caittā dvau saṃvarau teṣāṃ cetaso lakṣaṇāni ca /
>
> cittānuvartinaḥ kālaphalādiśubhatādibhiḥ // 2.51 //

> The mental factors, the two restraints, and their marks, together with
> the marks of consciousness, follow consciousness in time, result, and
> ethical status, and related respects.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Which dharmas follow consciousness? The mental factors, the two
restraints, and their marks and the marks of consciousness. All the
mental factors associated with consciousness are included. The two
restraints are meditative and uncontaminated restraint; their marks,
such as arising, and those of consciousness are included too.

How do these dharmas follow consciousness? In brief, by time, result,
and ethical status, among other respects. In time, they arise, persist,
and cease together with it and belong to the same period. In result,
they share fruit, maturation, and homogeneous outflow. The word
“same” here means “together.” In ethical status, they are wholesome,
unwholesome, or indeterminate as consciousness is. Thus they follow
consciousness in ten respects.

The smallest consciousness is a co-arisen cause of fifty-eight dharmas:
ten universal mental factors, forty marks of those factors, and eight
of its own marks and secondary marks. Fifty-four dharmas are, in turn,
co-arisen causes of that consciousness, excluding its own secondary
marks. Others propose fourteen: the ten universal factors and only
consciousness's own marks. This is rejected by appeal to a cited
*Prakaraṇa* passage about identity-view, its associated factors, and
their marks. The Kashmir teachers say that even if the words about
associated factors are omitted in a copy, they must be read or the
sense must be understood that way. The cited wording is damaged in
the running witness; the precise text needs collation.

Is everything caused through a co-arisen cause itself co-arisen? Yes.
But something can co-arise without being a co-arisen cause: a dharma's
secondary marks, including those following consciousness; certain
forms derived from the elements; and acquisitions arising together
with what is acquired. Their fruits, maturation, and outflows need
not be the same, and acquisitions do not accompany the acquired
thing both before and after its arising.

An objection presses the causal relation between things that arise
simultaneously. Consider lamp and light, or sprout and shadow: perhaps
the earlier collection of conditions produces both together. A reply
appeals to their fixed presence and absence together as a sign of
cause and result. The critic asks how this establishes causation in
both directions, and whether the same reasoning would wrongly extend
to derived form and secondary marks.

The defense compares three sticks standing through mutual support.
The critic asks whether their standing instead comes from the earlier
conditions, and notes that thread, pegs, or earth may also support
them. The close adds that these dharmas can have other causes,
including homogeneous cause; co-arisen cause is thereby maintained.
The next question asks what homogeneous cause is.

## 4. Movement and Voice of the Commentary

The first movement specifies the follower groups and the ten shared
respects. The second counts co-arisen causality in opposite directions:
58 from consciousness, 54 toward it. A proposed count of fourteen is
refused by appeal to an earlier textual authority. The last movement
distinguishes co-arising from co-arisen cause and stages objections
to simultaneous causal reciprocity. Lamp and light, sprout and shadow,
and the three sticks are disputed analogies, not independent proofs
that every simultaneous pair is mutually causal. The closing defense
retains co-arisen cause while allowing other causal relations.
