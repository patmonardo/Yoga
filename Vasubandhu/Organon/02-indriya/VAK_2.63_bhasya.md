# VAK_2.63 Bhāṣya — Activity and the State of the Conditioned Dharma

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> jāyamāne tato 'nyau tu pratyayau tadviparyayāt // 2.63 //

> Two causes perform their activity with respect to the ceasing
> [dharma]; three [do so] with respect to the arising [dharma].

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Now, when these conditions perform their activity, in what state of
a dharma do they perform it? Cause-condition has been stated to be
fivefold. Of these, “two causes perform their activity with respect
to the ceasing [dharma].” What is called “ceasing” is the present,
because it is directed toward cessation. Co-arisen and associated
causes perform their activity with respect to it. Their operation
concerns a fruit even though it has arisen together with them.
“Three [perform their activity] with respect to the arising [dharma].”
What is called “arising” is the future insofar as it is directed
toward arising. Homogeneous, pervasive, and maturation causes perform
their activity with respect to it. So much for cause-condition.

“The other two conditions, however, [operate] in the reverse order.”
Immediately antecedent condition and object-condition should be
understood by reversing the order in which cause-condition was divided
into two and explained according to its activity. Immediately antecedent
condition performs its activity with respect to the arising [dharma],
by giving an opening. Object-condition [performs its activity] with
respect to the ceasing [dharma], because present mind and mental factors
apprehend [the object]. Predominant condition, however, stands in every
state through its character of non-obstruction; precisely this is its
activity. The conditions have been explained together with their activities.

## 4. Whose State Is Being Specified?

The opening question supplies the governing construction: *kimavasthe
dharme*, “with respect to a dharma in what state?” The states named
throughout are those of the conditioned dharma toward which activity
is directed. They are not automatically the temporal states of the
causes themselves.

| Relation | State of the conditioned dharma | Explanation given |
|---|---|---|
| Co-arisen and associated causes | Present, directed toward cessation | The fruit has arisen together with them |
| Homogeneous, pervasive, and maturation causes | Future, directed toward arising | The three remaining kinds of cause-condition |
| Immediately antecedent condition | Future, directed toward arising | Giving an opening |
| Object-condition | Present, directed toward cessation | Present mind and mental factors apprehend the object |
| Predominant condition | Every state | Non-obstruction itself is its activity |

The first two rows divide the five causes constituting one condition,
*hetupratyaya*. The next two reverse their order of target-states:
arising first, ceasing second. The prose then adds predominant condition,
completing the four-condition account.

“Ceasing” therefore does not mean already ceased, nor does it mean
that the two causes destroy their fruit. “Arising” is likewise qualified:
it concerns the future dharma directed toward arising, not an
indiscriminate identification of every future dharma with an impending
result. A future-directed target does not make homogeneous cause itself
future or contradict the earlier account of a past maturation cause
giving its fruit.

## 5. Translation and Textual Decisions

The source's *trayaḥ*, “three,” belongs with *jāyamāne*, “with respect
to the arising,” across the kārikā's metrical line break. The verb
“perform their activity” is supplied from the preceding construction.
The Bhāṣya explicitly names both groups, leaving neither count open.

*Avakāśadāna* is rendered “giving an opening.” The research Bhāṣya's
explanation that the predecessor “vacates the place” is an interpretive
expansion, not additional Sanskrit in this unit. No spatial container
is required by the translation. Similarly, *grahaṇa* here is apprehension
of the object by present mind and mental factors, not the taking of
a fruit discussed in the earlier causal account.

The continuous Sanskrit retains *dvī hetū*, *varktamānam*,
*sahabhūsaṃprayuktakahetu*, *hetupratyayau*, *samanatarapratyaya*,
*samantarapratyaya*, and *cittacaitair*. The anchor and English use
the readings recoverable from the verse witness and the explicit
exposition. In particular, *hetupratyayau* in the prose is construed
as the one cause-condition divided into two groups, not as two
independent cause-conditions. These are disclosed contextual construals;
no corrected critical Sanskrit edition is claimed.

The passage contains an expository question and its answer, without
an explicitly attributed rival position. Its final sentence closes
the account of conditions together with their activity. The next
question, how many conditions different dharmas arise from, belongs
to 2.64.

## 6. Philosophical and Organon Study

As Organon interpretation, the passage adds a necessary determination
to the name of a relation: the state of the term in relation to which
it operates. Naming a cause or condition does not yet say when and
how its activity is exercised. The opening question directs attention
to precisely that missing determination.

The distinction between giving an opening for a succeeding cognition
and serving as the object of present apprehension is especially useful.
These are different relations within the explanation of cognition;
one cannot substitute for the other. Predominant conditioning, in turn,
has its activity specified as non-obstruction rather than confined
to either of those states.

This gives our inquiry into grounds and conditions a more precise
basis. Any proposed synthesis must preserve the difference between
the conditioning relation, its activity, and the state of the conditioned
dharma. The passage does not itself identify their articulation with
Samādhi or divide causes and conditions into exclusively objective
and subjective domains.
