# VAK_2.18 Bhāṣya — What Possession Entails

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> caturbhiḥ sukhakāyābhyāṃ pañcabhiś cakṣurādimān /
>
> saumanasyī ca duḥkhī tu saptabhiḥ strīndriyādimān // 2.18 //

> One possessing Pleasure-Faculty or Body-Faculty necessarily possesses
> four Faculties; one possessing eye or another of that group, five;
> one possessing gladness also, five; one possessing pain, however,
> seven. One possessing Female-Faculty and so forth [necessarily
> possesses eight].

The unit is printed 50.23–51.15 in
[the Sanskrit witness](../../Sources/kosabhasya.txt). It includes
2.19's opening eight and its explanatory prose. The next clause at
51.16 continues 2.19 with eleven.

Word division is editorial. Śaraddhādīnām is read as śraddhādīnām. The short answer to the second-dhyāna question is not expanded in the Sanskrit. No independent collation is claimed.

## 3. Continuous Translation

Possession of the Pleasure-Faculty or the Body-Faculty entails possession of four faculties in each case. With pleasure, these are neutral feeling, life, Mind, and pleasure. With body, they are neutral feeling, life, Mind, and body. Possession of the eye entails five: neutral feeling, life, Mind, body, and eye. The same five-member pattern applies separately to ear, nose, and tongue, with the named sensory faculty in the fifth place.

One possessing gladness also necessarily possesses five: neutral feeling, life, Mind, pleasure, and gladness. With which Pleasure-Faculty is someone born in the second dhyāna, who has not attained the third, endowed? With an afflicted one belonging to the level of the third dhyāna. One possessing pain, however, necessarily possesses seven: body, life, Mind, and four feeling-faculties—pain, pleasure, gladness, and neutral feeling.

One possessing the Female-Faculty and so forth necessarily possesses eight. For the Female-Faculty, these are the seven just named and the Female-Faculty; the same pattern gives the seven plus the Male-Faculty or distress. “And so forth” also includes the faculties beginning with faith. Anyone possessing one of those necessarily possesses all five, together with neutral feeling, life, and Mind: a different set of eight.

## 4. Movement and Voice of the Commentary

The possession question of 2.17 continues. Four, five, seven, and eight are distributed, and the members are named. The second-dhyāna question tests the pleasure inside the gladness set.

No school. No opponent. The answer is short. The scale is kept.

## 5. Four, Five, and Seven

The triad is neutral feeling, life, and mind.

| Given | Set | Count |
|---|---|---:|
| Pleasure | triad and pleasure | 4 |
| Body | triad and body | 4 |
| Eye, ear, nose, or tongue, each | triad, body, and that faculty | 5 |
| Gladness | triad, pleasure, and gladness | 5 |
| Pain | body, life, mind, and four feelings | 7 |

Four is two cases, not pleasure and body in one set of four. Eye and so forth is a separate five for each.

The four feelings are pain, pleasure, gladness, and neutral feeling. Distress is not entailed by pain. The continuation adds it as an eighth.

## 6. The Second-Dhyāna Question

Gladness entails pleasure. The question names one born in the second dhyāna who has not attained the third. Which pleasure? An afflicted pleasure of the third level.

Birth, attainment, and possession stay apart. The person has not attained the third dhyāna. Pleasure and gladness are not said to be manifest together.

No further mechanism. Latent would add a theory the answer does not give. The claim is possession of that afflicted pleasure, the dhyāna not attained.

## 7. Equal Counts, Different Members

Female, male, and distress: the seven plus the named faculty. The seven is body, life, mind, and the four feelings. Not seven sensory faculties.

The faith-group is another eight. Any one of faith, vigor, mindfulness,
concentration, or discriminative comprehension entails the five and
the triad. It does not add the pain-holder's seven. Tu in the prose
marks the difference; the verse's tu contrasts pain with the five-count.

Necessary accompaniment. Not a claim that no other faculty can be possessed. Equal counts are not interchangeable.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for Translation

Necessarily possesses carries from 2.17, and avaśyam repeats it. Currently experiences would change the question, especially where several feelings are counted.

Ādi has local ranges. After eye: ear, nose, tongue. After female: male, distress, the five. Not one fixed list.

Aṣṭābhiḥ completes the last subject. The eleven and thirteen stay for 2.19.

The study translation marks eight as supplied from the cross-verse
continuation and makes necessary possession explicit. The count is
not an exclusive total, and the opening dual distributes over two
cases rather than combining pleasure and body into one four.

## 9. Interpretation

Possession is relational. The same numeral can be different sets. Possession can extend past what is manifest and past what is attained.

Four for pleasure is not four for body. Eight for distress is not eight for faith. The members, the initiating faculty, and the relation have to travel with the count.

Necessary co-possession is not each member producing the others. The
second-dhyāna question tests possession independently of attainment
and manifestation, without giving a mechanism for that possession.

Mind belongs to the common possession triad; this does not identify it
with the other Faculties or explain the mechanism of their possession.
The wider Organon account of Cognition, Perception, and Conception
remains to be tested elsewhere.

## 10. Review Status

Reviewed with the kārikā against printed 50.23–51.15. The study
translation states necessary possession and marks the eight supplied
from 2.19; the anchor matches exactly. The two uses of tu, verse and
prose, are distinguished.

Grammar–Logic tests the distributive four, eye-group and gladness
fives, pain-seven, and different cross-verse eights. The brief
second-dhyāna answer is preserved without a latent-state theory.
The opening eight of 2.19 has been reviewed; its eleven and thirteen
remain unreviewed.
