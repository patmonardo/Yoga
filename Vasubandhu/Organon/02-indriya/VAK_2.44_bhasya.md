# VAK_2.44 Bhāṣya — Basis, Loss, and the Return of Consciousness

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> bodhilabhyā muner na prāk catustriṃśatkṣaṇāptitaḥ /
>
> kāmarūpāśraye bhūte nirodhākhyādito nṛṣu // 2.44 //

> For the Sage, the attainment of cessation is acquired with awakening,
> not beforehand, since awakening is attained in thirty-four moments.
> Both attainments arise with a Desire- or Form-Realm Basis.
> The attainment called cessation is first produced among humans.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Is it acquired through application even for the Blessed One?
No: for the Sage it is acquired through awakening. Buddhas
obtain it at the same time as Knowledge of exhaustion.
Nothing of the Buddhas is called application-acquired;
manifestation of all their excellent qualities depends only
on their wish. Thus for them it is acquired through complete
dispassion.

How is the Blessed One established as liberated in both
respects at Knowledge of exhaustion without having produced
cessation attainment? Through mastery over it, as though
it had been produced. Western teachers say the bodhisattva
produces it beforehand while still in training. Why is that
not accepted? Then the elder Upagupta's guiding statement
would also be authoritative: “Having produced cessation
attainment, he produces Knowledge of exhaustion”—this is
to be said of the Tathāgata [quotation's construction incomplete].

“Not beforehand,” say the Kāśmīra teachers: earlier production
is untenable because awakening is attained in thirty-four
moments. The bodhisattva, it is said, attained awakening in
thirty-four consciousness-moments: sixteen for direct realization
of the truths and eighteen for dispassion toward the summit,
through nine uninterrupted and nine liberation paths abandoning
the nine grades of affliction. These make thirty-four.
Since he enters certainty already dispassionate toward
the Essence of nothingness, lower-level afflictions no
longer remain to be abandoned. Dissimilar consciousness
cannot occur in this interval, so cessation attainment
is excluded.

What if dissimilar consciousness were made manifest in
between? There would be an intention to emerge, whereas
bodhisattvas intend not to emerge. True, they intend not
to emerge, but this does not mean non-emergence from
the path [contextual reading of the damaged clause,
discussed in the preceding paired witness]. How, then,
is the pledge understood: “I will not break the cross-legged
posture before attaining exhaustion of *āsrava*”?
The teachers of the outer regions say it means not departing
from this resolve and completing the whole purpose in
one course. The Kāśmīra teachers maintain the former account.

Although the two attainments differ in many ways, both the
non-reflective attainment and cessation attainment arise in the
Desire and Form Realms, with such a Basis present. Those who do
not accept non-reflective attainment in the Form Realm contradict
this treatise passage: “Can there be Form-Realm existence that
is not *pañcavyavacāra* [predicate damaged]? There can: the
existence of Form-Realm beings—reflective gods in a dissimilar
consciousness, those entered into non-reflective or cessation
attainment, and non-reflective gods who have obtained the
non-reflective result.” Thus both attainments have a Desire-
or Form-Realm Basis.

Even here there is a distinction: the attainment called cessation
is first produced among humans, and later in the Form Realm
by those who had previously lost it. Is there loss even from
this attainment? There is, it replies; otherwise the Udāyin
sūtra would be contradicted:

“Friends, a monk is accomplished in conduct, concentration,
and discriminative comprehension. He repeatedly enters and
emerges from cessation of reflection and feeling. He understands
as it is that this possibility exists: if he does not attain
final knowledge through practice in this very life [contextual
reading of the damaged clause], nor at death, then at the
breakup of the body, passing beyond gods who consume food in
morsels, he is reborn in a divine mind-made body. Reborn there,
he repeatedly enters and emerges from cessation of reflection
and feeling, and understands as it is that this possibility exists.”

Here the Blessed One calls the divine mind-made body Form-Realm.
But this attainment belongs to the summit of existence. How
could one who possessed it without having lost it be reborn in
the Form Realm? Another school accepts cessation attainment
also at the level of fourth dhyāna; for them this is possible
even without loss. But this very point is not established—that
it also belongs to fourth dhyāna. Why? Because the sūtra says
“nine successive attainments.” If this is a fixed order, how
can there be attainments out of order? This rule concerns the
beginner. Those who have obtained mastery at will may enter
even by skipping levels.

Thus the two attainments differ by level: fourth dhyāna and
the summit. By application: attention preceded by reflection
on escape and on abiding. By continuum: ordinary and noble
persons' continua. By result: the non-reflective result and
the summit of existence. By maturation: fixed next-rebirth
maturation versus the two possible timings and unfixed maturation.
By first production: in two Realms versus among humans.

Why, when both have cessation of consciousness and mental
factors as their nature, are they called non-reflective attainment
and attainment of cessation of reflection and feeling? Because
entry-practice opposes those named factors. This is like speaking
of Knowledge of another's consciousness even though feeling
and the other factors are also known.

How does consciousness arise again after consciousness has
ceased for a long time? The Vaibhāṣikas accept the past as
immediately preceding Condition, because it too exists. Others
reply: how does Form arise again for those reborn in Form
existence after Form has ceased for a long time [problematic
realm reading retained from the witness]? It arises
from consciousness, not from Form. Likewise consciousness
arises from this body equipped with Faculties, not from
consciousness. Consciousness and the body equipped with
Faculties are seeds of one another, say the earlier teachers.

The venerable Vasumitra says in the *Paripṛcchā*: “This fault
belongs to one whose cessation attainment is without consciousness;
my attainment has consciousness.” The venerable Ghoṣaka says
this is untenable: the Blessed One has said, “With Cognition
present, the meeting of the three is contact; conditioned by
contact are feeling, reflection, and volition.” Thus even
reflection and feeling would not cease here.

Suppose one replies: “It is said that craving is conditioned
by feeling, yet an arhat does not develop craving although
feeling is present. Likewise feeling and the other factors
need not arise even with contact present.” The cases are not
equal, because contact in the production of feeling has not
been qualified that way. Craving is said to arise dependent
on feeling born of contact with ignorance; contact in the
production of feeling is not similarly qualified. Therefore
cessation attainment is without consciousness, say the
Vaibhāṣikas.

How can it be an attainment when without consciousness?
Because it brings the Great Elements into equilibrium, and
because one enters or reaches it through the consciousness
of entry into attainment. Are these two attainments real
entities or designations? “Real entities, because they obstruct
the arising of consciousness.” No: that obstruction is produced
by the entry-consciousness itself. The entry-consciousness
arises opposed to another consciousness, bringing about a
Basis opposed to its arising, so that for an interval there
is merely non-operation of consciousness. That mere non-operation
is designated attainment. It did not exist beforehand and
does not exist afterward once one has emerged, so the
attainment is designated conditioned. Or attainment is
just the bringing of the Basis into that condition.

The non-reflective result should be understood likewise:
one obtains there a consciousness opposed to the operation
of consciousness, and its mere non-operation is designated
the non-reflective result. They do not accept this account.
The two attainments have been explained.

## 4. Movement and Voice

The singular awakening clause continues the preceding debate;
the shared Basis then concerns both attainments. The loss
inference from the sūtra depends on the summit-level account;
the other-school fourth-dhyāna alternative is challenged, not
silently omitted. Six comparisons lead to the naming question,
then competing accounts of resumed consciousness, and finally
entity versus designation. The closing rejection is preserved.

## 5. Basis, Level, Loss, and First Production

A Desire- or Form-Realm Basis does not make cessation a Desire-
or Form-level attainment: the main account assigns it to the
summit. First production of cessation is human; non-reflective
attainment is first produced in the two stated Realms.
Later Form-Realm production after loss is the main account's
reading of the Udāyin passage. The other school's fourth-dhyāna
assignment removes the need for loss but is disputed from the
nine successive attainments. Skipping concerns mastery rather
than the beginner's order, not an agreed reassignment of level.

## 6. Names

Cessation of reflection and feeling is the sūtra name. It does not leave the other mental factors operating. The analogy is Knowledge of another's consciousness.

## 7. Return

Immediately preceding, here, names a Condition. The question
presupposes a long interval. Reciprocal seed is the earlier
teachers' account; consciousness in the attainment is Vasumitra's.
Contact and feeling are Ghoṣaka's objection. The comparison with
an arhat's feeling without craving is answered by distinguishing
the qualified contact-with-ignorance premise from unqualified
contact in the production of feeling. Without consciousness is
the Vaibhāṣika conclusion about the state, not the class-name
of non-reflective beings. The subsequent real-entity/designation
dispute remains distinct.

## 8. Reading Control

The passage asks two separate questions after comparing the two
attainments. First, how can consciousness resume after a long
interval? The Vaibhāṣika appeal to past consciousness, the earlier
teachers' reciprocal-seed account, and Vasumitra's claim that the
attainment retains consciousness are different answers. Ghoṣaka's
contact argument contests Vasumitra, and the Vaibhāṣikas conclude
that cessation attainment lacks consciousness. Second, must the
interval's non-operation be a distinct real entity? The final
designation account challenges that further claim, and the closing
negative reports its rejection. Neither dispute is settled by the
kārikā's shared-Basis clause alone.
