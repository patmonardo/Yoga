# VAK_2.06 Bhāṣya — Pravṛtti, Nivṛtti, and the Limits of Facultyhood

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> pravṛtter āśrayotpattisthitipratyupabhogataḥ /
> caturdaśa tathānyāni nivṛtter indriyāṇi vā // 2.06 //

> Or, fourteen are Faculties with respect to the support, arising,
> persistence, and experience of the ongoing course of existence;
> likewise, the others are faculties with respect to withdrawal.

The Bhāṣya marks this as an alternative opinion and distributes both
groups through the same four functions. “The others” are the remaining
eight. The terms *pravṛtti* and *nivṛtti* are retained in the study
to keep their relation visible.

## 3. Scientific English Rendering

The word “or” indicates a difference of opinion. Others say:
the six Faculties are the support of the active course (*pravṛtti*).
The Female and Male Faculties account for its arising, because it
arises from them. The Life Faculty accounts for its persistence,
because it continues through that faculty. Experience occurs through
the feelings. These, therefore, are the fourteen faculties. In just
the same manner, the others concern withdrawal. For those beginning
with confidence are the supports of withdrawal; the faculty called
“I shall know” is its arising; the knowledge-faculty is its persistence;
and its experience is through the faculty of one who has known.
For this reason there are precisely this many faculties, and for
this same reason they have this order.

Speech, however, is not a Faculty with respect to speaking, because
speaking depends upon particular training. Nor are hand and foot
faculties, because they are not different from taking hold and moving
about. For that very thing, arisen in another manner or in another
place, is called taking hold or moving about. Moreover, snakes and
other such creatures take hold and move about even without hands
and feet. The anus likewise is not a faculty with respect to excretion,
because heavy matter falls wherever there is space, and because wind
propels it. Nor is the genital organ a faculty with respect to
pleasure, for that pleasure is produced by the female and male
faculties. Otherwise the throat, teeth, eyelids, and joints would
also have to be faculties with respect to swallowing, chewing,
opening and closing the eyes, and contracting and extending—or
indeed everything that serves as a cause would be a faculty with
respect to its own activity. Thus it is unreasonable to regard
speech and the others as faculties.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The first sentence explicitly explains *vā* as *matavikalpa*, a
difference of opinion. “Others say” introduces another justification
for the number and order of the twenty-two faculties. It follows
the previous unit's six-function account but should retain its
status as an alternative, rather than being silently merged with it.

The discussion then returns to the proposed additions from VAK 2.05.
It gives particular reasons for excluding speech and the bodily
action-capacities, before generalizing the problem: an unrestricted
criterion would make every cause a faculty of its own activity.

The transition is marked by “speech, however,” not by a new named
speaker. The text allows us to distinguish the reported alternative
and the ensuing rebuttal as argumentative movements. It does not
unambiguously mark where the reported speakers' own speech ends,
or assign either movement to a named school.

## 5. One Fourfold Arrangement, Two Courses

The commentary distributes the same four functions across *pravṛtti*
and *nivṛtti*:

| Function | Pravṛtti: fourteen | Nivṛtti: eight |
|---|---|---|
| Support | Six sensory and Mind Faculties | Five beginning with confidence |
| Arising | Female and Male Faculties | Faculty called “I shall know” |
| Persistence | Life Faculty | Knowledge Faculty |
| Experience | Five feelings | Faculty of one who has known |

The first group totals six, two, one, and five; the second totals
five, one, one, and one. The conclusion concerns both number and
order. The order is explained through the sequence of functions,
not merely repeated as a received list.

The text assigns the Female and Male Faculties to arising because
arising proceeds from them. It does not give a complete account
of generation here. Likewise, life explains continued standing,
while feeling supplies experience. In the second course, the five
support withdrawal and the three realization faculties respectively
explain its arising, persistence, and experience.

This parallels the preceding explanation without erasing its
distinctions. The experience through the faculty of one who has
known can be read alongside VAK 2.04's pleasant liberated abiding.
The present unit itself gives the assignment briefly; it does not
repeat that earlier explanation.

## 6. Why Speech, Hands, and Feet Are Not Added

The argument about speech appeals to dependence on particular
training. This is the reason offered against assigning speech
faculty-status in speaking. It should not become a universal rule
that no cultivated capacity can be a faculty: the same unit includes
the faculties supporting and accomplishing withdrawal.

For hands and feet, the text first invokes *ananyatva*, non-difference
from taking hold and moving about. The following sentence explains
those activities through the same bodily referent arising differently
or elsewhere. The translation retains the compressed “that very
thing”; context identifies the bodily referent, but “material
formation” would be an explanatory supplement, not an expressed noun.

The argument therefore challenges the separation of an operative
member from an independently posited action. A second reason then
addresses necessity: the stated activities occur in creatures such
as snakes without hands or feet. The example is the source's claim;
it does not require us to supply a modern zoological explanation.

## 7. Excretion, Pleasure, and the Expansion to Every Cause

Excretion is explained by the falling of heavy matter and its
propulsion by wind. These are the passage's stated explanatory
factors. Translating them as a modern theory of gravity or physiology
would add a theory the Sanskrit does not articulate.

Pleasure associated with the genital organ is assigned to the female
and male faculties already admitted to the enumeration. This continues
the need to distinguish a bodily designation from the function under
which faculty-status is proposed. It does not exclude the female
and male faculties while retaining them elsewhere in the same list.

The final examples broaden the objection: if performing an activity
were sufficient, throat, teeth, eyelids, and joints would require
additional faculty-status. The argument ends with every cause in
relation to its own activity. This is the threatened expansion of
the category, not a denial that these bodily parts function.

The reasons are therefore heterogeneous: dependence on training,
non-difference from the proposed activity, activity without the
proposed organ, alternative explanation, and an already assigned
faculty-function. The final generalization should preserve those
different grounds rather than replace them with one formula.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for Translation

*Pravṛtti* is rendered “the active course,” and
*nivṛtti* “withdrawal.” Their contextual range is given by the four
functions and their faculty assignments. The latter rendering allows
the path's support, arising, persistence, and experience to remain
intelligible; it does not by itself identify withdrawal with a new
substance or make unconditioned nirvāṇa a produced thing.

*Tathā* carries the fourfold explanation into the second group.
*Vā* marks an alternative opinion. Both particles materially govern
the reading. The Bhāṣya's *prabhava* supplies “arising” for the
first realization faculty, parallel to *utpatti* in the verse.

*Pratyupabhoga* is explained by *upabhoga*, translated “experience.”
“Enjoyment” is also possible, but must not suggest that every feeling
in the first group is pleasurable. Stronger expressions such as
“appropriation of a mode of existence” belong to interpretation.

The claim about *anukrama*, order, remains explicit. The final
*ayuktam* gives the conclusion that the proposed faculty-status
is unreasonable; it does not mean that speech and bodily action
are unreal or impossible.

## 9. Interpretation

The Bhāṣya explicitly marks the fourfold distribution as an
alternative account (*matavikalpa*). It assigns support, arising,
persistence, and experience to both *pravṛtti* and *nivṛtti*, with
different Faculties and counts in each. This parallel arrangement
explains the number and order of the twenty-two; it does not make
the two courses identical or turn their functions into a chronology.

The closing argument further limits faculty designation: a proposed
activity or causal relation does not by itself establish an additional
Faculty. The commentary gives distinct grounds for rejecting speech,
hands and feet, excretion, and pleasure, then warns that an unrestricted
criterion would classify every cause as a Faculty of its activity.
These arguments remain specific to the examples and wording of this
passage.

The project crosswalk with Dhātu 1.48 is reciprocal: Dhātu maps
Faculty-status within the Base–Essence–Principle architecture; this
account arranges the same Faculties by function across two courses.
That is the Organon's comparative framework, not a statement that
the Bhāṣya itself makes.

Across the Kośa, Vijñāna is Discriminative Cognition joining Perception
and Conception without reducing their unity to one homogeneous
operation; it governs Mind and guides the reading of Dharma Base. The
same *avijñapti* remains one content in the Form Base and Dharma Base
classifications, and Vijñāna bears a *prati* relation to it. This
cross-chapter synthesis is an Organon framework, not a claim made by
this Bhāṣya.

In a Kant-informed Techne reading, the four stated functions offer a
rule for determining how this alternative account groups Faculties.
This is a project-level interpretation, not terminology or philosophical
attribution supplied by the source.
