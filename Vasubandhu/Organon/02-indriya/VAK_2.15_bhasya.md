# VAK_2.15 Bhāṣya — Cessation of Faculties at Death

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> nirodhayaty uparamān nārūpye jīvitaṃ manaḥ /
> upekṣāṃ caiva rūpe 'ṣṭau kāme daśa navāṣṭau vā // 2.15 //

> At death in the formless realm, one brings life, mind, and neutral
> feeling to cessation; in the form realm, eight faculties; in the
> desire realm, ten, nine, or eight.

The opening translation follows the commentary's explicit question
about dying. The transmitted division *uparamān nārūpye* is retained
as uncertain rather than silently repaired. The natural death unit
continues with the opening of VAK 2.16:

> kramamṛtyau tu catvāri śubhe sarvatra pañca ca /

> But in gradual death, four; with a wholesome mind, everywhere
> five in addition.

These clauses complete the death counts and are translated here.
The fruit-attainment inquiry in the remainder of VAK 2.16 is reserved
for the next study.

## 3. Scientific English Rendering

Loss must now be explained: when dying in a given realm, how many
faculties does one bring to cessation? “At death in the formless
realm, one brings life, mind, and neutral feeling to cessation;
in the form realm, eight.” One dying in the form realm brings
eight to cessation: those three and the five beginning with the
eye. For all beings there are spontaneously born; they arise and
die with their faculties complete.

“In the desire realm, ten, nine, or eight.” One dying in the desire
realm with both sexual characteristics brings ten faculties to
cessation: those eight together with the female and male faculties.
One with a single sexual characteristic brings nine to cessation;
one without either, eight. This is the rule for death occurring
all at once. “But in gradual death, four”: one dying gradually
brings four faculties to cessation—body, life, mind, and neutral
feeling—for these do not cease separately. This rule is to be
understood of dying with an afflicted or ethically indeterminate mind.

When, however, one dies with a wholesome mind, then “everywhere
five in addition.” In every case, one dying with a wholesome mind
brings to cessation the faculties already stated, with the five
beginning with confidence in addition; for these necessarily occur
in a wholesome mind. Thus in the formless realm one brings eight
to cessation, and in the form realm thirteen. The remaining counts
are to be worked out in detail in this way.

## 4. Movement and Voice of the Commentary

The transition from acquisition to loss introduces a question about
death. The prose explains the realm counts, qualifies the desire-
realm count by simultaneous versus gradual dying, then qualifies
the whole account by the ethical condition of the terminal mind.
There is no opponent or named school in this unit.

The last qualification is essential to the scope of the earlier
numbers. Stopping at the end of the numbered verse would leave
their conditions unstated. The following move to fruit-attainment
marks a new inquiry rather than another qualification of death.

## 5. Realm and Mode of Dying

The initial formless group is life, mind, and neutral feeling.
The form-realm group adds the five sensory faculties, giving eight.
For the desire-realm case in which death occurs all at once, the
presence of neither, one, or both sexual faculties gives eight,
nine, or ten respectively.

The form-realm explanation invokes spontaneous birth with complete
faculties. Its claim about completeness is stated in that local
context. It should not be expanded into an unqualified account of
every possible bodily history merely from the phrase “all beings.”

In gradual death, the final four are body, life, mind, and neutral
feeling. “These do not cease separately” concerns their cessation
in the stated terminal context. It does not establish that the
four are one substance or that every change in one at any time
must be a simultaneous change in the others.

## 6. The Wholesome-Mind Addition

The earlier rule applies to afflicted or ethically indeterminate
terminal mind. With wholesome mind, the five beginning with confidence
are added because the text asserts their necessary presence in
that mind. These are confidence, vigor, recollection, concentration,
and prajñā.

| Case | Earlier count | With wholesome terminal mind |
|---|---:|---:|
| Formless realm | 3 | 8 |
| Form realm | 8 | 13 |
| Desire realm, all at once, both sexual faculties | 10 | 15 |
| Desire realm, all at once, one sexual faculty | 9 | 14 |
| Desire realm, all at once, neither sexual faculty | 8 | 13 |
| Gradual death | 4 | 9 |

The prose explicitly gives eight and thirteen for the first two
rows. The remaining wholesome counts are arithmetic applications
of its instruction to add five in every case. The table makes
that derivation visible without presenting the derived numbers
as individually recited by the Sanskrit.

## 7. Cessation Here and Abandonment Earlier

The wholesome five cease at death in the stated case. Their cessation
does not make them unwholesome or establish that they are objects
of path-abandonment. VAK 2.13 classified what is to be abandoned;
the present passage counts the cessation of faculties at death.
The two questions must retain their distinct predicates.

Nor is this a simple reversal of VAK 2.14's acquisition count.
That inquiry restricted its numbers to initial acquisition as
maturation. The present inquiry includes mind and neutral feeling
and, in the wholesome case, the five beginning with confidence. A shared
realm does not make those differently qualified inventories identical.

The text describes the termination of the present life in faculty
terms. It does not assert the cessation of every possible future
continuation merely by using *nirodha* here.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for Translation

*Tyāga* is rendered “loss” in this context. *Nirodhayati* literally
has causative form, “brings to cessation,” but its grammatical
subject is the dying person; that form does not by itself imply
a deliberate act. “The faculties cease at death” is a readable
study paraphrase of the same account.

*Sakṛnmaraṇa* and *kramamṛtyu* distinguish death all at once and
gradual death. The latter belongs to the opening of VAK 2.16,
although both research Bhāṣya files include its explanation.
The cross-verse continuation is marked explicitly here.

*Śubhe* is explained by *kuśale cetasi*, with a wholesome mind.
*Pañca ca* means five in addition, as the prose's *pañcādhikāni*
confirms. *Avyākṛta* is ethical indeterminacy, whereas *upekṣā*
is the neutral-feeling faculty; their different senses of neutrality
remain visible in the translation.

## 9. Interpretation

The counts depend on realm, the stated bodily configuration, mode
of dying, and quality of the terminal mind. Simultaneous death is
the scope of the verse's realm counts; the gradual-death cluster and
the five-Faculty addition with a wholesome mind are supplied at the
opening of VAK 2.16. Cessation at death, initial maturation-acquisition,
and path-abandonment are distinct classifications.

The project crosswalk with Dhātu 1.48 relates these terminal sets
to the Base–Essence–Principle map without replacing or relocating
its Faculty-bearing loci. The terminal count concerns which Faculties
cease under the stated conditions.

The Kośa's governing synthesis: Vijñāna is Discriminative Cognition joining
and governing Perception and Conception. Their unity is Inconceivable as a
homogeneous operation; its Idea is disclosed in the Cognition Base. Vijñāna
governs Mind and guides the reading of Dharma Base. *Avijñapti* is the
bridge, one Dharma classified in both Form Base and Dharma Base; Vijñāna
bears a *prati* relation to it.

In a Kant-informed Techne reading, realm, possession, death-mode,
and terminal ethical condition jointly delimit the relevant count.
This is a project-level interpretation, not a universal account of
death or terminology supplied by the source.
