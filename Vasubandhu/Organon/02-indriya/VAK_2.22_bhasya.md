## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

The following is now considered: do these conditioned dharmas,
just as they have distinct characteristics, also arise separately,
or are there some that necessarily arise together? There are,
he says. All these dharmas fall into five classes: material form,
mind, mental factors, formations dissociated from mind, and the
unconditioned. Of these, the unconditioned does not arise at all.
For material dharmas, however, the following rule applies.

“In the desire realm, a soundless minimal aggregate without a
faculty consists of eight constituents.” The most subtle aggregate
of material form is called a *paramāṇu*, beyond which a further
[smaller aggregate] could not be discerned. In the desire realm,
when without sound and without a faculty, it arises with eight
constituents, lacking none of them. The eight are the four great
elements and four derived forms: visible form, odor, taste, and
the tangible. A soundless minimal aggregate possessing a faculty,
however, arises with nine or ten constituents. First, “one possessing
the body faculty has nine constituents.” It is called body-faculty-
bearing because the body faculty is present in it. Its nine are
those eight and the body faculty. “One possessing another faculty
has ten constituents.” Where another faculty is present in the
minimal aggregate, there are ten: those same nine and one among
the eye, ear, nose, and tongue faculties. When these aggregates
arise with sound, they respectively arise with nine, ten, and
eleven constituents. For there is also sound inseparable from a
faculty, caused by appropriated great elements.

If the great elements are inseparable here, how does one aggregate
arise as hard, another as fluid, hot, or moving? Whichever is most
intense in a given aggregate, standing out through its efficacy,
is apprehended there—as with the touch of a bundle of needles
[the transmitted compound is uncertain], or the taste of flour
mixed with powdered salt. But how is the existence of the remaining
elements in these aggregates known? From their activities: holding
together, sustaining, ripening, and spreading apart; and because,
when the conditions are available, hard things and the like become
fluid and so forth. Others say that heat in water is known from
the greater degree of cold. But a greater degree of cold could
occur even without admixture [of heat], just as there can be
greater intensity of sound or feeling. Others say that the elements
are present in those aggregates as seeds, not in their own actual
forms, on the authority of the statement, “There are various
elements in this log of wood.”

How, then, is visible color present in wind? This matter is to be
accepted on trust, not inferred. [An inference is nevertheless
offered:] because odor is apprehended from moving [air], and odor
does not occur without visible color. The absence of odor and
taste in the form realm has already been stated. Accordingly,
the minimal aggregates there have six, seven, or eight constituents;
since this has thereby already been indicated, it is not stated
again.

Now, is “constituent” here taken as an individual real constituent,
or as a sense-base? What follows from that? If it means an individual
real constituent, saying “eight constituents,” “nine,” or “ten”
is too little. For there must also be shape in that material
[the wording is uncertain], since shape too is an accumulation
of minimal particles; and either heaviness or lightness, either
smoothness or roughness, and sometimes cold, hunger, and thirst.
But if a sense-base is taken as a constituent, saying “eight
constituents” is too much: four should be stated, since the great
elements also belong to the tangible sense-base. Here, in some
cases an individual real constituent is counted—what serves as
support; in other cases a sense-base is counted—what is supported.
Even so, the elemental constituents would be more numerous,
since each derived form depends on its own quartet of great
elements. Here, however, a constituent is counted by kind: the
other quartets of great elements do not go beyond their respective
kinds. But why the effort to speak with such distinctions? Words
may indeed be used as one chooses; it is the meaning that must
be examined.

The bracketed expressions mark interpretive supplements, not missing
Sanskrit supplied as a new witness. The wind exchange is especially
compressed: the source places a denial of inference immediately
before what reads as an inference. The translation preserves that
tension rather than silently removing the denial or assigning
unmarked positions to named schools.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

This is a new inquiry: distinct characteristics need not imply
separate arising. The fivefold classification locates its scope;
the unconditioned is mentioned precisely to exclude it from arising.
The material case then supplies the first rule of necessary
co-arising, followed by objections to its elemental composition
and to the units used in its counts.

The running source continues far beyond the immediate research
Bhāṣya, which ends after the sound-bearing variants. The present
study restores the questions about elemental predominance, the
presence of nonmanifest elements, wind and visible color, the
form realm, and the meaning of *dravya*.

Two positions are expressly introduced as those of “others”:
the argument from degrees of cold and the account of presence
as seeds. No school is named locally. Questions and replies
should not be collapsed into a single unqualified assertion by
Vasubandhu; the closing challenge also makes the method of counting
an object of examination.

## 5. The Material Counts and Their Scope

| Desire-realm aggregate | Without sound | With sound |
|---|---:|---:|
| Without a faculty | 8 | 9 |
| With the body faculty | 9 | 10 |
| With one of eye, ear, nose, or tongue, in addition to body | 10 | 11 |

The eight comprise the four great elements and visible form,
odor, taste, and derived tangible form. The tangible in this
initial list cannot mean the whole tangible sense-base, since
that would include the four great elements already counted
separately. This distinction becomes explicit in the later objection.

The body faculty adds one; one of the four further faculties
adds another. “Another faculty” is locally specified by the prose
and should not be extended indiscriminately to all twenty-two
faculties. Sound adds one to each applicable case. The statement
about sound caused by appropriated great elements explains its
possible conjunction with a faculty-bearing aggregate; it does
not make every such aggregate continuously sound-producing.

The form-realm totals six, seven, and eight follow by removing
odor and taste from the corresponding soundless cases. The source
does not repeat an entire second table. The arithmetic depends
on the realm qualification, not on a universal eightfold material
minimum applying everywhere.

## 6. Elemental Presence, Predominance, and Seeds

The predominance question distinguishes what is apprehended from
what the account says is present. Hardness does not by itself
exclude the other great elements: the response appeals to relative
efficacy. The next question then asks for grounds of their presence,
answered through characteristic activities and transformations
when conditions become available.

The coldness argument is a separate proposal, followed by a
counterargument: differences in intensity need not result from
mixture with an opposite. The analogy with sound and feeling
belongs to that counterargument, not to the original proof of heat.

The seed account makes a further distinction. Presence as a seed
is expressly contrasted with presence in actual form, *svarūpataḥ*.
It must not be paraphrased as simply the same claim of simultaneous
actual existence in different language. The cited statement about
the log of wood supports this alternative in the passage; the
local text does not identify its scriptural source more precisely.

## 7. What Does the Number Count?

The final debate qualifies the apparent simplicity of the initial
counts. Counting every individual material determination would
require more than eight; counting only sense-bases would yield
four in the facultyless, soundless desire-realm case: visible form,
odor, taste, and the tangible, with the elements included under
the last.

The reply uses different counting levels for support and supported:
the great elements are distinguished as real constituents, while
the derived forms are grouped by sense-base. The further objection
then notes that each derived form has its own elemental quartet.
The answer counts those elemental constituents by kind,
*jātidravya*, so repeated quartets do not multiply the four kinds.

Consequently, “eight” is not an unqualified total of eight individual
particles. The passage itself examines the counting convention.
The closing insistence that meaning must be examined prevents
terminological choice from settling the substantive question by
itself.

## 8. Philological and Translation Decisions

*Paramāṇu* is locally defined as the most subtle *rūpasaṃghāta*,
material aggregate. The clause *yato nānyataro vijñāyeta* is terse;
“beyond which a further smaller aggregate could not be discerned”
is a contextual construal. Neither this clause nor the numerical
scheme should be recast as a claim of modern particle physics.

The transcription contains defects beyond spacing. Among them are
*kāma ghātāvaśabdako*, *aṣṭo dravyāṇi*, *saṃgrahaghṛti*,
*avyati bhede*, *ayivahṇidam*, and *kaḥ punaryatsa*. The English
contextually reads desire-realm location, eight constituents,
sustaining, absence of admixture, excessive counting, and a question
about the effort of making distinctions. These resolutions are
not presented as established Sanskrit emendations.

The needle-example compound *sūcītūnī kalāpasparśavat* and the
shape clause *taddravyasaṃsthānenāpi* remain less secure. Their
local argumentative functions are intelligible—predominance in
one case, additional material determinations in the other—but
the precise wording requires collation. The continuous Sanskrit
retains the available transcription so that those limits remain
reviewable.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The shift from possession to co-arising changes the relation being
examined. The preceding studies counted faculties possessed by
a continuum; this one investigates constraints on the arising
of material dharmas. A necessary co-possession rule cannot simply
be substituted for a necessary co-arising rule.

For Organon interpretation, the full commentary yields more than
an inventory of mandatory components. It distinguishes analytical
character, occurrence together, perceptible predominance, causal
activity, and presence as a seed. These determinations must remain
separate if the competing explanations are to remain intelligible.

The counting dispute adds a methodological requirement: identify
the unit of enumeration. Individual constituents, sense-bases,
and kinds do different work. An apparently precise number loses
its meaning if these levels are silently interchanged. The final
appeal to examining meaning is therefore continuous with the
argument, not merely a general maxim appended to it.
