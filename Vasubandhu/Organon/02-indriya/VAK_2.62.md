## 4. Grammar

The first four tokens define the immediately antecedent condition as arisen consciousness and mental factors that are not final. The next three define the object condition as all dharmas. The final three state that what is named the conditioning cause is held to be the predominant condition. The grammar keeps the three conditions distinct; the whole-complex reading, the exclusion of own-kind succession, the “as appropriate” range of object, and the identity with the conditioning cause are prose.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> Arisen consciousness and mental factors, other than the final, are
> immediately antecedent. All dharmas are object-conditions; what is named
> the conditioning cause is held to be predominant.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> Arisen consciousness and mental factors, except the final ones, are the
> immediately antecedent condition. All dharmas are object-conditions.
> What is called the conditioning cause is held to be the predominant
> condition.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> The verse defines the remaining three conditions. The Bhāṣya reads the antecedent as the whole preceding complex of the next whole complex (not own-kind succession), restricts object to the appropriate range, and identifies predominant with the conditioning cause.

## 9. Interpretive Note

**Translation note.** The Bhāṣya excludes an arhat's final consciousness and factors and treats the whole preceding complex as condition for the next whole complex. Object range is “as appropriate”: visible Form for eye-cognition and all dharmas for mental cognition. A question about knowing a future after a future is raised but not settled.

The verse completes the naming of the four conditions. The next verse turns to the timing of their function.
