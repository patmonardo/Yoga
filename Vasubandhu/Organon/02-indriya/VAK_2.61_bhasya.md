# VAK_2.61 Bhāṣya — Opening the Four Conditions

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> catvāraḥ pratyayā uktā hetvākhyaḥ pañca hetavaḥ // 2.61 //

> [The foregoing concern] mind and mental factors. The others also
> [arise] likewise, excluding associated cause. Four conditions are

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

What are the conditions? “Four conditions are taught.” Where are they
taught? In the sūtra: “There are four kinds of condition: cause-condition,
immediately antecedent condition, object-condition, and predominant
condition.” “Conditionality” means a kind of condition. Of these,
“the condition named ‘cause’ comprises five causes.” The five causes
apart from the conditioning cause constitute cause-condition.

## 4. What the Definition Establishes

The local gloss *pratyayajātiḥ pratyayatā* explains the abstract noun
*pratyayatā* through a kind or class of condition. Rendering the sūtra's
plural as “four kinds of condition” makes that explanation readable
without turning conditionality into an additional entity.

The five members of cause-condition are recovered from the six-cause
account already completed:

| Included cause | Sanskrit |
|---|---|
| Co-arisen | sahabhūhetu |
| Homogeneous | sabhāgahetu |
| Associated | saṃprayuktakahetu |
| Pervasive | sarvatragahetu |
| Maturation | vipākahetu |

The excluded sixth is *kāraṇahetu*, rendered “conditioning cause” in
this sequence. The prose explicitly names that exclusion. It is not
the associated cause excluded for material and dissociated dharmas in
the preceding unit. Those two exclusions answer different questions:
which causal relations apply to a given class of dharma, and which
cause-types are grouped under cause-condition.

The five are types within the definition. This does not assert that
all five operate in every instance: the differentiated causal profiles
of 2.60 remain in force. Nor are there five separate cause-conditions
in addition to the four conditions announced here.

## 5. Textual and Translation Decisions

The continuous Sanskrit retains the local transcription's *kvavoktāḥ*
and *paṇyca*. The English construes these as “where are [they] taught?”
and “five,” respectively; no silently corrected Sanskrit or independent
critical edition is claimed.

*Hetvākhyaḥ* is singular, qualifying an understood *pratyayaḥ*;
*pañca hetavaḥ* is its plural nominal predicate. The definition groups
five causes under one condition-type. “The condition named ‘cause’”
keeps that syntax visible.

The research Bhāṣya correctly identifies the excluded *kāraṇahetu*,
but incorrectly describes only the conditions half as the actual verse.
It also extends its translation into the subsequent discussion of
immediate antecedence and objects. Here the Sanskrit and translation
stop together at 98.08. That later material belongs to the next natural
unit and will be checked there against the running source.

The sūtra is cited without a title in this passage; none is supplied
editorially. The questions belong to the commentary's expository
movement. They do not, by themselves, establish a dispute between
named schools. The scriptural quotation is kept explicit within that
movement.

## 6. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage connects two classifications by stating their relation:
five of the six causes constitute one of the four conditions. Thus
“cause” and “condition” are neither interchangeable inventories nor
two wholly separate domains. Their relation has to be learned from
the specified grouping and exclusion.

As Organon interpretation, this is a useful discipline for the inquiry
into grounds and conditions. The earlier distinctions are carried
forward through an explicit relation rather than discarded. But the
short definition does not yet establish a synthesis of subjectivity
and objectivity, or a division into quantitative causes and qualitative
conditions. The other three conditions still require their own
explanations. What this unit teaches is exactly where the preceding
cause account enters the new classification.
