# VAK_2.41_bhasya

## 1. Text and Scope

This study covers the Bhāṣya at 67.13–68.27. It first examines whether
class-commonality is a distinct real Dharma, then defines the non-percipient
condition as a maturation-result among certain Bṛhatphala beings.

## 3. Scientific English Rendering

> What is class-commonality? “Class-commonality is sameness among sentient
> beings.” The Vaibhāṣikas say that it is a real Dharma, the resemblance
> among sentient beings, called common membership in a group. It is
> undivided as commonality among sentient beings and differentiated according
> to Principle-range, ground, destiny, birth mode, species, sex, discipline,
> trainee status, and so forth.
>
> If commonality of beings were not one undivided real Dharma, they say,
> there would be no undivided cognition or designation “sentient being”
> among beings differentiated from one another. The same argument applies
> to the classifications of Bases, Essences, and Principles.
>
> The response asks what work an additional ordinary-person commonality does
> beyond ordinary-person status; why commonality is not accepted for rice,
> barley, beans, fruit, iron, and gold that resemble their own kinds; and
> how different commonalities are all designated “commonality.” The
> Vaiśeṣika universal is compared. The Vaibhāṣikas cite a sūtra using
> “commonality with human beings.” The reply grants the expression but not
> a distinct entity: the relevant conditioned formations are simply such
> that “human” and similar classifications apply to them. The alternative is
> not further explained.
>
> What is the non-percipient condition? “Among non-percipient beings, it
> is cessation of consciousness and associated mental factors.” In certain
> Bṛhatphala beings, this is a real Dharma through which future
> consciousness and associated factors are prevented from arising for an
> interval, like river water held back by a dam. It is exclusively the
> maturation-result of the non-percipient attainment. Consciousness occurs
> at rebirth and departure. After a long interval, recognition arises and
> the beings depart, as if waking from a long sleep; they are reborn only
> in the Desire Principle because the force of the former attainment is
> exhausted and no new force has been accumulated.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The first half gives the Vaibhāṣika account of commonality, distinguishes
undivided and differentiated forms, and tests it through classification,
duplication, non-sentient resemblance, comparison with the Vaiśeṣika
universal, and the scope of scriptural wording. The answer gives an
alternative account but expressly does not develop it.

The second half changes topic. It identifies a conditioned result of the
non-percipient attainment, gives its causal source, cosmic location, and
boundary events, then explains its departure through exhaustion of the
prior attainment's formative force.

## 5. The Commonality Dispute

The Vaibhāṣika claim explains common cognition through a real common Dharma:

```text
same class cognition
    → one commonality present in each relevant sentient continuum
```

The objections are not merely nominal. They ask whether the added entity
duplicates ordinary-person status, whether it has any discernible operation,
why similarity among non-sentient kinds does not require the same entity,
and how a single class-name covers distinct commonality entities.

The sūtra establishes that a classification is used; it does not by itself
establish another real Dharma. The reply's positive alternative is modest:
the already conditioned formations can be such that a classification applies.
Its mechanism is not supplied and must not be invented.

## 6. The Non-Percipient Maturation-Result

The non-percipient condition has a different argumentative role. It is not
the proposed ground of a shared class, but a maturation-result that prevents
future consciousness and associated factors from arising for an interval.
The river-dam simile specifies prevention of arising rather than destruction
of all conditions.

The Bhāṣya limits the condition to some Bṛhatphala beings in a distinct
region. It explicitly allows consciousness at entry and departure, so
“non-percipient” cannot be expanded into a claim of a timeless or absolute
absence of all cognition. The subsequent Desire-Principle rebirth follows
the exhaustion of the former attainment's formative force.

## 7. Interpretation

The two topics clarify different relations at the Hub. Commonality asks
whether a graph classification requires an extra universal node. The
Bhāṣya's critique allows common classification to be grounded in the
relation of already specified formations without multiplying another real
Dharma.

The non-percipient condition instead specifies a causal restriction inside
the Impure Wheel. It is a finite maturation-result whose force prevents
future cognitive operation for an interval and then exhausts. It is not
the Hub, not liberation, and not the unitary Vijñāna that governs
Perception and Conception.

The Kośa-wide synthesis remains: Vijñāna is Discriminative Cognition joining
and governing Perception and Conception; their unity is Inconceivable as
homogeneous operation, and its Idea is disclosed in the Cognition Base.
Vijñāna governs Mind and guides Dharma Base. *Avijñapti* is the same Dharma
classified in Form Base and Dharma Base, with Vijñāna bearing a *prati*
relation to it. These system-level relations remain distinct from the
conditioned interruption discussed locally.

## 8. Review Status

The kārikā and Bhāṣya have been compared with the source transcription at
67.13–68.27. The Vaibhāṣika claim, its differentiated forms, all central
objections, the limited alternative account, the non-percipient maturation
condition, the river-dam simile, Bṛhatphala placement, boundary cognition,
and Desire-Principle rebirth are retained. VAK 2.42 is unchanged.
