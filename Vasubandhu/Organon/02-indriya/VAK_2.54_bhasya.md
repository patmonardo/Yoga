# VAK_2.54 Bhāṣya — Pervasive Cause and Karmic Maturation

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> sarvatragākhyaḥ kliṣṭānāṃ svabhūmau pūrvasarvagāḥ /
> vipākahetur aśubhāḥ kuśalāś caiva sāsravāḥ // 2.54 //

> The earlier pervasive dharmas on their own level are the cause
> called pervasive of afflicted dharmas there. Unwholesome dharmas
> and wholesome dharmas with influxes are maturation causes.

The continuous unit gives both causal accounts, ending with maturation's
dependence on a continuing series. The temporal classification of the
six causes begins the next study.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

What is the pervasive cause? “The earlier pervasive dharmas on their
own level are the cause called pervasive of afflicted dharmas there.”
Previously arisen pervasive dharmas belonging to a given level are
pervasive causes of later afflicted dharmas on that level. We shall
explain those pervasive dharmas later, in the chapter on the latent
tendencies. This cause is established separately from the homogeneous
cause because it is a common cause of afflicted dharmas: it also causes
those belonging to another class, since afflictions of other classes
arise through its influence.

Do even the afflicted dharmas of a noble person have pervasive causes?
The Kāśmīras say that all afflicted dharmas have causes belonging to
what is abandoned through seeing. Accordingly, the Prakaraṇa texts say:
“Which dharmas have causes abandoned through seeing? Afflicted dharmas
and the maturation of dharmas abandoned through seeing.” They also say:
“Which dharmas have indeterminate causes? Conditioned indeterminate
dharmas and unwholesome dharmas.” And the truth-of-suffering discussion
is given at length: “There can be something belonging to the truth of
suffering that has identity-view as a cause but is not a cause of
identity-view,” through the passage excepting arising, aging, persistence,
and impermanence and referring to the remaining afflicted truth of
suffering [the transmitted quotation is compressed and irregular].

How, then, is this statement of the Prajñapti commentary interpreted:
“Can an unwholesome dharma have only an unwholesome cause? Yes: the first
afflicted volition that a noble person manifests when falling away from
dispassion toward the desire realm”? This is said with reference to
an unabandoned cause. Its cause belonging to what is abandoned through
seeing is not mentioned because that cause has been abandoned.
The pervasive cause has been explained.

What is the maturation cause? “Unwholesome dharmas and wholesome dharmas
with influxes are maturation causes.” Unwholesome and contaminated
wholesome dharmas are maturation causes because they have the nature
of maturation. Why do indeterminate dharmas not produce maturation?
Because they are weak, like rotten seeds. Why do uncontaminated dharmas
not do so? Because they are not moistened by craving, like sound seeds
that have not been moistened. Being unaffiliated with a realm, what
realm-affiliated maturation could they produce? The remaining dharmas
produce it because they possess both features, like sound, moistened
seeds.

How should “maturation cause” be understood: a cause of maturation,
or a cause that is itself maturation? What follows? If it is a cause
of maturation, the expression “the eye born of maturation” would not
fit. If it is maturation itself that is the cause, “the maturation of
karma” would not fit. There is no fault: it was explained earlier that
the expression can be construed in both ways.

What does “maturation” mean? A dissimilar ripening is maturation.
The Vaibhāṣikas say that the other causes have similar ripening, while
one has both kinds. But “ripening” is not appropriate for those others.
Ripening is the culmination in a result born of a specific transformation
of a continuum. The co-arisen and associated causes do not have results
born of such a transformation. Nor do the homogeneous cause and the
others have a termination in a result, since they repeatedly produce
wholesome and other results throughout saṃsāra.

In the desire realm, a maturation cause consisting of one aggregate
has one result: acquisition and its arising and other marks [contextual
reading]. One consisting of two aggregates has one result: bodily or
verbal action and its arising and other marks. One consisting of four
aggregates has one result: wholesome or unwholesome mind and mental
factors together with arising and the other marks.

In the form realm, a maturation cause consisting of one aggregate has
one result: acquisition or non-percipient attainment together with
arising and the other marks. One consisting of two aggregates has one
result: manifest action in the first concentration together with arising
and the other marks. One consisting of four aggregates has one result
when wholesome mind is unconcentrated; one consisting of five aggregates
has one result when it is concentrated.

In the formless realm, a maturation cause consisting of one aggregate
has one result: acquisition or cessation-attainment together with arising
and the other marks. One consisting of four aggregates consists of
wholesome mind and mental factors and their arising and other marks.

There is karma whose maturation ripens as only one sense-base: the
dharma-base, namely the life faculty. Where its maturation includes
the mind-base, it includes two: mind and dharma. Likewise for the
tangible-base. Where it includes the body-base, it includes three:
body, tangible, and dharma. Likewise for visible form, smell, and taste
[each with tangible and dharma]. Where it includes the eye-base, it
includes four: eye, body, tangible, and dharma. Likewise for ear, nose,
and tongue. There is karma whose maturation ripens as five, six,
seven, eight, nine, ten, or eleven sense-bases. For karma has varied
or unvaried results, like external seeds: some yield varied results,
as with lotus, pomegranate, and banyan; others unvaried results,
as with barley and wheat.

Karma belonging to one temporal period has maturation belonging to
three temporal periods. But karma belonging even to two temporal
periods does not have maturation belonging to just one, lest the
result be excessively small relative to its cause. Likewise, karma
of a single moment has maturation extending over many moments, but
not conversely [provisional reading of a damaged sentence]. Maturation
does not ripen simultaneously with karma, nor immediately afterward,
since the immediately succeeding moment is drawn forth by the
immediately antecedent condition. The maturation cause depends upon
a continuing series.

## 4. Pervasiveness Has a Determinate Range

The pervasive cause crosses classes of abandonment while remaining
restricted to its own level and to prior arising. “Pervasive” therefore
does not mean unrestricted influence on every dharma. Its detailed
membership is expressly deferred to the chapter on latent tendencies.
The present account does not turn every affliction into a pervasive cause.

The noble-person objection distinguishes having an abandoned cause
from presently possessing that cause unabandoned. The Kāśmīra reply
interprets the cited “only” as restricted to unabandoned causes. It
does not say that the noble person has reacquired the views abandoned
through seeing. This is a reported doctrinal resolution, not an
unqualified assertion that abandonment leaves every causal relation
operative in the same manner.

## 5. Maturation and the Limits of Ripening

The seed comparisons distinguish weakness from lack of moistening by
craving. Exclusion from maturation causality does not mean exclusion
from all causality: uncontaminated path dharmas already entered the
homogeneous-cause discussion. Nor does the contaminated wholesome
category mean that every wholesome moment is simultaneously accompanied
by craving.

The Vaibhāṣika explanation extends “ripening” to other causes, with one
cause admitting both similar and dissimilar results. The broad
conditioning cause is the contextual referent of that unnamed “one.”
The following criticism restricts ripening to a result culminating
through transformation of a continuum. These positions must remain
distinct. A culmination of this causal process is not a claim that
the resulting dharma has no further effects or that saṃsāra ends there.

## 6. Aggregate and Sense-Base Counts

The aggregate counts describe the constitution of a cause-complex
having one result; they are not counts of aggregates in that result.
Acquisition and the marks fall within the formations aggregate, while
bodily or verbal action adds material form. The five-aggregate case
of concentrated wholesome mind in the form realm includes the material
component of meditative restraint; this explains the contrast with
the four-aggregate unconcentrated case.

The sense-base paragraph instead counts the constitution of maturation.
The life faculty belongs to the dharma-base; it is not itself an
additional sense-base. The tangible-base belongs with dharma in the
two-base case. Body, visible form, smell, and taste occur in the
three-base pattern, while each of the four remaining sensory faculties
occurs in the four-base pattern. These elliptical extensions are
expanded in the English. The research translation incorrectly groups
the tangible-base with the three-base case and omits the visible-form,
smell, and taste extension.

## 7. The Temporal Conclusion

The source adds a temporal paragraph omitted from the research file.
It contrasts the temporal extent of karma with that of its maturation,
then explicitly excludes simultaneous and immediately subsequent
maturation. The reason given refers to the immediately antecedent
condition and to dependence upon a continuing series.

The three-period statement uses the technical past/present/future
classification; it should not be converted into a claim that a karmic
act produces a result before that act occurs. The one-moment/many-moment
sentence is textually damaged and remains provisional. These temporal
claims close the present maturation discussion; the next question asks
for the temporal classification of all the causes.

## 8. Textual and Translation Decisions

The Sanskrit covers 89.01–90.22. The next unit begins at 90.23 with
the temporal classification of causes in VAK 2.55. The full pervasive
cause discussion, previously placed in the research 2.53 file, is
restored here, and the omitted ending of maturation is included.

The transcription is retained apart from source labels and surrounding
spacing. The abbreviated identity-view quotation at 89.10–13 is
irregular; the English gives its expressed scope without inventing a
complete quotation. *Sarvadagahetuḥ*, *klilṣṭāñ*, and *vīpāka* are among
the retained transcription forms.

At 90.04 the witness has *pratyayaḥ*, “condition,” where the parallel
one-aggregate cases suggest *prāptiḥ*, acquisition. The translation
uses “acquisition” explicitly as a contextual repair requiring collation.
The research file's unqualified “a condition” conceals this difficulty.

At 90.20–21, *ekamekakṣanikasya bahukṣāṇiko nanu viparyayāt* is rendered
provisionally as the one-moment/many-moment contrast, not treated as
secure wording. At 90.21–22 the exclusion of simultaneous or immediately
subsequent maturation is explicit. The two constructions of *vipākahetu*
are translated as a linguistic dispute; neither is silently discarded
in favor of a modern uniform use of “cause.”

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

As Organon interpretation, the two causes require different questions.
Pervasive causality asks how an earlier affliction conditions later
afflicted dharmas across classes. Maturation asks how an ethically
qualified cause culminates in a dissimilar result through a continuing
series. Neither relation is exhausted by similarity of cause and result.

The exposition also teaches us to distinguish causal ancestry,
unabandoned possession, composition of a causal complex, and composition
of its result. These distinctions make the inquiry into grounds and
conditions more determinate. “Transformation” alone would not supply
that knowledge; the particular relation and its limits must be learned.
