# VAK_2.59 Bhāṣya — Taking and Giving Results

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> vartamānāḥ phalaṃ pañca gṛhṇanti dvau prayacchataḥ /
> vartamānābhyatītau dvau eko 'tītaḥ prayacchati // 2.59 //

> Five take a result when present; two give it then.
> Two give it when present or past; one gives it when past.

The continuous unit explains the temporal operations, their fourfold
cases, and an alternative result-classification before closing the
account of causes and results.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Which of these causes takes a result at which time, and which gives it?
“Five take a result when present.” Not when past, because they have
already taken it; nor when future, because they lack operative activity.
The conditioning cause is also like this, but it does not necessarily
have a result, so it is not mentioned.

“Two give it.” The co-arisen and associated causes give their result
when present. Their taking and giving of a result occur at the same
time. “Two, when present or past”: the homogeneous and pervasive causes
give a result. That they do so when past is understandable. But how
do they give a homogeneous-outflow result when present? Through bringing
it about immediately afterward. When the result has come about, those
two causes are past, and the result has already been given; they do
not give that same result again.

A fourfold analysis asks whether a wholesome homogeneous cause takes
a result without giving one. The first case is the acquisitions abandoned
last when one cuts off wholesome roots. The second is the acquisitions
obtained first when one reconnects wholesome roots. But it should be
stated this way: those very [previous acquisitions] when one reconnects.
The third case is the remaining states of one whose wholesome roots
have not been cut off. The fourth excludes these cases.

For an unwholesome cause, the first case is the acquisitions abandoned
last when one attains dispassion toward the desire realm. The second
is those obtained first when one falls from that dispassion. But it
should be stated this way: those very [previous acquisitions] when
one falls. The third is the remaining states of one not free from
passion toward the desire realm. The fourth excludes these cases.
For the obscured-indeterminate, the account should similarly be applied,
as appropriate, through the attainment of and decline from arhatship.

For the unobscured-indeterminate, [the transmitted introductory phrase
is unclear]: whatever gives a result also takes one. There can be
something that takes without giving: an arhat's final aggregates.

Restricting the account to what has an object, a moment-by-moment
fourfold analysis asks whether a wholesome homogeneous cause takes
a result without giving one. The first case is when an afflicted or
indeterminate mind becomes manifest immediately after a wholesome mind.
The second reverses this. The third is a wholesome mind immediately
after a wholesome mind. The fourth excludes these cases. Unwholesome
and other cases should be applied similarly.

How is a result taken? Through assuming the state of a seed for it.
“One gives when past.” The maturation cause gives its result only
when past, because maturation is neither simultaneous nor immediately
subsequent.

Others speak of four kinds of result: a result of support, as the
water-circle is a result of the wind-circle, and so on through grasses
and the like as results of the earth; a result of application, as
[the results] of contemplation of the unattractive extend through
knowledge of non-arising; a result of an assemblage, as eye-consciousness
and the other consciousnesses are results of the eye and the other
conditions; and a result of cultivation, as a transformation is a
result of form-realm mind. These are included within the results of
efficacy and predominance. Causes and results have been explained.

## 4. Taking and Giving Are Distinct Operations

The cause takes its result by assuming seed-status in relation to it.
This does not mean receiving an already manifest object. Giving concerns
its efficacy in bringing about the result. The present homogeneous
cause is said to give the immediately subsequent result; by the time
that result has arisen, the cause is past and the giving is complete.
The research translation incorrectly makes both cause and result past
at this point.

| Cause | Takes a result | Gives a result |
|---|---|---|
| Co-arisen and associated | When present | When present |
| Homogeneous and pervasive | When present | When present or past |
| Maturation | When present | When past |

The conditioning cause follows the taking rule where applicable but
is omitted from the five because it does not invariably have a result.
The table states the source's temporal account; it does not supply a
new theory of how past causal capacity persists.

## 5. The Acquisition Examples

The four cases are taking without giving, giving without taking, both,
and neither. The corrections in the wholesome and unwholesome examples
matter: the second case must concern the earlier acquisitions in their
past causal role, rather than newly arisen acquisitions taking results
now. The supplied “previous acquisitions” makes this contextual construal
visible; the Sanskrit says simply “those very ones.”

The arhatship example concerns obscured-indeterminate dharmas and retains
the account's acceptance of decline from arhatship. It is not presented
as a position shared by every school. The unobscured-indeterminate
example restricts the available cases, with an arhat's final aggregates
illustrating taking without giving.

These examples prevent seed-status from being read as a guarantee that
a corresponding result must eventually manifest. They also preserve
the distinction between acquisition and the dharma acquired.

## 6. The Moment-by-Moment Analysis

*Sālambananiyamena* restricts the inquiry to dharmas having an object;
it does not say “according to a fixed object,” as the research translation
does. The sequences compare wholesome, afflicted, and indeterminate
minds. They examine taking and giving within that restricted temporal
comparison, not whether a wholesome mind can have any later wholesome
result after an intervening different mind.

A transition to an unlike immediately following mind does not abolish
all homogeneous causality. The scope of the example must remain as
narrow as the question that introduces it.

## 7. Four Additional Result-Names

The support example runs from wind-circle to water-circle, not from
water supporting wind. The alternative classification also includes
application, assemblage, and cultivation, with an example for each.
The closing sentence includes these within efficacy and predominance;
it does not add four independent results to the established five.

The next discussion asks which classes of dharmas arise from how many
causes. The research file's transition directly to the four conditions
is premature.

## 8. Textual and Translation Decisions

The unit runs from 96.11 through 97.15 and ends with the explicit close
of causes and results. Source labels and surrounding spacing are removed;
the running Sanskrit otherwise remains intact, including *nātītāḥḥ*,
*catuṣkokṭikaḥ*, and *skndhāḥ*.

The phrase *anivṛtāvyākṛtasya paścātpādakaḥ* at 97.02 is unclear and
is marked rather than omitted or confidently reconstructed. The following
claims about giving, taking, and the final aggregates are intelligible
and translated. *Yāstāvat* at 97.02–03 is read contextually with the
correlative *saḥ*. The two *syāt tā eva* corrections are preserved
rather than replaced by a summary of the fourfold analysis.

The application-result example *yathā 'śubhāyā yāvad anutpādajñānam*
is elliptical. The English supplies “the results” and treats the span
as an illustrative range, not as an explicit claim that one exercise
alone suffices for the entire path. *Nirmāṇa* in the cultivation example
means transformation; the source here does not explicitly repeat
“transformation-mind.” These construals remain provisional.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

As Organon interpretation, the decisive distinction is between acquiring
a determinate causal role and exercising it in relation to a result.
The fourfold cases make those operations separable. Seed-language
therefore specifies causal capacity without requiring the finished
effect to pre-exist or promising its manifestation.

Learning the causal account requires more than replacing “cause” with
“seed.” One must determine the temporal status, the kind of result,
and whether the example concerns taking, giving, or both. The source's
corrections of its own acquisition examples model that precision.
