# VAK_2.53 Bhāṣya — Cultivated Qualities and Associated Cause

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> prayogajās tayor eva śrutacintāmayādikāḥ /
> saṃprayuktakahetus tu cittacaittāḥ samāśrayāḥ // 2.53 //

> Application-produced qualities, born of hearing, reflection, and
> the rest, are homogeneous causes only of those two [the equal and
> superior]. The associated cause consists of mind and mental factors
> having the same support.

The opening completes the homogeneous-cause account begun in VAK 2.52.
The second half introduces associated causality and follows it through
its explicit close. Pervasive causality belongs to the next study.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Is it only the path that is a homogeneous cause of what is equal or
superior? No. Worldly qualities too, when “produced through application,
are causes only of those two”: they are homogeneous causes of what is
equal or superior, not of what is inferior. Which qualities? “Those
born of hearing, reflection, and the rest.” These qualities produced
through application—born of hearing, reflection, and cultivation—are
causes only of what is equal or superior, not of what is inferior.

Thus, in the desire realm, qualities born of hearing are causes of those
born of hearing and reflection; those born of reflection are causes of
those born of reflection, since qualities born of cultivation are absent
there. In the form realm, those born of hearing are causes of those born
of hearing and cultivation, since qualities born of reflection are absent
there; those born of cultivation are causes only of those born of
cultivation. In the formless realm, those born of cultivation are causes
only of those born of cultivation. Since these too are divided into
nine grades, the weak-weak are causes of all nine, the weak-middle of
eight, and so on according to this rule.

Wholesome dharmas acquired through birth, however, in all nine grades,
are homogeneous causes of one another. Afflicted dharmas are likewise.
Unobscured-indeterminate dharmas are fourfold: those born of maturation,
those pertaining to bodily deportment, those pertaining to crafts, and
those arising together with a transformation-mind. In that order they
are homogeneous causes of four, three, two, and one [classes]. Even a
desire-realm transformation-mind is a result of the four concentrations;
within these, a result of a higher concentration is not a cause of a
result of a lower concentration. A homogeneous cause produced through
deliberate effort does not have an inferior result—as rice [is not such
a cause] of barley and the like—lest effort be fruitless.

For this very reason it is said: Can an arisen uncontaminated dharma
fail to be a cause of an unarisen [uncontaminated] dharma? Yes: for
example, arisen dharma-knowledge concerning suffering is not a cause
of unarisen receptivities to dharma-knowledge concerning suffering.
And everything superior is not a cause of what is inferior [within
the relation under discussion].

Can an uncontaminated dharma, restricted to one continuum and acquired
earlier, fail to be a cause of something that arises afterward? Yes:
future receptivities to dharma-knowledge concerning suffering are not
causes of the dharma-knowledge concerning suffering. For a result does
not exist earlier [than its cause], nor is there a future homogeneous
cause.

Can a previously arisen uncontaminated dharma fail to be a cause of a
subsequently arisen uncontaminated dharma? Yes: a superior one is not
a cause of an inferior one. For example, when someone has fallen from
a higher fruit and a lower fruit becomes manifest; and an acquisition
of dharma-knowledge concerning suffering is not a cause of acquisitions
of receptivity to dharma-knowledge concerning suffering that arise
together with successively later moments, because they are inferior.
The homogeneous cause has been explained.

“But the associated cause consists of mind and mental factors.” On
that account, mind and mental factors born at different times and in
different continua would also be associated causes of one another.
Then let them have the same aspect and object. Even so, the same
consequence follows. Then let them occur at the same time. On that
account, the consequence still follows for those born in different
continua—for example, people looking at the new moon. Therefore they
must have “the same support.”

Mind and mental factors whose support is the same are associated causes
of one another. “Same” means non-different. For example, the very moment
of the eye faculty that supports eye-consciousness also supports feeling
and the other factors associated with it. This should be understood
in the same way through the mental faculty: its moment supports mental
consciousness and the factors associated with it.

Whatever is an associated cause is also a co-arisen cause. In what
respect, then, is it a co-arisen cause, and in what respect an associated
cause? It is a co-arisen cause in the sense of being mutually a result,
like fellow travelers proceeding along a road through one another's
strength. It is an associated cause in the sense of association through
the five equalities, like those same travelers jointly undertaking the
use of the same food, drink, and other provisions. For without even
one [member or requisite of the association], they are not associated
with the whole [provisional construal]. This constitutes their causal
status. The associated cause has been explained.

## 4. Application and the Direction of Homogeneous Causality

*Tayor eva*, “only of those two,” refers back to the equal and superior
of VAK 2.52. The present clause extends that restriction to worldly
qualities produced through application. The contrasting account of
birth-acquired wholesome dharmas and afflicted dharmas prevents the
restriction from becoming a rule for all homogeneous causality.

The hearing/reflection/cultivation distinctions report the availability
of these technical classes in the stated realms. They do not mean that
ordinary desire-realm life contains no practice or learning. Nor does
the form-realm statement establish a general absence of thought there.
The nine-grade rule specifies which later grades a given cultivated
grade can cause; it does not promise that every effort inevitably
produces improvement.

The rice-and-barley analogy supports the qualitative direction of the
specific causal relation. It should not be turned into a claim that
practice can never be interrupted or that a practitioner cannot decline.
The text itself immediately includes falling from a higher fruit.

## 5. Acquisition, Arising, and Manifestation

The three questions distinguish three failures of homogeneous causality:
a superior arisen dharma is not a cause of an inferior unarisen one;
prior acquisition does not turn a future dharma into an already arisen
cause; and prior arising does not remove the equal-or-superior restriction.

The second is particularly important: possessing a future receptivity
before a knowledge arises does not make that future receptivity a
homogeneous cause of the knowledge. Earlier possession and earlier
arising are different determinations. The third then includes both a
lower fruit becoming manifest after decline and relations between
acquisitions themselves. These acquisitions should not be silently
replaced by the knowledge and receptivity acquired.

“Not a cause” in these questions is governed by the homogeneous-cause
inquiry. It need not deny every other causal relation in the sixfold
account. Likewise, the four unobscured-indeterminate classes receive
an ordered causal classification; this does not identify their ethical
status with that of every action involving deportment or a craft.

## 6. What Makes Association One Association

The associated-cause definition is progressively specified. Mind and
mental factors are insufficient as a bare class description. Shared
aspect and object still allow different occurrences; shared time still
allows different continua. The new-moon example makes the latter
possibility concrete. The final qualification requires the numerically
same support, not merely supports of the same kind.

The successive qualifications accumulate. “Same support” does not cancel
aspect, object, or time. The passage explicitly returns to the five
equalities already explained in the mind-and-mental-factor account:
support, object, aspect, time, and equality in the number of instances.
The last concerns one instance of each associated factor, not one
substance shared by all of them or a complex containing just one factor.
These five are recalled here for analysis; the present Sanskrit invokes
them without listing them again.

## 7. Associated and Co-arisen Cause

The two causal descriptions apply to the same mind-and-factor complex
under different determinations. Mutual resulthood establishes its
co-arisen-cause character; association through the five equalities
establishes its associated-cause character. The text states that every
associated cause is also co-arisen. It does not assert the converse:
the great elements, for example, belong to the earlier co-arisen account
without becoming associated mental factors.

The travelers illustrate the distinction within one group: advancing
through mutual strength and participating in shared provisions. The
analogy does not replace the technical criteria with social cooperation
or claim that different people's minds are associated in the sense
just excluded by the new-moon example.

## 8. Textual and Translation Decisions

The Sanskrit unit runs from 87.20 through 88.27, covering the rest of
homogeneous causality and the complete associated-cause account. The
pervasive-cause question at 89.01 begins VAK 2.54 and is reserved. The
research 2.53 file omits the opening part of this unit and instead
includes the pervasive cause; the running source governs this edition.

The witness is retained apart from source labels and surrounding spacing.
At 88.07–08 it reads *anutpannasyāsravasya*, apparently “of an unarisen
contaminated [dharma].” The example is an uncontaminated receptivity;
the translation supplies “[uncontaminated]” and marks the discrepancy.
At 88.12, *avimātro* is contextually rendered “superior,” without
silently repairing the Sanskrit. The compressed acquisition example
at 88.12–14 is translated separately from the decline example; its
precise moment-by-moment construction remains provisional.

Other transcription defects include *nirmāṇacittamapoi*,
*caturdhyanaphalam*, *utpanām*, and the interrupted mental-faculty
sentence at 88.20–22. Their contextual English does not constitute
a critically established replacement text.

The final *ekena api ... sarveṇa* construction does not explicitly name
what “one” refers to. The research translation takes it as one of the
conditions; a member of the associated complex is also a possible
construal. The English leaves that point visible rather than silently
settling it. Both readings occur within the explicit account of
association through five equalities.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

As Organon interpretation, the two halves teach different discriminations.
For serial cultivation, we must distinguish acquisition from arising
and identify the permitted direction of a homogeneous result. For
association, resemblance among occurrences must be distinguished from
membership in one supported cognitive complex.

This supplies a precise form to our discussion of learning. “These belong
together” becomes intelligible only when we determine how: as earlier
and later cultivated qualities, as possession and what is possessed,
or as mind and associated factors. A single claim of unity would conceal
those relations. The text's examples make their differences learnable
without requiring a separate speculative theory in the translation.
