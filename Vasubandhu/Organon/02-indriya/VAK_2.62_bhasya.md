## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“Arisen mind and mental factors, except the final ones, constitute the
immediately antecedent condition.” Arisen mind and mental factors,
excluding the final ones of an arhat, are the immediately antecedent
condition. This condition is both equal and immediate; hence it is
called the equal-and-immediate condition.

For this very reason material form is not an immediately antecedent
condition: its arising is unequal. For example, after desire-realm
material form there sometimes arise desire-realm material form and
form-realm non-informative form, and sometimes desire-realm material
form and uncontaminated non-informative form. Thus the manifestation
of material form is mixed. The immediately antecedent condition,
however, is unmixed. Venerable Vasumitra explains [the exclusion of
material form] by the arising of a second stream of accumulated material
form while a first has not yet ceased. The venerable elder explains
it by the arising of lesser and greater quantities. Sometimes a small
amount of material form arises from a large amount, as ashes from a
heap of straw; sometimes a large amount arises from a small amount,
as from a banyan seed there develops, gradually, a banyan with many
branches and descending roots.

But do mental factors not also arise in lesser and greater numbers,
in wholesome, unwholesome, and indeterminate minds, and in the three
concentrations beginning with that accompanied by applied and sustained
thought? They do, with respect to different kinds, but not within their
own kind. A greater plurality of feelings never arises, nor of
perceptions and the other factors. Does a factor, then, serve as
immediately antecedent condition only for its own kind? No. The entire
complex is the immediately antecedent condition for the entire next
complex. All that has been said here is that a greater quantity of
feeling-entities and the like does not arise from a smaller quantity
of those entities.

Those who maintain homogeneity of the continuum, however, hold that
an immediately antecedent condition is such only for its own kind:
mind for mind alone, feeling for feeling alone, and so on. When an
afflicted state arises after an unafflicted one, they take a previously
ceased affliction as the immediately antecedent condition of that
affliction, just as the mind [preceding] the attainment of cessation
is the condition of the mind emerging from it. This is not accepted,
because it would follow that the first uncontaminated mind could
not arise. Formations dissociated from mind likewise are not immediately
antecedent conditions, for the same reason of mixed manifestation:
those belonging to the three realms and those not connected with
them become manifest simultaneously.

Why is the future not admitted as immediately antecedent condition?
Because the future period is unordered: earlier and later are absent
there. How, then, does the Blessed One know that this future event
will occur immediately after that future event? Through inference
from the past and present. It is said that the Blessed One sees in
the past that a maturation of a certain kind arose from an action
of a certain kind, or that one dharma arose from another. This present
action is also of that kind; therefore he knows that a maturation
of that kind will arise from it, or one dharma from another.

Nor [according to this explanation] is his knowledge merely inferential:
through inference from the past and present, the Blessed One directly
sees the scattered future entities and knows, “By this person performing
such an action, this future fruit has been taken up.” In that case,
however, without seeing the earlier limit he would not know the later
limit. Others say that in the continuum of beings there exists a
special formation dissociated from mind which serves as a sign of
the fruit. Observing it, the Blessed One knows the future even without
making meditation and supernormal knowledge manifest. But then the
Blessed One would be a reader of signs, rather than one who directly
realizes. Therefore the Sautrāntikas say that the Blessed One knows
all simply by wishing to know. For the Blessed One has said, “The
cognitive domain of the Buddhas is inconceivable.”

But if a fixed order is not already established in the future, why
does only receptivity to dharma-knowledge concerning suffering arise
immediately after the highest mundane dharmas, and no other dharma?
And so on, up to this: why does only knowledge of exhaustion arise
immediately after the adamantine-like [concentration], and no other
dharma? Whatever has its arising dependent upon a given thing arises
immediately after it, as sprouts arise from seeds and so forth, even
without an immediately antecedent condition.

Why are an arhat's final mind and mental factors not immediately
antecedent conditions? Because they are not connected with another
mind. But mind that has just ceased is said to be the mental faculty.
If there is no subsequent consciousness, would the final mind then
also fail to be the mental faculty? The mental faculty is determined
by its character as support, not by its operative activity. Its
support-character therefore remains; another consciousness does not
arise because other requisite conditions are lacking. The immediately
antecedent condition, however, is determined by operative activity.
A dharma taken up as fruit by that condition cannot be prevented
from arising by all dharmas or by all living beings.

Are dharmas that are immediately successive to mind also temporally
contiguous with mind? There are four cases. First: the mind emerging
from an attainment without mind, and the second and later moments
of the attainment. Second: the arising and other characteristics of
the first moment of the attainment, and of a state accompanied by
mind. Third: the first moment of the attainment, and the state
accompanied by mind. Fourth: the arising and other characteristics
of the second and later moments of the attainment, and of the
emergence-mind.

Are dharmas that are immediately successive to mind also temporally
contiguous with the attainment? There are four cases. The previous
third and fourth cases become the first and second; the previous
first and second become the third and fourth. How, then, can the
emergence-mind, separated by a long interval, be called immediately
successive to the mind [preceding] the attainment? Because no other
mind intervenes. The immediately antecedent condition has been explained.

“All dharmas are object-condition.” As appropriate: visible form is
the object of eye-consciousness together with its associated factors;
sound, of ear-consciousness; smell, of nose-consciousness; taste, of
tongue-consciousness; tangibles, of body-consciousness; and all dharmas,
of mental consciousness. A dharma that is the object of a given dharma
is never not its object. Even when it is not being apprehended, it
has that character, just as wood and the like are called fuel even
when not being burned, because they have that character. These minds
and mental factors are each fixed with respect to their objects by
the determination of sense-field, entity, and characteristic.

Are they also fixed with respect to their support? Yes, he says.
Those that have arisen are joined to their support; [the transmitted
continuation is damaged: “unarisen … past … separated from support”].
Others say that past ones too are joined to their support. The
object-condition has been explained.

“What is named the conditioning cause is held to be the predominant
condition.” Precisely what is conditioning cause is predominant
condition. It is called predominant because this condition is more
extensive. But object-condition includes all dharmas, and predominant
condition also includes all dharmas: what greater extent is there?
Co-arisen dharmas never serve as objects [of one another], whereas
they do serve as predominant conditions. That is its greater extent.
Alternatively, it is a condition of a greater range: every dharma
is such a condition for every conditioned dharma other than itself.

Can a dharma fail to be a condition of another dharma under any of
the four conditions? Yes: a dharma in relation to itself. Can something
other than it also fail to be its condition? Yes: the conditioned
in relation to the unconditioned, and the unconditioned in relation
to the unconditioned.

## 4. The Whole Complex and Its Successor

The variation objection is essential to the account. Ethical and
meditative states contain different complements of mental factors,
but this does not mean that several feelings arise together as the
successor to one feeling. The answer explicitly makes the whole
complex the antecedent condition of the succeeding whole complex.
Its claim about numerical uniformity within a factor-kind must not
be converted into the opposing school's own-kind-only succession rule.

The latter rule encounters the first uncontaminated mind: it cannot
require a previously arisen uncontaminated predecessor of its own
kind without excluding the very first occurrence. This argument
concerns immediately antecedent conditioning. It does not abolish
the distinct requirements of homogeneous cause.

Vasumitra's overlapping material streams explain why material form
fails the relevant unmixed succession requirement. They do not defend
material form as an immediately antecedent condition. The seed-and-sprout
example later makes clear that excluding this specific condition does
not exclude causal dependence.

## 5. Two Fourfold Analyses, Two Senses of Immediacy

The translation distinguishes technical succession (*samanantara*)
from temporal contiguity (*nirantara*). The four cases mean, in order:
first relation only, second relation only, both, neither.

| Case | Successive to mind | Contiguous with mind | Examples given |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Yes | No | Emergence-mind; second and later attainment moments |
| Second | No | Yes | Arising and other characteristics of the first attainment moment and of a minded state |
| Third | Yes | Yes | First attainment moment; minded state |
| Fourth | No | No | Characteristics of later attainment moments and of emergence-mind |

For the second question, keep “successive to mind” but replace
“contiguous with mind” by “contiguous with the attainment.” The old
third, fourth, first, and second cases then supply the new first,
second, third, and fourth respectively. The text gives that permutation
rather than repeating the examples.

Thus *cittanirantara* must not be paraphrased here as merely “no other
mind intervenes”: that would erase the contrast which the examples
establish. The final *cittāntarāvyavahitatva*, absence of another
intervening mind, explains how technical mental succession survives
a long non-mental interval. It does not make that interval disappear.

The attainment moments can stand in succession to the entering mind
without themselves becoming mental antecedent conditions for the
emerging mind. Being conditioned in this relation and serving as its
condition are distinct roles.

## 6. Future Knowledge, Support, and Objects

The future-knowledge discussion contains reported explanations and
criticisms, not one settled account of inference. The first proposal
tries to retain direct vision of future entities while using past
and present patterns to identify their relation. Its reply about
direct knowledge must remain in the translation before the objection
that knowledge of the later would depend on seeing the earlier.
The sign theory is separately proposed and challenged; the Sautrāntikas
are explicitly named only at the final alternative.

Likewise, the answer about fixed path succession explains dependence
without requiring a prearranged future sequence. Its sprout analogy
expressly goes beyond the mental antecedent condition. It should not
be used to redefine every causal sequence as *samanantara*.

The final arhat mind retains support-character but lacks the operative
antecedent relation to a further mind. In the object account, a different
distinction separates object-character from actual apprehension.
“All dharmas” is distributed according to the appropriate consciousness;
it does not make every dharma an object of every sensory cognition,
nor assert that all are apprehended together.

## 7. Predominance and Its Limits

Predominant condition corresponds to *kāraṇahetu*. Its greater range
is explained relationally: co-arisen dharmas can condition one another
predominantly without being objects of one another. The alternative
explanation again concerns greater extent, not a newly introduced
class of powerful objects.

The concluding question asks for cases in which none of the four
conditioning relations applies. It does not ask whether all four
apply simultaneously. The cases exclude self-conditioning and
conditioning an unconditioned target. The latter exclusion does not
prevent an unconditioned dharma from serving as predominant condition
for a conditioned dharma within this account.

## 8. Textual and Translation Decisions

The Sanskrit reproduces 98.09–100.18 with only source labels and
surrounding spacing removed. It retains transcription problems such
as *aupa cayika*, *ottam*, *vatsryate*, *traivātuka*, *amuṣyānāgatattyānantaram*,
*varjo pamānantaram*, *kpratigṛhītaḥ*, *dvīrtīyādīnāṃ*, *mannovijñānasya*,
and *savandharmāḥ*. The translation follows the locally recoverable
argument without presenting those forms as a corrected edition.

At 99.05, *nacānyat jñānamānumānikaṃ bhavati* is awkward. The English
renders the defense indicated by its following explanation: the proposed
knowledge is not merely inferential, since direct seeing is asserted.
That construal is contextual; the displayed Sanskrit remains unchanged.
At 99.20–21 the damaged fruit-taking phrase is rendered with the dharma
as the fruit taken up by the antecedent condition. This preserves the
relative construction and avoids making the fruit itself the agent
that takes another fruit.

At 100.09–10, *utpannās tv āśrayasahitā anutpannā hy atītā
āśrayaviśliṣṭāḥ* does not yield the first pass's confident three-time
account. The clear opening and explicit alternative about past dharmas
are translated; the intervening damaged wording is marked in place.
Resolving it requires another textual witness. No smooth sentence about
future supports has been supplied by inference.

Bracketed material makes contextual supplements visible, notably the
mind preceding cessation attainment and the object relation between
co-arisen dharmas. “Equal” in the etymological gloss does not mean
that successive minds have identical ethical or meditative status.
“Object-condition” remains distinct from “support,” translating
*ālambana* and *āśraya* respectively.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

For the Organon study, the passage teaches the need to determine
which relation is at issue. Being earlier, being a support, being
an object, and being a predominant condition give different answers.
The whole-complex argument and the two fourfold analyses make that
lesson concrete: neither a common label nor temporal proximity alone
settles the conditioning relation.

This advances the inquiry into grounds and conditions without assigning
all causes to an objective domain and all conditions to a subjective
one. The cause-condition already incorporates five causes, and
predominance extends to conditioned targets beyond cognition. A broader
philosophical synthesis must preserve these distinctions and their
arguments. Here the work of learning is to remove the conflations
that the objections expose.
