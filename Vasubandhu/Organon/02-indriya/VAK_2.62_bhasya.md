# VAK_2.62 Bhāṣya — Antecedent, Object, Predominant

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> cittacaittā acaramā utpannāḥ samanantaraḥ /
>
> ālambanaṃ sarvadharmāḥ kāraṇākhyo 'dhipaḥ smṛtaḥ // 2.62 //

> Arisen mind and mental factors, except the final ones, are the
> immediately antecedent Condition. All dharmas can be Supports as
> Conditions. What is called the conditioning cause is held to be
> the predominant Condition.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Arisen mind and mental factors, except the final ones, are the
immediately antecedent Condition. The exception is the arhat's
last mind and factors. This Condition is both “equal” and
“immediately next.” Form cannot fill this role, because its
arising is irregular. After Form belonging to the Desire Realm,
nonmanifest Form of the Desire and Form Realms can sometimes arise;
at other times Desire-Realm and *āsrava*-free Form occur. Its
appearance is mixed, whereas an immediately antecedent Condition
is not. Venerable Vasumitra adds that a second production can occur
within one continuing, accumulated Form before the first has
ceased. Another elder appeals to variations in amount: a small
result, like ashes, can arise from a large heap of straw, while
a great banyan with many descending branches develops from a
small seed.

Do mental factors not also increase and decrease in number across
wholesome, unwholesome, and indeterminate minds, and across the
three kinds of concentration beginning with one possessing thought
and examination? They do across different kinds, but not within
the same kind: there is never a greater quantity of Feeling, or
of Conception and the other factors, within their own kind.
Does only an item of the same kind provide the immediately
antecedent Condition? No. The whole preceding group conditions
the whole next group. The point is that numerous items of Feeling
and the like do not arise from fewer items of their own kind.

The proponents of continuity by kind, however, say that only the
same kind provides this Condition: mind for mind, Feeling for
Feeling, and so forth. When an afflicted factor follows an
unafflicted interval, they assign it an earlier ceased affliction
as its immediately antecedent Condition; they compare the mind
preceding cessation attainment with the mind on emerging from it.
This account is rejected: it would make the first mind without
*āsrava* impossible. Mind-dissociated formations are also excluded
from the immediately antecedent role because their appearances are
mixed; formations belonging to the three Realms and to no Realm
can appear together.

Why is a future dharma not an immediately antecedent Condition?
The future is disordered and has no settled earlier and later.
How, then, does the Blessed One know that this future event will
follow that one? One answer appeals to inference from past and
present: the Blessed One sees that a given kind of action in the
past produced a given kind of maturation, or that one dharma
followed another; seeing the same kind of action now, he knows
what will follow. Yet his knowledge is not merely inferential:
through those past and present signs he directly sees the future
entities in their dispersed condition and knows the future fruit
appropriated by the person now acting. The objection is that he
would then be unable to know a later future without first seeing
an earlier one.

Others propose a special mind-dissociated formation in the
continuum of beings, a sign of future fruit, by which the Blessed
One knows the future without entering meditation or supernormal
knowledge. The reply says this would make him a reader of signs,
not one who directly realizes. The Sautrāntikas therefore say
that the Blessed One knows whatever he wishes to know; the range
of Buddhas' knowledge is declared inconceivable. Another objection
asks why, if the future has no fixed order, receptivity to Dharma
Knowledge of suffering follows the highest worldly dharma, and
Knowledge of exhaustion follows the diamond-like state. The reply
is that what is bound to arise dependent on a given event arises
immediately after it, as a sprout follows a seed without requiring
an immediately antecedent Condition of this kind.

Why is the arhat's final mind not such a Condition? No further mind
is connected to it. But would it then fail to be *manas*, since
*manas* is a mind that has just ceased? No: *manas* is characterized
by serving as a Basis, not by actually producing the next Cognition.
It can have the status of Basis even when some other cause needed
for another Cognition is absent. The immediately antecedent
Condition, by contrast, is characterized by causal activity: once
it has taken a fruit, all dharmas and all beings together cannot
prevent that fruit from arising.

Are dharmas immediately after mind also uninterrupted by mind?
The text gives four cases. The first comprises the emerging mind
from a mindless attainment and the second and later moments of
that attainment. The second comprises the marks of the first
moment of attainment and of a state with mind. The third comprises
the first moment of attainment and a state with mind. The fourth
comprises the marks of the second and later attainment moments and
of the emerging mind. For the corresponding comparison with what
is uninterrupted by an attainment, reverse the first and second
cases with the third and fourth as the text directs [the compact
fourfold terminology needs closer specialty study]. How can the
emerging mind be called immediately next to the mind before an
attainment when a long interval intervenes? Because no other mind
intervenes. The immediately antecedent Condition has been explained.

All dharmas can be Supports as Conditions, each as appropriate:
visible Form for eye Cognition and its associated factors, sound
for ear Cognition, smell for nose Cognition, taste for tongue
Cognition, tangible for body Cognition, and all dharmas for mind
Cognition. Once a dharma is the Support of another, it does not
cease to be its Support even when it is not presently attended to;
wood is still called fuel when it is not burning. Mind and mental
factors are fixed with respect to their own Supports by the Essence,
entity, and characteristic of what is supported.

Are they also fixed with respect to Basis? Yes for those that have
arisen together with a Basis. Past dharmas that are no longer
arising are separated from their Basis; others maintain that even
past dharmas remain joined to it. The Support-condition has been
explained. What is called the conditioning cause is the
predominant Condition. It is “predominant” because this Condition
has wider scope. An objection says that both Support-condition
and predominant Condition concern all dharmas. The reply says
co-arising dharmas are never Supports of one another, although
they can be predominant Conditions for one another. Alternatively,
it is the Condition *of* what is predominant: every dharma other
than a conditioned dharma's own nature can serve it. Can one
dharma fail to condition another in all four ways? Yes: a dharma
in relation to itself, or even another dharma in some cases;
a conditioned dharma in relation to an unconditioned one, and
an unconditioned dharma in relation to another unconditioned one.

## 4. Interpretation

The three conditions are differentiated by their functions: immediate
mental succession, objecthood relative to cognition, and
predominance. The whole-complex analysis and the relevant-cognition
restriction specify the first two definitions without changing their
verse wording.
