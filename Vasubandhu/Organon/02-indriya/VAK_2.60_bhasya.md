# VAK_2.60 Bhāṣya — Dharma Classes and Their Causes

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> kliṣṭā vipākajāḥ śeṣāḥ prathamāryā yathākramam /
> vipākaṃ sarvagaṃ hitvā tau sabhāgaṃ ca śeṣajāḥ // 2.60 //

> Afflicted, maturation-born, remaining, and first noble dharmas
> arise respectively from the causes remaining after excluding
> maturation, pervasive cause, those two, and those two together
> with homogeneous cause.

The natural unit at 97.16–98.02 includes the opening qualification
of VAK 2.61: the initial counts concern mind and mental factors;
other dharmas also exclude associated cause. It ends with the
explicit completion of the detailed cause account.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Which dharmas arise from how many causes? In brief, these dharmas
are fourfold: afflicted dharmas, maturation-born dharmas, the first
uncontaminated dharmas, and those remaining. What are the remaining
ones? Indeterminate dharmas apart from maturation-born ones, and
wholesome dharmas apart from the first uncontaminated moment.
These are the four classes.

“Afflicted, maturation-born, remaining, and first noble dharmas
arise respectively from the causes remaining after excluding
maturation, pervasive cause, those two, and those two together
with homogeneous cause.” Afflicted dharmas arise from the five
remaining causes, excluding maturation cause. Maturation-born
dharmas likewise arise from five, excluding pervasive cause.
The remaining dharmas arise from four, excluding those two,
maturation and pervasive causes. The first uncontaminated dharmas
arise from three, excluding those two and homogeneous cause.

Which dharmas have been described in this fourfold way? “Mind
and mental factors.” What, then, of dharmas dissociated from mind
and material dharmas? “The others likewise, but without associated
cause.” The other afflicted and remaining classes arise just as
mind and mental factors do, with associated cause additionally
excluded. Thus the afflicted and maturation-born arise from four,
the remaining from three, and the first uncontaminated from two.
No dharma arises from just one cause. The detailed exposition
of causes is complete.

## 4. Four Classes, Distinct Exclusions

The verse orders its classes differently from the opening prose:
afflicted, maturation-born, remaining, then first noble. *Yathākramam*,
“respectively,” links that verse order to the exclusions. *Śeṣa*
names the remaining class; *śeṣaja* says that each class arises
from the causes left after its specified exclusions.

The “remaining” indeterminate dharmas must be read after setting
aside the already named afflicted and maturation-born classes.
Otherwise obscured-indeterminate dharmas would be counted twice.
The prose's compressed description does not erase the initial
fourfold division.

## 5. Counts Depend on the Kind of Dharma

| Class | Causes excluded initially | Mind and mental factors | Material and dissociated dharmas |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Afflicted | Maturation | 5 | 4 |
| Maturation-born | Pervasive | 5 | 4 |
| Remaining | Maturation and pervasive | 4 | 3 |
| First uncontaminated | Maturation, pervasive, and homogeneous | 3 | 2 |

The last column additionally excludes associated cause. These are
counts of causal types, not counts of individual things participating
in production. Five causes for the afflicted and five for maturation-born
dharmas do not give identical causal descriptions: the omissions differ.

## 6. The First Uncontaminated Occurrence

The Bhāṣya specifies the verse's “first noble” as the first
uncontaminated moment. Homogeneous cause is excluded in addition
to maturation and pervasive causes. In the preceding account,
homogeneous causality required prior arising of the relevant
similar dharma; the first uncontaminated occurrence cannot have
an earlier uncontaminated occurrence of its own kind in that
continuum.

It nevertheless has conditioning, co-arisen, and associated causes
in the mind-and-factor account. In the material and dissociated
account, conditioning and co-arisen causes remain. The absence
of one kind of antecedent is therefore not absence of causal
explanation, nor does it mean that contaminated practice has
no role in preparation.

## 7. The Closing Claim

“No dharma arises from just one cause” closes the enumeration.
The scope is arising dharmas under the six-cause account. It does
not include the unconditioned as something produced, and it does
not by itself assert that every philosophical use of “cause” must
be counted in the same way.

The explicit closure comes after the domain qualification, not
immediately after the four initial counts. Keeping that qualification
here prevents the mind-and-factor counts from appearing universal.
The next question opens the four conditions.

## 8. Textual and Translation Decisions

The running Sanskrit is retained apart from source labels and
surrounding spacing. It includes *hetvā*, *caturbhryo*,
*prayuktakahetunaikena*, *cittacaīttāḥ*, and *catubhryo*. Their
contextual construals follow the repeated exclusions and counts;
no critically corrected Sanskrit is claimed.

The research translation stops before the mind-and-factor
qualification and refers it to 2.61. This edition instead carries
the opening clauses of that verse through the natural close at
98.02. The pratyaya commentary unit for VAK 2.61 begins at 98.03
with the question about conditions; the already translated clauses
are identified as such in its anchor.

The translation says “arise from,” following *jāyante* and
*utpadyante*. The analysis distinguishes the several causal
relations without replacing the source's claim of arising with
a merely hypothetical list of possible causes.

## 9. Interpretation

As an Organon bridge, the verse and its continuation keep the cause account
intact while specifying its scope: four dharma-classes have distinct
exclusion profiles, and material or mind-dissociated dharmas additionally
exclude associated cause. The first uncontaminated occurrence lacks a prior
homogeneous instance but remains causally articulated.

The Bhāṣya closes the detailed cause account at 98.02 with the statement
that no dharma arises from only one cause. The next commentary unit begins at
98.03 by naming the four conditions. This is the source's transition from
which cause-types apply to a dharma to a new classification of conditional
relations; the broader Loka and Absolute Idealism synthesis is project-level,
not a claim of this passage.

The training point is that a cause-count is not a flat quantity. Each class
of Dharma has a determinate exclusion profile, and the first uncontaminated
occurrence is still causally articulated despite lacking homogeneous
continuity. This completes the Hetu census and makes the turn to conditions
necessary.
