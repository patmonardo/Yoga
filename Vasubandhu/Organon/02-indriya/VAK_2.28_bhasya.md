# VAK_2.28 Bhāṣya — Wholesome Consciousness and Remorse

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> savitarkavicāratvāt kuśale kāmacetasi /
> dvāviṃśatiś caitasikāḥ kaukṛtyam adhikaṃ kvacit // 2.28 //

> Because wholesome Desire-Principle consciousness is accompanied by
> *vitarka* and *vicāra*, it has twenty-two mental factors; remorse is
> additional in some cases.

The unit begins with the general question of how many factors necessarily
occur in each consciousness and includes the complete explanation of
remorse through 58.03.

## 3. Scientific English Rendering

It must now be stated how many mental factors necessarily occur in which
consciousness. First, Desire-Principle consciousness is fivefold:
wholesome and unwholesome—the unwholesome is twofold, *āveṇika* and
associated with another affliction—and ethically indeterminate, which
is twofold, obscured and unobscured.

Desire-Principle consciousness is necessarily accompanied by *vitarka*
and *vicāra*. Hence: “Because wholesome Desire-Principle consciousness
is accompanied by *vitarka* and *vicāra*, there are twenty-two mental
factors.” They necessarily occur: ten great-ground factors, ten
wholesome great-ground factors, *vitarka*, and *vicāra*. “Remorse is
additional in some cases.” Remorse is not present in every wholesome
consciousness. Where it is present, adding that factor makes
twenty-three.

What is *kaukṛtya*? It is the state of what was badly done. Here,
however, the mental Dharma that takes what was badly done as its
object-support is called *kaukṛtya*: regret in consciousness.
Similarly, the liberation-gateway that takes emptiness as its
object-support is called “emptiness,” and non-greed that takes the
unattractive as its object-support is called “the unattractive.”

In ordinary speech too, occupants are designated by their place:
“The whole village has arrived,” “The whole country has arrived.”
What was badly done is the basis of regret. Alternatively, this is
cause-to-effect transfer of designation, as in the statement, “These
six Contact Essences are to be understood as former action.”

But how is regret that takes what was not done as its object-support
called *kaukṛtya*? Even what was not done can receive the designation
“done”: “I did not do well in not doing it.” Which regret is wholesome?
That in which one is troubled after failing to do what is wholesome,
or after doing what is unwholesome. By the reverse, regret is
unwholesome. Both kinds thus have a twofold basis.

## 4. Movement and Scope

The opening question introduces counts for particular consciousnesses.
Desire-Principle consciousness is divided into five types, but the verse
treats only the wholesome case. The count of twenty-two is followed by
remorse as a conditional addition, prompting an explanation of what
*kaukṛtya* names.

The questions about the name and about regret for what was not done test
the relation between the mental factor and what it concerns. The
commentary gives object-support, place-to-occupant, and cause-to-effect
designation as distinct explanations. The unit ends by distinguishing
wholesome and unwholesome regret. No named school or explicit rival
position is introduced.

## 5. The Fivefold Setting and the Count

The five Desire-Principle types are wholesome, two unwholesome types,
and two ethically indeterminate types. The unwholesome pair is
*āveṇika* and associated with another affliction; the indeterminate
pair is obscured and unobscured. This passage sets out the division
without developing these categories; the next verse continues the
counting inquiry.

The twenty-two are ten universal great-ground factors, ten wholesome
great-ground factors, *vitarka*, and *vicāra*. Consciousness itself is
not counted as a mental factor. These are counts of *caitasika*
Dharmas, not of the Faculties counted earlier in the chapter. The
Bhāṣya states the required *vitarka* and *vicāra* accompaniment but
does not define them here.

## 6. The Conditional Addition of Remorse

*Kvacit*, “in some cases,” prevents remorse from being included among
the factors present in every wholesome consciousness. Where it occurs
in addition to the required twenty-two, the count is twenty-three.
This is the local counting rule; later conditions must be considered
before treating it as an exhaustive account of all possible
consciousnesses.

The factor is *cetaso vipratisāraḥ*, regret in consciousness. It is
distinct from the badly done action or omission serving as its
object-support. Confusing those roles would turn a mental factor into
the conduct about which it arises.

The treatment clarifies the earlier designation *aniyata*, unfixed.
The occurrence of remorse is conditional, and its ethical character
is not fixed as exclusively wholesome. “Unfixed” does not mean
conditionless.

## 7. Naming, Object-Support, and Cause

The first explanation identifies remorse through the object-support it
takes. The examples preserve the distinction between an object and the
Dharma directed toward it: a liberation-gateway taking emptiness as
object is called “emptiness,” and non-greed taking the unattractive as
object is called “the unattractive.”

The village and country examples illustrate designation of occupants
through their place. The badly done is the basis of regret. Alternatively,
the Bhāṣya proposes *hetūpacāra*, transferring the cause's designation
to its effect. The quoted statement calls the six Contact Essences
“former action.” This direction is from cause-name to effect; it is
not an identification of cause and effect.

These explanations should remain distinct. The mental factor is not
its object-support, basis, or cause, even when a related term supplies
its designation.

## 8. Omission and Ethical Direction

The question about what was not done is answered through ordinary
evaluative speech: one may call the omission “done badly”—“I did not
do well in not doing it.” The account therefore includes omissions
without pretending the omitted act occurred.

| Regret | Concerning an act | Concerning an omission |
|---|---|---|
| Wholesome | regret for doing the unwholesome | regret for failing to do the wholesome |
| Unwholesome | regret for doing the wholesome | regret for failing to do the unwholesome |

The closing *ubhayādhiṣṭhānam*, “having a twofold basis,” follows
this treatment of acts and omissions. Both wholesome and unwholesome
conduct can enter the account. Neither the conduct's status alone nor
the mere presence of regret determines the ethical classification.

The source transcription has mechanical defects in the naming examples,
including *śūnyatālalmvanaṃ*, *vimokṣamukhṃ*, and
*aśubhālambanaścālobho*. The translation reads these contextually as
emptiness as object-support, a liberation-gateway, and non-greed taking
the unattractive as object-support. The Sanskrit transcription remains
visible above; these are interpretive resolutions, not a new
collation.

## 9. Interpretation

The passage moves from ground classification to counting in a specified
consciousness. The preceding classes supply necessary members; the
Desire-Principle setting adds *vitarka* and *vicāra*; remorse is an
additional factor only in some cases. The semantic discussion matters
to the count because *kaukṛtya* names a mental factor, not the act or
omission it takes as its basis.

The ethical account is relational. Regret for doing the unwholesome or
omitting the wholesome is wholesome; the reverse is unwholesome. An
action's ethical status does not simply transfer to the mental
evaluation of it. The source's examples of object-support and
cause-to-effect designation likewise preserve relations without
collapsing their terms.

In the inner-instrument Techne, *prajñā* identified in VAK 2.24 as
Science of Principles remains within the ten universal great-ground
factors; it is not added again to this count. The present calculation
concerns associated mental factors and does not substitute for the
Principle classification.

The Kośa's governing synthesis: Vijñāna is Discriminative Cognition joining
and governing Perception and Conception. Their unity is Inconceivable as a
homogeneous operation; its Idea is disclosed in the Cognition Base. Vijñāna
governs Mind and guides the reading of Dharma Base. Form Base and Dharma
Base both include the same *avijñapti* in distinct classifications, and
Vijñāna bears a *prati* relation to *avijñapti*. This larger synthesis
does not merge the count of mental factors with Faculties or Principles.
