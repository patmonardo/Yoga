# VAK_1.19 Bhāṣya — Paired Faculties and Principle-Unity

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> jātigocaravijñānasāmānyād ekadhātutā /
>
> dvitve 'pi cakṣurādīnāṃ śobhārthaṃ tu dvayodbhavaḥ // 1.19 //

> Because of commonality of kind, object-range, and cognition, the eye and
> the other faculties have one-principle status even though they are paired.
> Their arising as a pair, however, is for beauty.

The Bhāṣya tests the count of eighteen principles against the bodily pairing
of eye, ear, and nose. It distinguishes the grounds of principial unity
from its stated explanation of paired bodily arrangement.

## 2. Continuous Sanskrit

The natural unit runs from the twenty-one-principle objection at printed
location 012.17 through the explanation of paired sites at 012.25–013.01.
The following statement that the bases (*skandhas*), essences (*āyatanas*),
and principles (*dhātus*) have been explained opens the inquiry into their
meanings in 1.20.

Word division and punctuation are editorial. The running source's `tra`,
`tasmad`, `cakṣuradīnaṃ`/`cakṣurādīnaṃ`, `anya thā`, and `vairupyaṃ`
are normalized to `tatra`, `tasmād`, `cakṣurādīnāṃ`, `anyathā`, and
`vairūpyaṃ`. Quotation marks standing for avagrahas are regularized.
In the closing compound, `nāsikāvila` is read provisionally as
`nāsikābila`, nostril-opening. The research report retains `vila` but
translates it as an opening. That lexical repair remains marked for review.
The source's `ubhayo rūpaviṣayatvāt` is retained as sandhi of `ubhayoḥ`
before `rūpa`.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

> But should there not be twenty-one principles, because the eye is twofold,
> and likewise the ear and nose? There should not. For, because of commonality
> of kind, object-range, and cognition, the eye and the others have
> one-principle status even though they are paired.
>
> Here there is commonality of kind because both have the nature of eye;
> commonality of object-range because both have visible form as their object;
> and commonality of cognition because both are supports of one eye-cognition.
> Therefore there is just one eye-principle. The same is to be applied to the
> ear and nose.
>
> Their arising as a pair, however, is for beauty. Even though the eye and
> the others have one-principle status, their occurrence as two is for the
> beauty of the bodily support. Otherwise, through the occurrence of only
> one eye-site, one ear-site, and one nostril-opening, there would be great
> disfigurement.

## 4. Movement of the Commentary

The opening objection would add one principle for the second member of each
of three pairs: eighteen plus three gives twenty-one. The reply rejects
bodily number as a sufficient ground for increasing the principle count.

```text
paired eyes, ears, and nasal faculties
    → proposed increase from eighteen to twenty-one
three commonalities
    → one principle for each pair
continued bodily duality
    → explained through the beauty of the bodily support
```

The objection and reply are distinguishable voices within the exposition.
No historical opponent or school is explicitly named. The account of beauty
is the commentary's stated explanation; it follows the determination of
one-principle status rather than supplying its ground.

## 5. Three Grounds of One-Principle Status

The ablative `jātigocaravijñānasāmānyāt` gives the reason for
`ekadhātutā`, the status of being one principle. The locative `dvitve` with
`api` concedes the paired condition. Principial unity and bodily duality
are therefore asserted together.

The prose divides the compound into three explanations, each referring to
both eyes through the dual `ubhayoḥ`:

| Commonality | Bhāṣya's reason | Significance for principle-unity |
|---|---|---|
| kind (`jāti`) | both have eye-nature | bodily distinction does not introduce another faculty-kind |
| object-range (`gocara`) | both have visible form as object | the same essence-field is shared |
| Reason (*vijñāna*; conventionally, cognition) | both support one eye-cognition | their relation to one Reason does not require two eye-principles |

`Jāti` receives the specific gloss of shared eye-nature. It does not mean
birth, caste, or merely a resemblance of outward shape here. `Gocara` is
controlled by `rūpaviṣayatva`, having visible form as object. This concerns
the kind of sensory object, not a claim that each eye has an identical
spatial view or receives every visible detail equally.

`Ekacakṣurvijñānāśrayatva` says that both are supports of one eye-vijñāna.
The conventional translation retains “one eye-cognition.” In the Organon
reading, *vijñāna* here is Reason: the common rational relation in which the
two supports are comprehended together. It does not mean an extra mental
substance, and it does not claim that both eyes must be active in every
episode of seeing.

All three grounds belong to the answer. The text does not isolate Reason as
the sole criterion and discard kind and object-range. Principle-unity is
articulated through their conjunction.

## 6. Applying the Reasoning to Ear and Nose

`Evaṃ ... api yojyam`, “the same is also to be applied,” directs an
extension of the eye argument. The Bhāṣya does not repeat each clause.
Expanded in the analysis, the ear pair shares ear-nature, sound as its
object, and the corresponding auditory *vijñāna* (conventionally,
cognition); the nasal pair shares the olfactory faculty's nature, odor as
its object, and the corresponding olfactory *vijñāna*. In Organon terms,
these are shared relations of Reason, not separate mental substances.

The reference to nostril-openings in the closing sentence concerns the
bodily sites. It should not undo the distinction from 1.09 between the
sensory faculty as material clarity and the gross anatomical site.
The present objection is generated by bodily pairing, but the reply's
criteria concern the faculty and its sensory relations.

Thus the number of bodily sites does not itself determine the number of
principles. As in 1.17, the classification specifies a role without adding
a separate mental substance. Here the issue is different: two bodily
instances sustain one eye-principle because the faculty-kind, object-range,
and Reason-relation are common.

## 7. The Explanation through Beauty

The second half of the verse introduces `śobhārtham`, for beauty. The
prose supplies the genitive `āśrayasya`, of the support, here the bodily
support whose arrangement is being discussed. `Dvayoḥ saṃbhavaḥ` states
the occurrence of two, while `ekadhātutve 'pi` preserves the already
established one-principle status.

The concluding counterfactual is expressed by `anyathā ... syāt`: otherwise,
there would be considerable `vairūpya`, disfigurement. The imagined single
sites explain what the passage means by its appeal to beauty. This is the
text's aesthetic judgment about bodily arrangement, retained as such.

“Symmetry” can help a reader understand the contrast, but the explicit
term is beauty. The translation does not replace it with a claim about
sensory efficiency, adaptive advantage, or physiological necessity. Those
would require a different explanation from the one given here.

The two halves therefore answer different questions: why the principle is
counted once, and why its bodily supports occur as a pair. A reason for the
latter is not automatically a reason for the former.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

1. The counting objection concerns three paired faculties and the proposed
   increase from eighteen to twenty-one.
2. The three commonalities are separately explained through faculty-nature,
   sensory object, and the corresponding *vijñāna* relation (conventionally,
   cognition).
3. `Ekadhātutā` preserves classificatory unity while admitting bodily duality.
4. The eye argument is expressly extended to ear and nose.
5. “One eye-cognition” is retained without importing a fuller physiological
   theory into the local statement.
6. Pairing is explained through the beauty of the bodily support; that
   explanation remains distinct from the grounds of principle-unity.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

**Idea-base and Reason**

This passage speaks of *vijñāna* in
`jātigocara-vijñāna-sāmānya` and specifies the shared relation as
`ekacakṣurvijñānāśrayatva`: both eyes are supports of one eye-vijñāna.
The conventional translation retains “cognition” for this local textual
claim. The Organon interpretation distinguishes the terms by level:

```text
vijñānaskandha  → Idea-base
vijñāna         → Reason
saṃjñā          → Reflection
```

This verse does not name the *vijñānaskandha*; the Idea-base / Reason
distinction places its terms in the wider system without inserting the
skandha into this passage. The Idea-base is the gathered systematic
determination; *vijñāna* itself, considered in its own role, is Reason, not
another name for that base. Reason is the rational articulation through
which common faculty-kind and common object-field are comprehended as one
principle. *Saṃjñā*, Reflection, is not a term in this verse and is not
inserted into its translation; it names the related reflective moment in
the wider Organon vocabulary. The comparative Yoga alignment places
*saṃjñā* nearer *savikalpa* and *vijñāna* nearer
*nirvitarka*. These are interpretive correspondences, not lexical
translations or claims made by this Bhāṣya. The distinction keeps Idea,
Reason, and Reflection from being flattened into inherited “consciousness”
or “cognition” terminology.

The Kantian-transcendental question is what warrants the judgment that two
numerically distinct supports belong to one eye-principle. The Bhāṣya gives
the conditions: common kind, common object-range, and a shared relation to
one eye-*vijñāna*. In Organon terms, the shared kind is read on the
base-side, the object-range on the essence-side, and Reason completes their
principial determination. The inference is not from two organs to two
principles, nor from a presupposed unity that ignores the two organs.

The reflective movement preserves both terms of the judgment: the supports
remain two, while their common determinations ground one principle. Their
difference is not erased by unity, and their numerical difference does not
multiply the principial account. This is the Organon's Hegelian reading of
the Bhāṣya's threefold ground, not a translation claim about its historical
philosophical vocabulary.

The practical and reflective moments also remain in view. The reason for
principial unity is the three commonalities; the reason the bodily support
occurs in pairs is beauty. The latter is the Bhāṣya's practical-aesthetic
explanation, not a substitute for the classificatory ground. Reflection
keeps the two questions related but distinct. The passage therefore does
not abstract the Idea-base from embodied practice: it interprets practical
embodiment through the principial relation without reducing one to the other.
