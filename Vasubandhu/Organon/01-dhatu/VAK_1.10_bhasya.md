# VAK_1.10 Bhāṣya — Counts Within the Five Meanings

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> rūpaṃ dvidhā viṃśatidhā śabdas tv aṣṭavidhaḥ rasaḥ /
>
> ṣoḍhā caturvidho gandhaḥ spṛśyam ekādaśātmakam // 1.10 //

From [`VAK_1.10.md`](./VAK_1.10.md):

Visible Form is twofold and twentyfold. Sound is eightfold. Taste is
sixfold. Odor is fourfold. The tangible has eleven as its nature.

The commentary elaborates the counts and then examines whether sensory
Principles arise from one or many substances and how their Conditions
retain particular character. Span: Pradhan
`[006|07]`–`[007|28]`. The next topic is *avijñapti*.

## 3. Continuous Translation

> Visible Form is twofold: color and configuration. Color is fourfold,
> beginning with blue. Configuration is eightfold, from long through
> uneven. That same visible-Form Essence is further enumerated as
> twentyfold: blue, yellow, red, white, long, short, round, circular,
> raised, lowered, even, uneven, cloud, smoke, dust, mist, shadow,
> sunlight, light, and darkness. Some include the sky as one color and
> recite twenty-one.
>
> Even is an even configuration; uneven is an uneven configuration. Mist
> is fog. Sunlight is the sun's radiance. Light is the radiance of the
> moon, stars, fire, herbs, and jewels. Shadow is where visible Forms are
> seen; darkness is the reverse. The rest is not elaborated because it is
> easy to understand.
>
> There is a visible-Form Essence known by color and not by configuration:
> blue, yellow, red, white, shadow, sunlight, light, and darkness. There is
> one known by configuration and not by color. The intervening line is
> damaged in the witness; it concerns a portion whose nature is bodily
> *vijñapti*. There is one known in both ways: the remaining visible-Form
> Essence. Others say only sunlight and light are known by color, since
> long and the other configurations are discerned in blue and the other
> colors.
>
> Objection: How can one substance be known in both ways?
>
> Answer: Because both are discerned there. Here *vid* means knowing, not
> existence.
>
> Then the same consequence would follow for bodily *vijñapti*. The
> visible-Form Essence has been explained.
>
> Sound, however, is eightfold. It is fourfold according to whether it is
> caused by appropriated or unappropriated Great Elements and whether it
> is designated sentient or non-sentient; it is then eightfold through
> agreeable and disagreeable. Sound of a hand or voice is caused by
> appropriated Great Elements; sound of wind, trees, or a river by
> unappropriated Great Elements. Vocal *vijñapti* is designated sentient;
> the other sound is designated non-sentient. Others say that sound can
> also be caused by both appropriated and unappropriated Great Elements,
> as sound arising from the conjunction of hand and drum. This is not
> accepted, just as one color-atom is not held to depend on two sets of
> four Elements. Sound has been explained.
>
> Taste is sixfold: sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter, and astringent.
> Odor is fourfold because pleasant and unpleasant are each even and
> uneven. In the treatise, however, it is threefold: “pleasant,
> unpleasant, and even.”
>
> The tangible has eleven as its nature. It has the nature of eleven
> substances: the four Great Elements, smoothness, roughness, heaviness,
> lightness, cold, hunger, and thirst. The Great Elements will be
> explained later. Smoothness is softness; roughness is harshness.
> Heaviness is that by which things are weighed against one another;
> lightness is the reverse. Cold gives rise to the desire for warmth, and
> hunger to the desire to eat; the effect is named in place of its cause.
> In the Form Realm (*rūpadhātu*), hunger and thirst are absent; the rest
> are present. Garments there are not weighed one by one, but are weighed
> when collected. Harmful cold is absent; supportive cold is said to be
> present.
>
> Of the manifold visible Form described here, sometimes the eye-Principle
> arises from one substance, when its kind is distinguished, and sometimes
> from many, when it is not distinguished—like seeing from afar an array
> of an army with many colors and configurations, or a heap of jewels.
> The ear-Principle and the rest are to be understood in the same way.
> Some say the body-Principle arises from at most five tangibles: the four
> Great Elements and one such as smoothness. Others say it arises from all
> eleven.
>
> Objection: If the object is the collected whole, the five groups of
> Principles would have common Conditions, not Conditions of particular
> character.
>
> Answer: There is no fault. They are held to have Conditions of
> particular character relative to the specific character of the Essence,
> not relative to the specific character of a substance.
>
> This is examined: when the body and tongue Faculties reach a Condition
> simultaneously, which Principle arises first? The one whose Condition
> is keener. When the Conditions are equal, the tongue-Principle arises
> first, because the continuum is inclined toward eating.
>
> The five Faculty-meanings and the way they are apprehended have been
> explained.

## 5. The Twenty

The twenty comprise:

```text
four colors:
    blue, yellow, red, white

eight configurations:
    long, short, round, circular, raised, lowered, even, uneven

eight further visible-Form determinations:
    cloud, smoke, dust, mist, shadow, sunlight, light, darkness
```

Some add the sky as a twenty-first item. The Bhāṣya also distinguishes
visible-Form Essences known through color, through configuration, or
through both. Its intervening line on what is known through configuration
alone is damaged; no repair is imposed. The objection that one substance
cannot be known both ways is answered by the meaning of *vid*: knowing,
not a claim that the substance exists in two ways.

## 6. Sound, Taste, Odor, and Tangible

Sound's fourfold division crosses two distinctions: appropriated or
unappropriated Great Elements as cause, and sentient or non-sentient
designation. Agreeable and disagreeable further yield eight. A proposed
sound caused jointly by appropriated and unappropriated Elements is
rejected by analogy with one color-atom not depending on two sets of four
Elements.

Taste is sixfold. Odor is fourfold in the verse, while the cited treatise
gives three. The difference is retained rather than harmonized.

The tangible has eleven: four Great Elements and seven further
determinations—smoothness, roughness, heaviness, lightness, cold, hunger,
and thirst. Hunger and thirst are absent in the Form Realm. The Bhāṣya
explains cold and hunger through the desire they occasion, naming the
effect for the cause.

## 7. One and Many Substances; Specific Conditions

The eye-Principle may arise in relation to one substance when its kind is
distinguished, or to many when no such distinction is made. The examples
are a variegated army or a heap of jewels seen from afar. The ear-Principle
and the rest are treated analogously. The account of the body-Principle
remains disputed: some limit its Conditions to five tangibles, others
include all eleven.

An objection says that if the gathered whole is the object, the five
groups of Principles must take common Conditions rather than Conditions
of particular character. The reply locates the particular character at
the level of Essence, not in the unique substance. This preserves
particular-character Conditions without requiring each Condition to
correspond to one isolated substance.

When body and tongue Faculties reach Conditions together, the Principle
whose Condition is keener arises first. If they are equal, the
tongue-Principle arises first because the continuum is directed toward
eating.

## 8. Decisions for the Kārikā

1. Visible Form is twofold as color and configuration and is enumerated
   as twenty; some add a twenty-first, the sky.
2. Sound is eightfold, taste sixfold, odor fourfold in the verse and
   threefold in the treatise.
3. The tangible has eleven as its nature: four Great Elements and seven
   further determinations.
4. *Vid* here concerns knowing, not existence; it is not the noun
   *vijñāna*, rendered Principle.
5. The sensory Principles may depend on one or many Conditions without
   becoming general in the relevant Essence-specific determination.
6. The damaged line remains marked and unrepaired.

## 9. Interpretation

The Kantian-transcendental question begins with the objection about one
substance known through both color and configuration. The Bhāṣya answers
at the level of knowing: *vid* concerns how a visible-Form Essence is
determined, not a second mode of existence. One appearance can therefore
bear distinct determinations without becoming two substances.

The later objection similarly asks whether a collected object yields only
common Conditions. The answer distinguishes the particular character of
an Essence from the unique character of a substance. In the Organon
framework, the counts articulate the Seen within the Form Base, while
Essence-specific Conditions ground the sensory Principles' particular
determination. This is a project-level Kantian comparison, not a historical
attribution or a claim that the Bhāṣya states Kant's theory.

This count is one specification within the larger `<Base, Essence,
Principle>` Concept. It determines content within the Form Base; it does
not turn that content into a second Base or exhaust the Dharma System.
