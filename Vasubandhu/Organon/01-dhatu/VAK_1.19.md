# VAK_1.19

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

> जातिगोचरविज्ञानसामान्यादेकधातुता ।
>
> द्वित्वेऽपि चक्षुरादीनां शोभार्थं तु द्वयोद्भवः ॥ १.१९ ॥

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

> jātigocaravijñānasāmānyād ekadhātutā /
>
> dvitve 'pi cakṣurādīnāṃ śobhārthaṃ tu dvayodbhavaḥ // 1.19 //

## 3. Lexical Analysis

```text
jātigocaravijñānasāmānyāt → jāti-gocara-vijñāna-sāmānyāt
ekadhātutā                 → eka-dhātutā
dvitve 'pi                 → dvitve api
cakṣurādīnām               → cakṣus-ādīnām
śobhārtham                 → śobhā-artham
dvayodbhavaḥ               → dvaya-udbhavaḥ
```

| Form | Morphology | Lexical force here |
|---|---|---|
| jāti | feminine noun in compound | shared faculty-kind, as specified by the Bhāṣya |
| gocara | masculine noun in compound | object-range; the Bhāṣya specifies visible form for the eye |
| vijñāna | neuter noun in compound | corresponding cognition |
| sāmānyāt | ablative neuter singular | because of commonality or shared determination |
| eka-dhātutā | nominative feminine singular abstract noun | status of constituting one principle |
| dvitve | locative neuter singular | despite or in the condition of duality |
| api | concessive particle | even though |
| cakṣus-ādīnām | genitive plural | of the eye and the analogous paired faculties |
| śobhā-artham | accusative neuter singular used adverbially | for the sake of beauty or symmetry |
| tu | contrastive particle | however; changes from classification to bodily arrangement |
| dvaya-udbhavaḥ | nominative masculine singular | arising or occurrence as a pair |

The Bhāṣya restricts `cakṣus-ādīni` here to the paired eye, ear, and nose
faculties. Tongue and body do not generate the proposed increase from
eighteen to twenty-one principles.

## 4. Grammar

The first line gives an ablative cause:

```text
jāti-gocara-vijñāna-sāmānyāt
    because of commonality of
        kind,
        object-range,
        and corresponding cognition

eka-dhātutā
    there is one-principle status
```

`Dvitve api` is concessive: the bodily faculty occurs in two sites, yet this
numerical duality does not overturn principial unity.

The Bhāṣya distributes the three commonalities for the eye:

```text
two eyes
    share eye-nature                 → base / faculty-kind
    have visible form as object      → essence / object-field
    support one eye-cognition        → principle / cognitive relation
```

The Bhāṣya says “one eye-cognition”; this establishes the shared cognitive
relation for the count without specifying whether both supports are active
in every particular act of seeing.

The same construction is to be applied analogically to the two ears and two
nostril sites. The three moments must be considered together: shared kind
alone, shared object-field alone, or the cognition term alone is not offered
as the sufficient ground.

`Tu` marks a change of question in the second line. The first line explains
why two bodily sites count as one principle. The second explains why that
one principle has two bodily sites:

```text
classificatory unity
    → base + essence + principle

paired embodiment
    → practical bodily arrangement for beauty
```

The aesthetic explanation is not a fourth criterion of principial unity.

## 5. Translation

### Close syntactic construe

> Because of commonality of kind, object-range, and cognition, the eye and the others have one-principle status even though dual. Their arising as a pair, however, is for the sake of beauty.

### Bhāṣya-informed translation

> The two eyes constitute one eye-principle because they share one faculty-kind, have visible form as their object, and are supports of one eye-cognition. The same reasoning applies to the paired ears and nasal faculties. Their bodily occurrence in pairs is said to serve the beauty of the bodily support; it does not multiply the principles.

The second translation distinguishes paired bodily instances from the
principial determination they jointly support. “Base” here renders
*skandha* in the Organon vocabulary; the bodily *āśraya* remains a support,
not a skandha-base.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> One-principle status is not numerical singleness. Multiple bodily sites form one principial determination when they share an intrinsic kind, an object-range, and a corresponding mode of cognition. Principle is therefore a unity of lawful determination rather than a count of visible organs.

**Organon reconstruction: empirical plurality and principial unity**

As an Organon mapping—not as lexical equivalences in the verse—the shared
faculty-kind functions on the base-side, the shared object-range on the
essence-side, and the cognition relation on the principle-side of the
machine.

The Kantian-transcendental question is: what warrants counting two bodily
supports as one principle? The verse does not infer unity from anatomy or
impose it by convention. It gives three grounds: common faculty-kind
(read by the Organon as base-determination), common object-range (read as
essence-determination), and the shared relation to one cognition (read as
principial closure). Their conjunction is the condition under which the
one-principle judgment is justified.

The Hegelian-reflective movement preserves both sides of the determination.
The two supports remain numerically distinct; through their common kind,
field, and cognitive relation, that plurality is comprehended as one
principle. Unity does not erase difference, and difference does not force a
second principle. This is the Organon reconstruction of the verse's logic,
not terminology stated by the Kārikā.

The practical and reflective questions are also kept distinct. “Why one
principle?” is answered by the three commonalities. “Why two bodily sites?”
is answered by the Bhāṣya's stated reason, beauty of the bodily support.
The latter is not the transcendental ground of classification, but it is a
real determination of embodied life in the commentary. Reflection keeps
these explanations in relation without substituting one for the other.

VAK 1.18 established inclusion through own-nature and distinction from
another's nature. VAK 1.19 now determines how a common base, essence, and
cognitive relation ground one principle across more than one bodily instance.

## 8. Logical Determination

The objector proposes an anatomical counting rule:

```text
18 principles
+ second eye
+ second ear
+ second nostril site
= 21 principles
```

Vasubandhu rejects the inference:

```text
NumericallyDistinct(x, y)
    ↛ PrincipleDistinct(x, y)
```

For these paired faculties, the three commonalities establish one-principle
status:

```text
PairedFacultySupports(x, y)
    ∧ SameObjectRange(x, y)
    ∧ SameFacultyKind(x, y)
    ∧ SupportsOneCorrespondingCognition(x, y)
    → SamePrinciple(x, y)
```

For the eyes:

```text
EyeNature(leftEye) = EyeNature(rightEye)
ObjectRange(leftEye) = VisibleForm = ObjectRange(rightEye)
CommonCognitionRelation(leftEye, rightEye) = support of eye-cognition
    → OneEyePrinciple
```

The explanatory levels remain distinct:

```text
Why one principle?
    → shared kind, range, and cognition

Why two bodily sites?
    → symmetry of the support
```

The method therefore determines principles intensionally and relationally
rather than by gross anatomical enumeration.

## 9. Interpretive Note

VAK 1.19 is a worked test of principial determination within the chapter's
setup. The system encounters an apparent counterexample: if classification
follows what a dharma is, should two eyes not constitute two eye-principles?
The answer refines `svabhāva`. Own-nature is not numerical individuality.
It is grasped here through the common faculty-kind, object-range, and
cognition relation.

This is an important reason the principle is not an externally counted
thing. A merely outward count sees two organs and demands two principles.
The Organon reconstruction comprehends the two as moments of one visual
architecture:

```text
shared faculty-kind / base
    ↕
shared visible object-field / essence
    ↕
shared visual cognition-relation / principle
```

The cognitive criterion is essential but not solitary. Vasubandhu gives three
irreducible grounds—kind, object-range, and cognition. We should therefore
not rewrite the verse as though cognition alone produces the unity. The
principle is determined through the structured correlation of all three.

This gives the Organon method a second rule:

```text
do not multiply principles merely because their empirical supports are many;
determine whether kind, range, and cognition are genuinely different
```

The remark about beauty belongs to the practical embodiment of the faculty,
not to the ground of its principial unity. It is not disposable as an
embarrassing aside: it distinguishes why the body is arranged in pairs from
why the pairs count as one principle.
