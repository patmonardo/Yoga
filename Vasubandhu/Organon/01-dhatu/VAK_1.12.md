## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> The Elements are the Earth Principle and the Water, Fire, and Wind Principles. They are established in functions beginning with support: hardness, cohesion, heat, and impulsion.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> The four Great Elements are the Earth, Water, Fire, and Wind Principles, each sustaining its own-character and derived Form. In that order, their functions are support, gathering, ripening, and spreading (growth and extension), while their own-characters are hardness, cohesion, heat, and impulsion.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

Again, two lines. The first names the four. The second is function, then own-character.

1.11 said depending on the Great Elements. This verse says which. Earth, Water, Fire, and Wind Principles. Each holds its own-character and derived Form. Each is a Principle, not a Base or Essence.

They hold their own-character and derived Form. That is why each is a Principle. Greatness is two readings, both kept. Magnitude, as support of all other Form. Or a great gathering in the masses, where that Principle operates prominently. *Skandha* in that sentence is the mass. Not one of the five.

The functions are support, gathering, ripening, and spreading. Spreading is increase and extension. The own-characters are hardness, cohesion, heat, and impulsion. Impulsion is that by which the stream of Great Elements is driven to arise in another place, as a lamp-flame moves.

The treatises say the Wind Principle is lightness of movement. That lightness is also said to be derived Form. Therefore Wind is the Dharma whose own-character is impulsion. The function makes the character evident. Not the reverse, and not a fifth Element.

The next question distinguishes worldly Form from the Principle itself. Not this verse.
