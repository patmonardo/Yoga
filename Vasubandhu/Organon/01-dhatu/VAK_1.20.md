# VAK_1.20

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

> राश्यायद्वारगोत्रार्थाः स्कन्धायतनधातवः ।
>
> मोहेन्द्रियरुचित्रैधात्तिस्रः स्कन्धादिदेशनाः ॥ १.२० ॥

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

> rāśyāyadvāragotrārthāḥ skandhāyatanadhātavaḥ /
>

## 4. Grammar

The first line consists of a subject and a distributive predicate:

```text
skandha-āyatana-dhātavaḥ
    are respectively
rāśi-āya-dvāra-gotra-arthāḥ
```

The Bhāṣya unfolds each compact meaning.

For Essence:

```text
citta-caittānām āyaṃ tanvanti
    they extend the arrival or occurrence
    of mind and mental factors

therefore:
Essence = functional access-gateway
```

For Principle, the Bhāṣya uses the image of one mountain containing distinct
mines of iron, copper, silver, and gold:

```text
gotra = ākara
      = mine, source, generative deposit
```

Within one support or continuum, the eighteen Principles are eighteen such
source-kinds. A conditioned Principle functions as a homogeneous cause
(`sabhāga-hetu`) of a subsequent occurrence of its own kind.

An objection notes that this generative definition would exclude the
unconditioned. The alternative teachers therefore generalize Principle as the
`jāti` or `svabhāva` of each of the eighteen dharmas. The grammar of `gotra`
must consequently retain two connected senses:

```text
conditioned Principle
    → source reproducing its own kind

Principle generally
    → intrinsic kind or nature
```

The second line gives an ablative cause: because those to be trained differ
threefold in delusion, Faculty, and inclination, there are three pedagogical
expositions. `Tisraḥ` agrees with the feminine plural `deśanāḥ`.

## 5. Translation

### Close syntactic construe

> Base, Essence, and Principle have respectively the meanings of collection,
> gateway of mental extension, and source-kind. Because delusion, Faculty,
> and inclination are each threefold, there are the three teachings
> beginning with Base.

### Bhāṣya-informed translation

> A Base is a conceptual gathering; an Essence is an access-gateway through
> which consciousness and mental factors extend; and a Principle is an
> intrinsic source-kind, like a distinct
> mine within a mountain and, for conditioned factors, a homogeneous cause
> of its own kind. The Buddha teaches the Dharma through these three
> presentations because those to be trained differ in what confuses them,
> in Faculty, and in preference for brief, intermediate, or extensive
> exposition.

The Bhāṣya insists that Base retain its scriptural collective meaning:
it is a conceptual designation (`prajñapti`), not an additional substance
over and above its constituents. This generic meaning does not erase the
distinct classification of the Cognition Base.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> A Dharma is articulated through three major top-level systems:
> **Base, Essence, and Principle**. Base gathers a manifold as a collection.
> Essence opens the access-gateway through which consciousness and mental
> factors arise and extend; Principle determines source-kind. These are the
> three determinations of one Dharma
> System, not nested layers or independent substances. In conditioned
> dharmas, source-kind can continue through homogeneous causation; the
> unconditioned requires the broader determination of intrinsic kind. The
> teaching presents this system in forms suited to learners' differences.

This is the Organon-level formulation of the verse, not a claim that the
Sanskrit compounds literally say “three top-level systems.” The Bhāṣya's
definitions ground the three functions; the complete Dharma Concept is
`<Base, Essence, Principle>`.
The established census is five Bases, twelve Essences, and eighteen
Principles: these counts articulate the three systems without turning them
into independent ontologies.

## 8. Logical Determination

The verse gives three respective meanings; the Organon expresses their
relation as one triadic Dharma Concept:

```text
Dharma(x)
    → DefinedConcept(x, <Base, Essence, Principle>)

Base(x)
    → gathers(x, collection)

Essence(x)
    → opensAccessFor(x, consciousnessAndMentalFactors)

Principle(x)
    → determines(x, sourceKind)
```

For conditioned principles:

```text
ConditionedDhatu(d)
∧ HasKind(d, k)
∧ FunctionsAs(d, HomogeneousCause)
    → MayProduceLaterOccurrenceOfKind(d, k)
```

For the complete eighteen-principle system:

```text
Dhatu(d)
    → HasIntrinsicKind(d)

Unconditioned(d)
    ↛ CausallyReproducesOwnKind(d)
```

Thus causal sourcehood is strong but not sufficient to define every
Principle.

The three teachings correspond in order to three learner profiles:

| Teaching | Primary confusion | Capacity | Preferred extent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base | mental factors grasped as one undifferentiated personal unity | sharp | brief |
| Essence | form | middling | intermediate |
| Principle | both form and mind | weak | extensive |

Formally:

```text
LearnerCondition(moha, capacity, inclination)
    → SelectPedagogicalForm(Base | Essence | Principle)
```

The Dharma System remains one while its exposition varies.

## 9. Interpretive Note

VAK 1.20 gives the three systems their respective functional meanings:

```text
Base
    = collection

Essence
    = access-gate

Principle
    = intrinsic source-kind
```

The Organon reads these not as nested sublayers or parallel inventories,
but as three major top-level systems within the single Dharma System. Each
Dharma has the triadic Defined Concept `<Base, Essence, Principle>`: one
Dharma considered through three distinct determinations. The verse's
respective glosses specify how those systems function here; they do not
exhaust the full project-level account.

The Principle system is highly differentiated, not a superior substance
standing by itself. The Bhāṣya assigns its extensive teaching to trainees
confused about both form and mind; it does not rank them as intellectually
superior. The three systems are distinct, but the passage does not make any
one of them the whole Dharma.

The mine analogy clarifies Principle as source-kind, not a container of
facts. For conditioned dharmas, this kind can continue through homogeneous
causation:

```text
determinate kind
    → homogeneous causal continuity
    → new occurrence of that kind
```

The unconditioned objection limits this productive account: unconditioned
dharmas also receive Principle classification, although they do not
reproduce a later occurrence through homogeneous causation. Source-kind
therefore cannot be reduced to causal production alone.

The pedagogical conclusion shows why one Dharma System can be taught through
different determinations. Base, Essence, and Principle are not rival
ontologies or successive stages that every learner must traverse. The
Bhāṣya relates their presentations, in order, to differences in delusion,
Faculty, and inclination; that correspondence adapts exposition without
turning the three systems into interchangeable terms.
