## 6. Feeling

Feeling is undergoing. Three tones: pleasant, painful, neither. Six groups,
by contact, from the eye through mind. The tones are how it is undergone. The
groups are the contact it is born from. Not nine bases. No order against
reflection is stated.

## 7. Reflection

The nature is the taking up of the mark. The commentary gives the marks:
blue and yellow, long and short, woman and man, pleasant and painful, and the
transmitted *mitramitra*. The operation is reflection. The English does not
replace the Sanskrit.

*Asau saṃjñāskandhaḥ* gathers it as the reflection-base. *Vedanāvat* transfers
the six groups. It does not transfer the three tones, and it does not identify
the two bases.

Worldly designation in 1.13 was a name for color and configuration. This
reflection is the taking up of the mark. Not the same word in force.

## 9. Interpretation

*Te eva* is the hinge. Changing the arrangement does not create another organ
or another meaning. Ten essences, and the same ten principles. Identity is
kept. The arrangements are not two inventories.

Organ, here, because an organized being is in view. The five serve. Meaning is
what they are set toward. Essence is the arrangement of that pair. Principle
is the same pair under the chapter word. Relation is the later grade. It is
not the English of this verse.

Feeling undergoes. Reflection takes the mark. Blue is a mark. Long is a mark.
The mark is not yet the principle. The six groups run from eye-contact through
mind-contact. Mind is the sixth support of the group. Not a seventh substance,
and not stated as such here.

The continuity of 1.13 is absent from the ten. It remains in the form-base,
and it is not given an essence or a principle in this verse. That placement
is the next study. Not opened.

## 9. Interpretation

The first line offers a precise identity constraint: *te eva*, “these
very ones.” The five Faculties and five meanings already present in the
Form Base are arranged as ten Essences and the same ten Principles. A
Kantian-transcendental question is how one content can remain
identifiable through distinct determinations without turning those
determinations into separate inventories. This is an Organon question,
not a claim that the Bhāṣya states Kantian doctrine.

In the project's system, each Dharma is defined through
`<Base, Essence, Principle>`. The ten Essences and ten Principles here
are only ten of the full twelve Essences and eighteen Principles; this
verse does not enumerate the complete system or attain its
Concept-level closure. That closure belongs to the reciprocating
relation among the six *vijñāna*-Principles. Essence is not itself
Concept, and *dhātu* is Principle, not a translation of Concept or of
a Kantian category.

The latter half opens two further determinations. Feeling as undergoing
can be compared, provisionally, with receptivity, but not identified with
Kant's faculty of sensibility. The Bhāṣya distinguishes three tones from
six groups classified by contact. The first describes how Feeling is
undergone; the second specifies its contact-born grouping. Neither set
should be collapsed into the other.

Reflection's taking up of marks invites comparison with Kantian
synthesis of apprehension and recognition, but *saṃjñā* is not thereby
Kantian apperception or a synonym for Concept. The source states a
specific operation and gives examples; the Organon may ask how
recognition of marks relates sensible presentation to determinate
judgment. The comparison remains interpretive. In particular, the
worldly designation of 1.13 and the mark-taking defined here are related
questions, not interchangeable uses of *saṃjñā*.

The Form Base account closes before the definitions of Feeling and
Reflection; Formations follows in 1.15. The sequence is a transition
within the five-Base system, not a new flat inventory of Essences or
Principles.
