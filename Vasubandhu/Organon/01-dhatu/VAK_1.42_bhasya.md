# VAK_1.42 Bhāṣya — Does the Eye or Visual Cognition See?

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> cakṣuḥ paśyati rūpāṇi sabhāgaṃ na tadāśritam /
>
> vijñānaṃ dṛśyate rūpaṃ na kilāntaritaṃ yataḥ // 1.42 //

> The functionally participating Eye sees visible Forms, not the
> Cognition dependent upon it; reportedly, obstructed visible Form
> is not seen.

This states the position defended at the beginning of the discussion.
*Kila*, “reportedly,” keeps the stated reason attributable; the
Bhāṣya then subjects it to objections. The entire exchange cannot
be reduced to the anchor's affirmative thesis.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

But if the Eye does not ascertain, how is it seeing? In the sense
of disclosing visible Form. For the Eye sees visible Forms.

If the Eye sees, then even the Eye of someone engaged in another
Cognition would see. — Not every Eye sees. — Which, then? — The
functionally participating Eye. It sees when accompanied by visual
Cognition, not at another time.

Then let the Cognition supported by the Eye itself be what sees.
— It cannot be established that the Cognition dependent upon it sees.
— Why? — Because, reportedly, visible Form screened off by something
is not seen. For visible Form concealed by a wall or the like is
not seen. If Cognition saw, since it is non-resistant and encounters
no resistance in walls and the like, it would see even concealed Form.

But visual Cognition does not arise at all with regard to concealed
Form. How could it see without having arisen? — Why, then, does it
not arise? On the account that the Eye sees, the Eye's resistance
means that it does not operate on concealed Form. Cognition's failure
to arise is then intelligible, since it operates on the same field
as its support.

Does the Eye, like the Body Faculty, have a field reached by contact,
so that it would not see what is concealed? — [It does not see it]
because of its resistance. — Then how is Form seen when glass, a sheet
of mica, crystal, or water intervenes? The failure to see concealed
Form is [not] due to the Eye's resistance. What, then, is the reason?
Where light is not blocked, visual Cognition arises even with regard
to covered Form. Where light is blocked, it does not arise; because
it has not arisen, the covered Form is not seen.

What, then, of the sūtra's statement, “having seen visible Forms
with the Eye”? — Its intention here is “with that as support,” just
as it says “having cognized Essences with Mind.” Mind does not
cognize Essences, since it is past. What does? Mental Cognition.
Alternatively, the operation of what depends on a support is
figuratively attributed to the support, as when one says “the
platforms cry out.” Likewise, a sūtra speaks of desirable and
pleasing Forms “to be cognized by the Eye”; yet they are not
cognized by the Eye.

A sūtra also says, “The Eye, brahmin, is a door precisely for the
seeing of visible Forms.” From this it is understood that Cognition
sees through the Eye as a door. — There “door” is a designation
for seeing. — That does not fit: [it would amount to saying]
“the Eye is seeing for the seeing of visible Forms.”

If Cognition sees, what cognizes, and what difference is there between
the two? — The very Cognition of visible Form is its seeing. Just
as a certain *prajñā* is said both to see and to understand, so a
certain Cognition is said both to see and to cognize.

Others say that one should ask: “If the Eye sees, what distinct act
of seeing belongs to the Eye as agent?” — This is no objection.
For if it is admitted that Cognition cognizes, without a distinction
there between agent and act, the same applies here.

Others again say: “Visual Cognition is seeing. The Eye is said to
see because it is the support of that seeing, just as a bell is said
to sound because it is the support of the sound.”

But then, because the Eye is the support of Cognition, it would follow
that the Eye cognizes. — That does not follow. In ordinary usage,
that Cognition is established as “seeing.” When it arises, a Form
is said to have been seen, not to have been cognized. The Vibhāṣā
also says: “What is reached by the Eye and experienced by visual
Cognition is called seen.” Therefore one says that the Eye sees,
not that it cognizes. Cognition, however, is said to cognize visible
Form merely through its presence, just as the sun is called the
maker of day.

Here the Sautrāntikas say: “Why this chewing of empty space? Visual
Cognition arises dependent on the Eye and visible Forms. Who sees
there, or what is seen? These are only Essences and only cause and
result, without activity. For practical discourse, figurative
attributions are made as one chooses. One should not insist upon
‘the Eye sees’ or ‘Cognition cognizes.’ For the Blessed One said
that one should not insist upon local expression or overstep the
world's designation.”

This, however, is the established position of the Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣikas:
the Eye sees, the Ear hears, the Nose smells, the Tongue tastes,
the Body touches, and Mind cognizes.

The paired dashes distinguish questions and answers without assigning
every anonymous reply to a named school. Brackets mark contextual
supplements at compressed or defective places. “Without activity”
renders the Sautrāntika *nirvyāpāra*; its positive account of dependence
and cause and result remains part of the same statement.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The initial thesis is qualified: the Eye sees when accompanied by
its Cognition. The questioner proposes making that Cognition what sees.
The defense of the Eye invokes obstruction; the reply distinguishes
Cognition's non-resistance from the conditions required for its arising.

The transparent-media example presses that reply. The explanation
through blocked or unblocked light challenges the proposed sufficiency
of the Eye's resistance. The research summary labels this explanation
a Vaibhāṣika response and thereby obscures the argumentative turn.
The local missing negative at 30.18 must remain visible, but the
sequence of the objection and *kiṃ tarhi* supports the negative
construal used here.

The discussion then turns to scriptural language, transferred
attribution, and ordinary usage. Several interlocutors are introduced
only as “others.” The text explicitly names the Sautrāntikas near
the end and explicitly identifies the closing thesis as that of the
Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣikas. These named positions should remain distinct
from anonymous arguments and from Vasubandhu's reporting of them.

## 5. The Participating Eye and Obstruction

*Sabhāgam* qualifies the nominative neuter *cakṣuḥ*. It is the
functionally participating Eye that sees. Likewise, *tadāśritam
vijñānam* is nominative neuter in the rejected alternative, “[not] the
Cognition dependent upon it.” Their endings do not make these
accusative complements of *paśyati*; *rūpāṇi* is its direct complement.

*Savijñānaka* means accompanied by Cognition. It should not be
converted into the technical association of citta with mental factors:
the Eye remains a Faculty Principle within the Form grouping. The
qualification connects actual seeing with the relevant Cognition,
rather than making possession of an Eye Principle sufficient for seeing
at every moment.

The obstruction argument and its reply differ in what they explain:

| Argument | Explanatory step |
|---|---|
| Defense of the Eye | Non-resistant Cognition would not itself be blocked by a wall; the Eye's resistance supplies the restriction |
| Reply | Cognition that has not arisen cannot see; non-resistance does not guarantee arising |
| Further defense | Cognition shares its support's field, explaining non-arising through the Eye's failure to operate |
| Transparent-media challenge | Intervening conditions do not always prevent vision; blockage of light distinguishes the cases |

*Antaritam* concerns the visible Form screened off, not the intervening
Form itself. The research anchor's “an intervening visible Form is not
seen” reverses that relation. The glass and mica example also prevents
all intervening conditions from being treated as an opaque barrier.
Here *abhrapaṭala* is provisionally read as a sheet of mica in the
series of transparent media, rather than the research translation's
“cloud.”

## 6. Support, Door, and Transferred Attribution

The argument from “with the Eye” is answered through *āśraya*, support.
The instrumental can indicate the support through which dependent
Cognition occurs. Alternatively, *āśritakarma*, the operation of what
is supported, is attributed to its support through *upacāra*.
“The platforms cry out” supplies the example: their occupants cry out.

The Mind analogy invokes its past status in the support relation.
This local argument is not a new assertion that the Mind Principle has
no future or present members; 1.39 explicitly discussed that separate
classification across the times.

The door passage contains both the inference to Cognition's seeing
and a rejoinder identifying “door” with seeing, followed by criticism
of that rejoinder. The research excerpt omits the rejoinder and its
criticism. They are restored here, with the damaged last sentence
marked for review.

The bell analogy and its objection likewise need their full exchange.
If supporting seeing warrants “the Eye sees,” why does supporting
Cognition not warrant “the Eye cognizes”? The reply appeals to
established usage and the Vibhāṣā's formulation. Its claim concerns
how the event is named; it does not eliminate the distinction between
support and dependent Cognition.

## 7. Seeing, Cognizing, and the Named Conclusions

One reply identifies visual Cognition with its seeing. It compares
the two predicates with saying that a certain *prajñā* both sees and
understands. Another question asks for an act of seeing distinct from
the Eye as agent; the answer points out that “Cognition cognizes” need
not posit a separate agent and act either.
These are different argumentative moves and should not be merged.

The Sautrāntika intervention shifts attention to dependent arising:
Eye and visible Forms condition visual Cognition. Its polemical
“chewing empty space” targets the preceding dispute over attribution.
Its caution against insisting on conventional expressions is followed
by a scriptural appeal concerning local usage. The positive content
is the stated relation of conditions and result; the passage should
not be organized around a generalized metaphysical negation.

The final sentence reports the Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣika assignment of the
six operations to the six Faculties. The connective *tu*, “however,”
preserves its contrast with the preceding account. Reporting this
as the school conclusion does not make it a conclusion jointly
accepted by all the voices in the discussion.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

- Qualify the Eye by participation with its Cognition.
- Construe the Eye and dependent Cognition as alternative subjects
  of seeing, not as accusative complements.
- Translate obstructed visible Form as what is not seen.
- Retain the reportive *kila* and the ensuing challenge to its reason.
- Distinguish a Cognition's non-resistance from its conditional arising.
- Restore the door rejoinder, agent/act exchange, bell objection,
  ordinary-usage reply, and closing scriptural appeal.
- Preserve explicit school attributions without inventing affiliations
  for every anonymous interlocutor.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage distinguishes causal dependence, operative participation,
and the grammatical attribution of an act. “The Eye sees” may be
defended as the Faculty's proper operation, explained through its role
as support, or treated as a conventional expression for a conditioned
event. The debate makes those differences explicit.

The Organon reading for this project is recursive. The Dhātus are Pure
Principle—Abhidharma itself, understood as the Principle Pipeline.
Secondary beings are impure *prajñā*: determinate outputs standing upon
a Principle. A Principle, in turn, stands upon its supporting
Principles. An output may be impure and still be called a Principle
because its principial status comes from standing within the Principle
Pipeline; principlehood and purity are not synonyms.

Applied here, the Eye Principle, visible Form field, and conditions of
illumination and non-obstruction ground visual Cognition. That local
Cognition should not be collapsed into the terminal *prajñā* of 1.41:
the pipeline has distinct relations and products. This recursive model
is the project's interpretation, not a claim stated by the Bhāṣya.
The source debate remains about where seeing is attributed, not about
the full metaphysics of the Principle Pipeline.
