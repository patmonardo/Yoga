# VAK_1.35 Bhāṣya — Elements, Element-Dependent Form, and Aggregation

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> spraṣṭavyaṃ dvividhaṃ śeṣā rūpiṇo nava bhautikāḥ /
>
> dharmadhātvekadeśaś ca saṃcitā daśa rūpiṇaḥ // 1.35 //

> The Tangible Principle is twofold. The other nine Form-bearing
> Principles are Element-dependent, as is a portion of the Essence
> Principle. The ten Form-bearing Principles are aggregated.

The predicate *bhautika* carries into “a portion of the Essence
Principle.” The Bhāṣya identifies that portion as avijñapti. The final
statement introduces aggregation as a separate classification.

## 3. Continuous Close Translation

How many Principles have the nature of Elements, and how many are
Element-dependent?

The Tangible Principle is twofold: Elements and Element-dependent Form.
The Elements are four; Element-dependent tangibles are sevenfold,
beginning with smoothness. [They are Element-dependent] because they
arise in the Elements.

The other nine Form-bearing Principles are Element-dependent: the five
Faculty Principles and four sensory-field Principles. These nine are
Element-dependent alone. A portion of the Essence Principle is also
Element-dependent: that designated avijñapti. The Citta Principles and
the Essence Principle apart from avijñapti are neither of these two.

The venerable Buddhadeva says, “The ten Essence Bases are only Elements.”
This is not so: the sūtra determines the Elements as four, with hardness
and the other characteristics; moreover, they are tangible. Hardness
and the like are not apprehended by the Eye Faculty and the other
[non-bodily] Faculties, nor are color and the like apprehended by the
Body Faculty.

It is also said in a sūtra: “The Eye Faculty, monk, is an internal
Essence Base: a clarity of Form dependent upon the four Great Elements,
[Form-bearing], not visible, resistant.” So likewise through the Body
Faculty. “Visible Forms, monk, are an external Essence Base: dependent
upon the four Great Elements, Form-bearing, visible, resistant. Sound,
monk, is an external Essence Base: dependent upon the four Great
Elements, Form-bearing, not visible, resistant.” Likewise smells and
tastes. “Tangibles, monk, are an external Essence Base: the four Great
Elements and Form dependent upon the four Great Elements, Form-bearing,
not visible, resistant.”

Since the Elements are included in only one portion of the Tangible
Essence Base, the remaining portion is clearly shown not to be Elements.

But what of the sūtra's statement, “Whatever in the fleshy mass of the
Eye Faculty is hard has the character of hardness”? This is an
instruction concerning the fleshy mass [occurring inseparably with that
Faculty].

“A person, monk, consists of six Principles”: in the account of descent
into the womb, this serves to show the fundamental constituents of a
being. The six contact Essence Bases are mentioned again; otherwise
mental factors would be absent.

Nor is it reasonable to admit that mental factors are just Citta. For
a sūtra says, “Recognition and feeling are mental Essences, following
Citta and dependent upon Citta”; and there are statements about Citta
with passion and the like. Thus the Principles' being Elements or
Element-dependent is as stated.

How many are aggregated, and how many unaggregated? The ten
Form-bearing Principles are aggregated: the five Faculty Principles
and five sensory-field Principles. Because they are aggregates of
atoms, the remaining Principles are not aggregated.

The bracketed “Form-bearing” and flesh-clause translations express
provisional construals, not secure restorations of the damaged text.
The text refers to an account of descent into the womb; no independent
identification of a particular sūtra edition is asserted here.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The exposition first gives the Element/Element-dependent Form
distinction. Buddhadeva is then named as holding that the ten Essence
Bases are only Elements. The commentary rejects that position through
Element characteristics, differentiated sensory apprehension, and an
extended sūtra citation.

Two further scriptural formulations are considered: hardness in the
fleshy Eye Faculty and the person as six Principles. The first is
answered by distinguishing the Faculty from its inseparably accompanying
Form.
The second receives a contextual explanation and leads to an argument
for distinguishing mental factors from citta.

The attribution of Buddhadeva's initial statement is explicit. The
subsequent scriptural objections are not individually assigned to a
named speaker. The study therefore presents them as objections within
the discussion rather than new verbatim claims attributed to him.
The assertion “as stated” closes that discussion before the question
about aggregation begins.

## 5. The Distribution of Element and Element-Dependent Form

The Tangible Principle comprises the four Elements and seven
Element-dependent tangibles, here abbreviated as “beginning with
smoothness.” The prose relies on the earlier enumeration rather than
repeating all seven. *Bhūteṣu bhavatvāt* explains *bhautika* as arising
in or depending upon the Elements. A single undifferentiated Form label
would fail to distinguish Element from Element-dependent Form.

The other nine Form-bearing Principles are five Faculties plus visible
Form, Sound, smell, and taste. Avijñapti belongs to the Essence Principle
and is also Element-dependent. The seven Citta Principles and the
remainder of the Essence Principle are neither Elements nor
Element-dependent Form.

The rival statement uses *āyatana*, rendered here as Essence Base,
whereas the governing inventory uses *dhātu*, Principle. Its ten are
the five sensory Faculty Essence Bases and five sensory-field Essence
Bases relevant to the argument. This shift of inventory vocabulary
should be retained rather than silently rewritten in the Sanskrit
quotation.

## 6. Dependence, Apprehension, and the Scriptural Replies

The first refutation joins two determinations. The four Elements have
specified characteristics, and those characteristics are apprehended
through touch. The other sensory fields cannot simply be substituted
for them: color is not apprehended by the Body Faculty as color, and
hardness is not apprehended by vision as hardness.

The sūtra formula reinforces the distinction through *upādāya*,
“depending upon.” For the Faculties and non-tangible sensory fields it
gives Form dependent on the Elements; for the Tangible Essence Base it
expressly includes both the Elements and Element-dependent Form. The
distinction is therefore within the cited classification itself.

The Eye-Faculty-flesh objection tests whether inseparable occurrence
defeats that separation. Its reply locates hardness in the fleshy mass
accompanying the Faculty. Co-occurrence does not establish identity of
the two determinations. Because the transmitted sentence is damaged,
this study keeps that contextual construal provisional.

The six-Principle statement is likewise given a limited explanatory
task: showing fundamental constituents in the context of descent into
the womb. The reference to the six contact Essence Bases and the
consequence concerning mental factors resist treating the short formula
as an exhaustive reduction of every category. The text then expressly
rejects the identification of mental factors with Citta, citing their
dependence on Citta and expressions such as Citta “with passion.”
Dependence articulates a relation between distinguishable terms.

This restored section matters to the argument as a whole. The dispute
is not closed after the first elemental quotation; the commentary
also examines how abbreviated scriptural descriptions should be read.

## 7. Atomic Aggregation Is a Separate Predicate

*Saṃcita* is explained by *paramāṇusaṃghātatva*, being an aggregate
of atoms. Here it is a technical Form classification, not a general
claim about accumulation of experiences, karma, or conceptual content.

| Principle or portion | Element status | Aggregated? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tangible Principle | Elements and Element-dependent Form | Yes |
| Other nine Form-bearing Principles | Element-dependent | Yes |
| Avijñapti within Essence Principle | Element-dependent Form | No |
| Seven Citta Principles and remaining Essence Principle | Neither | No |

The first row prevents a misleading hierarchy in which all aggregated
Form would be Element-dependent Form alone. The ten aggregated
Principles include the Tangible Principle and therefore its Element
portion. The avijñapti row establishes the converse limit: Element-
dependence does not entail atomic aggregation.

The source distinguishes these predicates without making them three
successive stages of production. An arrow from Elements to
Element-dependent Form to aggregation would conceal the crossing
classifications.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

- “Twofold” divides the Tangible Principle into four Elements and seven
  Element-dependent tangibles.
- The remaining nine Form-bearing Principles are Element-dependent.
- *Bhautika* also governs the Essence-Principle portion identified as
  avijñapti.
- Buddhadeva's contrary thesis is explicitly attributed; the subsequent
  objections receive contextual replies that must not be omitted.
- The ten Form-bearing Principles are aggregated in the atomic sense.
- Avijñapti is Element-dependent but unaggregated; the Tangible
  Principle is aggregated and includes both Elements and
  Element-dependent portions.

The original research kārikā and commentary remain unchanged. Their
compressed translation and hierarchical reconstruction are refined
only in this official study.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage distinguishes dependence from identity in several
registers: Element-dependent Form arises in relation to the Elements;
the Faculty and its accompanying flesh remain distinguishable; and
mental factors depend on Citta without being identical to it. A common
basis does not erase differentiated characteristics or functions.

For a Fichtean nondual hypothesis, these are differences internal to
one system of determination, not independent substances joined from
outside. Hegelian logic preserves them as determinate relations: the
Element, its dependent Form, a Faculty, and its associated Form are
not interchangeable even when they belong to one living order. The
Bhāṣya's rejection of reducing mental factors to Citta reinforces the
same constraint for cognition.

The crossing classifications are a further discipline. Element-
dependence and atomic aggregation cannot be substituted for each other.
Avijñapti joins Element-dependent Form with membership in the Essence
Principle, yet falls outside the ten atomically aggregated Form-bearing
Principles. This gives the forthcoming Loka account of subtle Elements
a concrete structure to test: dependence, sensory apprehension, and
aggregation must remain distinct.

That structure can support the broader Organon account of
Prajñā/Vijñāna and its systematic unfolding. Its contribution here is
the differentiated Principle architecture; the Fichtean-Hegelian
synthesis remains our working interpretation.
