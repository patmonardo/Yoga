# VAK_8.31 Bhāṣya — Immeasurable Levels, Training, and Limits of Counteraction

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> dhyānayor muditā anyāni ṣaṭsu kecit tu pañcasu |
> na taiḥ prahāṇaṃ nṛṣv eva janyante tryanvito dhruvam || 8.31 ||

> Sympathetic joy is in the two dhyānas; the other three are on six levels,
> though some say five. There is no abandonment by them. They are generated
> only among humans. One who has attained them is invariably endowed with three.

## 3. Continuous Translation

> On how many grounds are these [immeasurables]? “Sympathetic joy is
> in the two dhyānas”: in the first and second dhyānas, because it is
> gladness. “The other [three] are on six”: the other three
> immeasurables occur on six grounds—*anāgamya*, intermediate dhyāna,
> and the [four] dhyānas—counting preparatory and principal states.
> “Some, however, [place them] on five”: some exclude *anāgamya* and
> accept five. Others say ten, including the desire domain and access
> attainments, on a reckoning that includes collected and uncollected,
> principal and preparatory states [the local compound is damaged].
>
> It was said that they counteract ill will and the rest. Does
> [the local `kleśagrahaṇaṃ` ask whether] afflictions are thereby
> abandoned? “There is no abandonment by them.” This is because
> [the principal immeasurables] belong to principal dhyāna grounds,
> use attention through resolve, and take beings as Supports. Yet
> they are called counteragents because preparatory practice
> suppresses ill will and the rest, and because of a further
> distancing [the local `prahāṇa dūrīkaraṇācca` is compressed].
> For friendliness and the others exist on desire-domain and
> *anāgamya* grounds in forms resembling the principal immeasurables.
> Having suppressed those [afflictions] by these [preparatory forms],
> one abandons them by Paths of abandonment. Then, in a state free
> from passion, one acquires the principal immeasurables. Even when
> powerful circumstances arise, one is not overpowered by them.
>
> How does a beginner apply himself to friendliness? As he considers
> himself happy, or hears that others—Buddhas, bodhisattvas, and noble
> disciples—are happy, so he resolves that happiness for beings:
> “May beings indeed be happy.” If he cannot, because the affliction
> is active and strong, he divides the friendly class into three and
> resolves that happiness first for the most beloved, then for the
> middling and mildly liked. If he gains equal friendliness toward
> these, he turns to neutral persons. Then he divides the hostile
> class into three and resolves happiness first for the mildly hostile,
> then the middling, then the most hostile. If the resolve toward
> happiness does not change [for the hostile person] from what it was
> for the most beloved friend, he gradually extends it to village
> and region, then pervades one direction and the whole world with
> friendliness.
>
> One who perceives good qualities in everyone quickly produces equal
> friendliness. For it is possible to discern a good quality even in
> someone whose wholesome roots have been cut off, and a fault even
> in a pratyekabuddha; the witness connects this with a prior display
> of the fruits of merit and demerit [the syntax is compressed].
> One applies oneself similarly to compassion and sympathetic joy,
> resolving that beings sunk in many kinds of misfortune may be freed
> from suffering and may rejoice greatly. Equanimity, however, starts
> with neutral persons.
>
> These immeasurables “are generated only among humans”: they arise
> among humans, not elsewhere. Is one endowed with one immeasurable
> necessarily endowed with all? Rather, “one is invariably endowed
> with three.” Someone reborn in the third or fourth dhyāna is not
> endowed with sympathetic joy; an attainer of the immeasurables is,
> however, always endowed with the three [others].

## 4. Movement of the Commentary

The question about grounds yields first and second dhyāna for sympathetic joy,
then six grounds for the other three: *anāgamya*, intermediate dhyāna, and the
four dhyānas. The Bhāṣya reports a five-ground view excluding *anāgamya* and
an *apare* ten-ground view that also includes the desire domain and access
attainments. The latter's compound does not securely enumerate all ten.

The question about counteraction receives a denial of abandonment by the
immeasurables. Preparatory forms suppress afflictions; Paths of abandonment
abandon them. Once the principal immeasurables have been acquired after
dispassion, the opposing tendencies cannot readily overwhelm their possessor.

The beginner's method moves through strongly, moderately, and mildly loved
persons; neutral persons; then mildly, moderately, and strongly hostile persons.
Equal resolve precedes extension through village, region, direction, and world.
The text also allows rapid equal friendliness for one who sees good qualities
in everyone. Compassion and sympathetic joy follow analogous applications;
equanimity begins with neutral persons. The closing questions distinguish
generation among humans from possession after rebirth in higher dhyānas.

## 5. Bhāṣya Explanation

`Na taiḥ prahāṇam` qualifies the counteractive role assigned earlier to the
immeasurables. Their principal forms belong to principal dhyāna, proceed by
attention through resolve, and take beings as Supports (*sattvālambana*).
The desire-domain and *anāgamya* forms resemble those principal forms and
suppress ill will and the other opposed afflictions. Abandonment belongs to
the *prahāṇamārga*. The later protection belongs to the condition of having
acquired the principal immeasurables; it is not itself a second account of
abandonment.

The final `tryanvitaḥ` is explicated by *samanvāgata*: someone reborn in the
third or fourth dhyāna lacks sympathetic joy but an attainer remains endowed
with the other three. Possession is distinct from initial generation and from
simultaneous practice.

## 6. Organon Determination

The relevant functions are suppression in preparation, abandonment by its
Paths, and resistance to renewed disturbance after acquisition. Keep those
functions separate when representing the immeasurables. The training sequence
also makes equal friendliness across personal relations a condition for its
spatial extension in the difficulty-conditioned procedure; the faster route
through perceiving good qualities is separately stated.

## 7. Comparative Synthesis

For the Yoga Vidyā and Yoga Technē comparison, this passage offers a bounded
case of meditative range and cultivated equalization. It does not establish
that an immeasurable itself eradicates affliction or that all practitioners
must use the graded fallback. These are limits imposed by the Bhāṣya on the
comparison, not further claims attributed to Vasubandhu.
