# VAK_8.09 Bhāṣya — Eleven Distinct Dhyāna Factors

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> dravyato daśa caikaṃ ca prasrabdhiḥ sukham ādyayoḥ |
> śraddhā prasādaḥ prītis tu saumanasyaṃ dvidhāgamāt || 8.09 ||

> The eighteen factors counted by name are, by substance, ten and
> one, that is, eleven distinct dharmas. In the first two dhyānas
> the pleasure is pliancy; inward clarity is confidence; rapture,
> however, is mental joy, as scripture states it in two ways.

Which factors fail in defiled dhyāna: 8.10.

## 3. Continuous Translation

> [438|13–15] “By substance, ten and one”: by distinct dharma these
> [factors] are eleven. Five belong to the first dhyāna; inward clarity
> is added in the second; in the third, equanimity, mindfulness, clear
> comprehension, and pleasure; and in the fourth, feeling that is neither
> painful nor pleasant.
>
> [438|16–19] Hence the question whether factors of the first dhyāna are
> also in the second is four-cornered. The first corner is vitarka and
> vicāra; the second is inward clarity; the third is joy, pleasure, and
> one-pointedness of Consciousness; the fourth is the dharmas excluded
> from those just named. All the dhyāna factors should be compared with
> one another in this way. Why is pleasure in the third dhyāna called a
> different dharma?
>
> [438|19–26] Because there it is pleasure as feeling, whereas in the
> first two dhyānas “the pleasure is pliancy.” It is stated that the first
> two have the pleasure of pliancy. Why call pleasure here pliancy and
> pleasure there feeling? Because the pleasure faculty is absent from the
> two dhyāna attainments: it cannot be bodily, since one who is absorbed
> lacks a body of [sense] Cognition; nor can it be mental, since joy is
> mentioned. For joy is pleasant-mindedness, and pleasure and pleasant-
> mindedness cannot occur at the same time. Nor is their alternating
> operation in a dhyāna tenable, since five factors are stated.
>
> [439|01–05] Others (*apare*) say instead that there is no mental
> pleasure faculty in any of the three dhyānas: bodily pleasure alone is
> established as a factor. But the sūtra says, “What is the pleasure
> faculty? When one is touched by contact to be felt as pleasant, a
> pleasant experience belonging to feeling, bodily and mental, arises;
> this is called the pleasure faculty.” [They reply:] someone has
> inserted this reading, because in all other recensions the reading
> is only “bodily.” And it is expressly said, “experiencing pleasure
> with the body.”
>
> [439|06–09] If “body” means the mental body, what is gained by saying
> so? Furthermore, pliancy is even more abundant in the fourth dhyāna,
> yet pleasure is not stated there. If pliancy favorable to pleasant
> feeling is called pleasure, why is the pleasure of pliancy not stated
> in the third? If because equanimity impedes it, no: equanimity itself
> increases it. [The source adds:] because of its difference from what
> was cultivated before.
>
> [439|09–13] In the sūtra saying that a noble disciple dwells having
> personally realized, with the body, joy born of seclusion, pliancy and
> pleasure are spoken of separately; therefore pliancy itself is not
> pleasure. If one asks how there can be bodily Cognition in one who is
> absorbed: a wind produced by a particular samādhi, called pliancy and
> felt as pleasant, stimulates the body. If this is outward distraction
> and a fall from samādhi, no.
>
> [439|13–18] Bodily pleasure arising within the body from samādhi is
> favorable to samādhi. If one would have emerged at the time of bodily
> Cognition, no, for that very reason. If a desire-realm bodily faculty
> cannot cognize a Form-realm tangible, no: Cognition of pliancy arises.
> Then a tangible and bodily Cognition would occur even in an attainment
> without influx. If one objects that a factor must not be partly with
> influx and partly without it, bodily pliancy is spoken of as a factor
> of awakening. If one concedes this because it is favorable to the
> awakening factors, [the reply is:] being without influx can be
> understood in the same way.
>
> [439|19–24] If this conflicts with the sūtra that calls the dharmas
> from the eye through the tangible “with influx,” no: that statement
> intends another tangible and bodily Cognition. If one again says that
> in an attainment without influx one factor cannot be with influx and
> another without it, what fault is there when they are not simultaneous?
> If pleasure and joy never occur together, it would not have five factors.
> No: the instruction concerns their possible occurrence, as with
> vitarka and vicāra. If this is said to require proof, it is established
> by the opposition between gross and subtle states of Consciousness,
> and because no fault has been stated.
>
> [439|24–440|04] Hence the second and subsequent dhyānas are established
> by subtracting two, three, and four factors from the five factors of
> the first. Those five are called factors of the first precisely because
> the higher dhyānas are established by their subtraction; perception and
> the rest are not called factors. Or why are just five so called? If
> because they are helpful, no: vitarka and vicāra are more harmful to
> mindfulness and Prajñā. Some hold this view, but the former teachers
> did not state it in this way [the closing words of this clause are
> damaged]. Therefore this must be examined.
>
> [440|04–08] What dharma is inward clarity? It is the peaceful flow of
> the continuum when the disturbance of vitarka and vicāra is absent.
> Like a river with waves, a continuum disturbed by vitarka and vicāra
> flows without clarity. But then inward clarity is no distinct dharma;
> how can the factors be eleven by distinct dharma? Therefore:
>
> [440|09–13] “Clarity is confidence.” For confidence arises [at the
> second dhyāna's departure from a lower collected ground; the witness
> is damaged here]. That is called inward clarity. Others say that
> vitarka, vicāra, and samādhi are not distinct dharmas, nor is inward
> clarity. How, without distinct-dharma status, is mental-factor status
> established? A state of Consciousness is called a mental factor.
> But this is not the position established by the śāstra.
>
> [440|13–17] As for the statement “joy is pleasant-mindedness,” how is
> this known? Why must it be so? Other schools wish joy to be a distinct
> mental factor, while pleasant-mindedness is pleasure in all three
> dhyānas. [The local witness repeats this report at [440|14–15].]
> But pleasure in the dhyānas cannot be pleasant-mindedness. “Joy,
> however, is pleasant-mindedness, because of scripture in two forms.”
>
> [440|18–22] For, after speaking of the third dhyāna in the
> Aviparītaka Sūtra, the Blessed One said, “the faculty of pleasant-
> mindedness arisen in him ceases here without remainder”; and he said
> that the pleasure faculty ceases in the fourth dhyāna. Again it is
> said, “with the abandoning of pleasure and pain, and with the earlier
> disappearance of pleasant-mindedness and dejection ...” Thus the
> faculty of pleasant-mindedness is not present in the third dhyāna.
> Therefore joy itself is pleasant-mindedness, not pleasure.

## 5. Same Word ≠ Same Dharma

Pariśuddhi of fourth upekṣā/smṛti does not add two new substances. Newly
counted as aṅga ≠ wholly absent earlier. Do not promote the bodily-pleasure
account to the principal list.

## 7. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

- `daśa caikam` = eleven;
- `ādyayoḥ` limits pliancy-sukha;
- `dvidhāgamāt` = two āgama passages, not two occurrences of prīti.

## 8. Interpretation

This section is reconstruction.

Marks are not identities. One name (`sukha`) can carry two dharmas; two names
(`prasāda` / `śraddhā`) can carry one. Vidyā does that discrimination before
Technē uses the limbs. Appearance would either freeze eighteen labels or smash
them into one glow. Knowing stays in the count: saṃprajñāna is a distinct
item. The debate is not closed by us.
