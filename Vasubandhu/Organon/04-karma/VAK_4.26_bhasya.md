# VAK 4.26 Bhāṣya — How the Three Restraints Are Acquired

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
dhyānajo dhyānabhūmyaiva labhyate 'nāsravas tayā /
āryayā prātimokṣākhyaḥ paravijñāpanādibhiḥ // 4.26 //
```

> Meditation-born restraint is acquired through a dhyāna ground;
> uncontaminated restraint through that same ground when noble.
> The restraint called prātimokṣa is acquired through notification
> by another and other recognized means.

Word division, punctuation, and line breaks in the long Vinaya
enumeration are editorial; the running witness has this enumeration
as one continuous stretch. It reads `dhyanabhūmyaiva` in the first
citation, `saṃdhād` where the context points to `saṃghād`, and
`ūpasaṃgrahaṇārtham` with an initial long vowel. These readings are
retained, not silently corrected. Several proper names and the
closing refuge phrase are compressed or irregular in this e-text;
the translations of those details remain provisional.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

How, then, are these restraints acquired? “Meditation-born restraint
is acquired through a dhyāna ground.” When one acquires a
contaminated dhyāna-level mind, whether of the fundamental level or
its neighboring level, one also acquires meditation restraint,
because the two arise together.

“Uncontaminated restraint is acquired through that [ground] when it
is noble.” One acquires uncontaminated restraint when that same
dhyāna ground is acquired as uncontaminated. There are six
uncontaminated dhyāna grounds: the four dhyānas, the preliminary
concentration, and the intermediate level. They will be explained
later.

“The restraint called prātimokṣa is acquired through notification by
another and the other means.” Prātimokṣa restraint is acquired
through another's making it known: another communicates with the
candidate, and the candidate with the other. That other may be the
Saṃgha or an individual. The restraints of monk, nun, and trainee nun
come from the Saṃgha; the others come from an individual. The
Vinayavibhāṣikas say that ordination is tenfold. The word “and
others” in the Kārikā includes those modes.

They enumerate self-arising for Buddhas and solitary Buddhas;
entry into certainty for the group of five; the “Come, monk” formula
for Yaśas and others; acceptance of the Teacher by Mahākāśyapa;
satisfying questions in the case of Sodāyin; acceptance of the
weighty rules by Mahāprajāpatī; ordination by messenger for
Dharmadinnā; a procedure with a Vinaya-holder and five in frontier
regions; a ten-member group in central regions; and a threefold
refuge procedure for the group named at the end of the list. Thus,
for those cases prātimokṣa restraint is not necessarily dependent
on another's manifestation.

## 4. Three Grounds of Acquisition

The repeated `labhyate` gives the question its method: classify each
restraint by the **ground adequate for its acquisition**, not by its
shared name alone.

| Restraint | Acquisition ground in this unit |
|---|---|
| Meditation-born | Acquisition of a contaminated dhyāna-level citta, fundamental or neighboring; restraint co-arises with it |
| Uncontaminated | Acquisition of the dhyāna ground as noble and uncontaminated |
| Prātimokṣa | Ordinarily interpersonal notification, with recognized alternative modes |

`Sahabhūtatvāt` explains the first relation as co-arising, not a
later vow added to an already complete meditative state. `Tayā
āryayā` qualifies the same general domain of dhyāna ground in a
different way. The six uncontaminated grounds are named here only
to delimit its range; their full treatment is postponed by the
Bhāṣya.

## 5. Procedure and Its Exceptions

`Paravijñāpana` involves another person and reciprocal making-known.
The prose distinguishes the Saṃgha as source for three listed
restraints from an individual as source for the others. Then the
`ādi` of `paravijñāpanādibhiḥ` prevents that ordinary mode from
becoming an exclusive rule. The tenfold enumeration is attributed
specifically to the **Vinayavibhāṣikas**. The examples include
self-arising and special recognitions alongside communal and
individual procedures.

The concluding claim is narrow and important: prātimokṣa restraint
is **not necessarily dependent on another person's vijñapti** in
every recognized case. It does not follow that all procedures are
equivalent, or that the ordinary Saṃgha and individual distinctions
can be discarded. The passage establishes plurality of valid
acquisition grounds within this Vinaya account.

## 6. Organon Contact Point — Classification as Jñāna Work

Here the Kośa gives our Jñāna inquiry a methodological task. To
classify a practical determination, ask **how it is acquired** and
which conditions make that acquisition valid. A name such as
`saṃvara` does not answer the question by itself. We can carry this
into the Organon without turning our Pure Reason vows into monastic
procedures: their acquisition conditions must be stated and tested
on their own grounds. This is the Organon's reflective use of the
Kośa classification, not a claim that Vasubandhu teaches our
Science of Knowing.
