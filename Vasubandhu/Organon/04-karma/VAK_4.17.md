# VAK_4.17

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

```sanskrit
प्रातिमोक्षान्विता अष्टौ ध्यानजेन तदन्वितः ।
अनास्रवेणार्यसत्त्वाः अन्त्यौ चित्तानुवर्तिनौ ॥ ४.१७ ॥
```

The opening is transmitted in separated form, `प्रातिमोक्षान्विता अष्टौ`. In
continuous sandhi it may be displayed as `प्रातिमोक्षान्विताष्टौ`. The
separation makes the underlying nominative plural `अन्विताः` easier to see.

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

```iast
prātimokṣānvitā aṣṭau dhyānajena tadanvitaḥ /
anāsraveṇāryasattvāḥ antyau cittānuvartinau // 4.17 //
```

Padapāṭha-style restoration:

```iast
prātimokṣa-anvitāḥ aṣṭau dhyāna-jena tad-anvitaḥ /
anāsraveṇa ārya-sattvāḥ antyau citta-anuvartinau //
```

The Bhāṣya resolves the compressed second quarter: one who is endowed with
`dhyāna` is necessarily endowed with the restraint born from or in `dhyāna`.

## 3. Padaccheda and Lexical Analysis

```text
prātimokṣa-anvitāḥ
aṣṭau

dhyāna-jena
tad-anvitaḥ

anāsraveṇa
ārya-sattvāḥ

antyau
citta-anuvartinau
```

| Form | Morphology | Lexical value here |
|---|---|---|
| `prātimokṣānvitāḥ` | nominative masculine plural of `prātimokṣa-anvita` | endowed with prātimokṣa restraint |
| `aṣṭau` | nominative masculine plural numeral | the eight disciplinary classes |
| `dhyānajena` | instrumental singular of `dhyāna-ja` | with the restraint born from or in dhyāna |
| `tadanvitaḥ` | nominative masculine singular, `tad-anvita` | one endowed with that, namely dhyāna |
| `anāsraveṇa` | instrumental singular of `anāsrava` | with uncontaminated restraint |
| `āryasattvāḥ` | nominative masculine plural | noble beings |
| `antyau` | nominative masculine dual | the latter two |
| `cittānuvartinau` | nominative masculine dual | following consciousness |

**`anvita` and `samanvāgata`**

`anvita`, “connected with” or “endowed with,” is unfolded in the Bhāṣya by
`samanvāgata`. The relation does not identify the restraint with its bearer.
It states that the restraint-dharma belongs to or is present in that
continuum.

**`dhyānaja`**

The Bhāṣya explicitly gives two expansions:

```text
dhyānāt jāta
    born from dhyāna

dhyāne jāta
    born in dhyāna
```

The compound therefore should not be forced into only one causal nuance.

**`cittānuvartin`**

```text
citta + anuvartin
    following consciousness
    accompanying consciousness
    continuing in conformity with consciousness
```

Here `citta` is rendered “consciousness.” The predicate is technical: the
restraint occurs and ceases in dependence upon the corresponding
consciousness.

## 4. Grammar

The verse consists of three bearer–restraint constructions followed by a
dual predication.

**First construction**

```text
prātimokṣa-anvitāḥ aṣṭau
```

`Aṣṭau` is the subject and `prātimokṣānvitāḥ` its plural predicate:

> The eight are endowed with prātimokṣa restraint.

The eight are the eight disciplinary classes distinguished in VAK 4.14.

**Second construction**

```text
dhyāna-jena tad-anvitaḥ
```

The syntax is elliptical. Taken alone, `tad-anvitaḥ` could leave the reference
of `tad` unclear. The Bhāṣya supplies the governing relation:

```text
yaḥ dhyānena samanvāgataḥ
saḥ avaśyaṃ dhyānasaṃvareṇa [samanvāgataḥ]
```

> Whoever is endowed with dhyāna is necessarily endowed with dhyāna
> restraint.

Thus `tad-anvitaḥ` designates the one endowed with that—dhyāna—and
`dhyānajena` supplies the restraint with which that person is necessarily
endowed.

**Third construction**

```text
anāsraveṇa ārya-sattvāḥ [anvitāḥ]
```

The predicate of possession is carried forward elliptically:

> Noble beings are endowed with uncontaminated restraint.

**Final dual predication**

```text
antyau citta-anuvartinau
```

Both words are nominative dual. `Antyau`, “the latter two,” refers to:

1. meditation-born restraint;
2. uncontaminated restraint.

They—not prātimokṣa restraint—are predicated as following consciousness.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> The eight are endowed with prātimokṣa restraint. One endowed with that
> [dhyāna] is endowed with the restraint born from dhyāna. Noble beings are
> endowed with uncontaminated restraint. The latter two follow consciousness.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> The eight disciplinary classes are endowed with prātimokṣa restraint.
> Whoever is endowed with dhyāna is necessarily endowed with the restraint
> born from or in dhyāna. Noble beings are endowed with uncontaminated
> restraint. Of the three restraints, the latter two follow consciousness.

The words “disciplinary classes,” “necessarily,” “restraint,” and “of the
three restraints” make explicit determinations supplied by the Bhāṣya. They
are not additional words of the kārikā.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> The three forms of restraint are distributed according to their bearers
> and their grounds. Prātimokṣa restraint belongs to the eight classes that
> undertake it. Restraint born from meditative absorption belongs necessarily
> to one established in that absorption, and uncontaminated restraint belongs
> to noble beings. The latter two accompany the consciousness that grounds
> them; prātimokṣa restraint can continue when the present consciousness has
> changed or is absent.

This translation includes the Bhāṣya’s contrastive criterion. “Grounds” is
interpretive language for expressing the different conditions of possession;
it is not a translation of a single Sanskrit term in the verse.

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**The eight disciplinary classes**

The “eight” carries forward the nominal series of VAK 4.14, from the monk and
nun through the upavāsa observer. This verse predicates prātimokṣa restraint
of all eight without reopening their internal classification.

**`samādānaśīla`**

The Bhāṣya asks whether outsiders possess no undertaken discipline. Its answer
is carefully differentiated:

```text
outsiders may possess samādānaśīla
outsiders do not thereby possess prātimokṣasaṃvara
```

Vasubandhu does not deny the reality of their discipline. He denies that it
has the specific character and end of prātimokṣa restraint.

**`bhavasaṃniśrita`**

The outsiders’ undertaken discipline is said to be dependent upon `bhava`,
continued existence or becoming. Therefore, according to the Bhāṣya, it does
not conduce to complete release from evil. This is Vasubandhu’s doctrinal
criterion in this passage, not a neutral definition of every non-Buddhist
discipline.

**`sāmantaka`**

The neighboring or preliminary attainment is included under `dhyāna` by
extended designation. The Bhāṣya compares this with calling the outskirts of
a village “the village.” The extension enlarges the range of `dhyāna` for the
present rule without erasing the technical distinction between a preliminary
attainment and dhyāna proper.

**`āryapudgala`**

The Bhāṣya identifies the noble bearers as:

```text
śaikṣa
    one still in training

aśaikṣa
    one requiring no further training
```

Both are endowed with uncontaminated restraint.

**`acittaka`**

`Acittaka`, a mindless condition, supplies the strongest contrastive test.
Prātimokṣa restraint can continue even there. This does not make it absolutely
uncaused or independent of a continuum; it establishes only that it does not
require the corresponding consciousness to be presently occurring.

**`sahabhūhetu`**

The Bhāṣya recalls the earlier doctrine of coexistent cause. Meditation-born
and uncontaminated restraint follow consciousness as co-occurring
determinations. Prātimokṣa restraint has a different mode of continuation.

## 8. Logical Determination

**Three bearers**

```text
prātimokṣa restraint
    → the eight disciplinary classes

meditation-born restraint
    → whoever is endowed with dhyāna
    → sāmantaka included by extended designation

uncontaminated restraint
    → noble persons
    → trainees and non-trainees
```

The relation at issue is `samanvāgama`, possession or endowment. The bearer is
not the restraint, and the restraint is not reduced to a verbal description
of the bearer.

**Two modes of persistence**

```text
mind-following persistence
    meditation-born restraint
    uncontaminated restraint

non-mind-following continuation
    prātimokṣa restraint
```

“Non-mind-following” must not be expanded into “uncaused,” “eternal,” or
“independent of all consciousness.” It means that the restraint continues
when another consciousness occurs and even in a mindless state.

**The Bhāṣya’s contrastive proof**

```text
if restraint R requires its corresponding present consciousness C,
then R is cittānuvartin

dhyāna restraint requires corresponding meditative consciousness
uncontaminated restraint requires corresponding uncontaminated consciousness

therefore the latter two are cittānuvartin
```

But:

```text
prātimokṣa restraint continues with another consciousness
prātimokṣa restraint continues without present consciousness

therefore prātimokṣa restraint is not cittānuvartin
```

**Outward similarity does not determine the restraint**

An undertaken discipline and prātimokṣa restraint may resemble one another at
the level of observable conduct. The Bhāṣya distinguishes them through their
orientation:

```text
undertaken discipline dependent upon bhava
    ≠
restraint tending toward complete release from evil
```

The practical determination is therefore not exhausted by the visible deed.
Its end belongs to what kind of restraint it is.

## 9. Interpretive Note

**Restraint persists according to its ground**

VAK 4.16 showed restraint arising through the manifest and non-manifest
Aspects of a practical undertaking. VAK 4.17 now prevents those Aspects from
being treated as a single undifferentiated mechanism. A determination can
persist in more than one way.

Meditation-born restraint and uncontaminated restraint are immediately
correlative with determinate consciousness. When that consciousness is
present, its restraint is present; when it is absent, that restraint does not
continue as though it had an independent substrate. Their form is
`cittānuvartin`: consciousness and restraint occur together.

Prātimokṣa restraint has been acquired through an undertaking, but its
continuation is not tied to the uninterrupted presence of the acquisition
consciousness. It can traverse different mental states and even a mindless
condition. The vow would not govern a life if it disappeared whenever
attention moved elsewhere.

This gives positive precision to `avijñapti`. It need not be imagined as a
hidden transcendent substance. It names a karmically effective determination
whose mode of persistence cannot be reduced to a presently manifest act or a
presently corresponding consciousness.

**The practical Copula**

At the Organon level, the verse differentiates two operations of the practical
Copula:

```text
corresponding consciousness
    ↔ its immediately accompanying restraint

undertaking
    → acquired practical determination
    → continuation through altered states
```

The first relation is reciprocal co-presence. The second is an instituted
continuity: the initial act establishes a determination that remains valid
through subsequent variation. Neither operation is adequately described by a
static “A is B.” Each states how a determination becomes effective in a
continuum.

This is the next turn of the wheel. The Middle does not merely project
`vijñapti` and `avijñapti`; it differentiates the modes by which their unity is
sustained. One form follows the present consciousness. Another, once
instituted, holds across its alteration. The extremes are thus not detached
entities but functional Aspects of distinct practical relations.

This is an Organon reconstruction. Vasubandhu’s explicit claim is the narrower
technical distinction between the two mind-following restraints and the
non-mind-following continuation of prātimokṣa restraint.

**Contact with yama**

The controlled Yoga contact becomes sharper here. `Yama` must govern conduct
when the practitioner is not presently rehearsing the vow. In that respect,
prātimokṣa restraint displays the practical structure required by a durable
vow: an undertaking becomes a determination capable of continuing through
changing mental content.

The comparison does not identify Yoga’s yamas with the Buddhist eight classes
or with the Sarvāstivāda theory of avijñapti. It isolates a shared practical
problem: how a discipline becomes continuously efficacious rather than
remaining an occasional intention.
