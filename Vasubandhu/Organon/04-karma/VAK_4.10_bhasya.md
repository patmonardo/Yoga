# VAK_4.10 Bhāṣya — Initiating and Accompanying Action

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> samutthānaṃ dvidhā hetu-tat-kṣaṇotthāna-saṃjñitam /
> pravartakaṃ tayoḥ ādyam dvitīyam anuvartakam // 4.10 //

> There are two kinds of originating condition: one that projects and
> initiates an action, and one present during the moment of its performance.
> The first sets the action in motion; the second accompanies its execution.
> Without the latter, an action already projected would not occur, like an
> action in a corpse. The Bhāṣya leaves open how this account applies to
> restraint arising without consciousness.

The distinction responds to questions left by the account of ethical
classification in VAK 4.09. The kārikā study is `VAK_4.10.md`.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

This is to be examined. If bodily and verbal karma are wholesome or
unwholesome because of their originating condition, why are the Great
Elements not classified in the same way? The agent's intention is directed
toward the action, not toward the Great Elements. But in the case of
`avijñapti` belonging to one who is concentrated, there is no intention; nor
can a non-concentrated consciousness produce it, since that consciousness
belongs to a different realm. How, then, is that `avijñapti` wholesome? And
would the divine Eye and Ear not also have to be wholesome? “Effort is needed
here.”

It was said that consciousness abandoned through seeing does not produce
manifestation. What, then, of the Blessed One's statement that from wrong
view wrong resolve arises, followed by wrong speech, wrong bodily action,
and so on? “There is no contradiction.”

“Origination is twofold, designated as causal origination and origination in
that very moment.” There are two kinds of originating condition: causal
origination and origination in that very moment, because the latter is
present in that very moment.

“Of those two, the first is initiating; the second is accompanying.” Causal
origination initiates because it projects the action. Origination in that
very moment accompanies because it continues with the action during its
performance.

What capacity does this accompanying condition have in the action? Without
it, the action, though already projected, would not occur, as in the case of
a corpse. How, then, does restraint arise in someone without consciousness?
The reply says that this capacity is clearer when consciousness is present;
it does not say that restraint cannot arise without it.

## 4. Questions and Voices

The opening challenge asks why an action inherits ethical classification
from its originating condition while the Great Elements do not. The initial
reply points to the agent's intention, but the cases of concentration-born
`avijñapti` and the divine Eye and Ear leave further questions; the Bhāṣya
explicitly says effort is needed.

The next question contrasts the claim that consciousness abandoned through
seeing does not produce manifestation with a scriptural sequence from wrong
view to wrong resolve and action. The answer is that the claims do not
conflict. The following distinction between initiating and contemporaneous
origination explains how a remote causal condition can initiate an action
without being the condition present during its performance.

The final exchange asks what the contemporaneous condition contributes and
raises restraint arising without consciousness. The reply identifies a
capacity more evident when consciousness is present; the question of
mindless restraint is not settled here.

## 5. Two Functions of Origination

The Bhāṣya assigns distinct functions:

```text
hetusamutthāna
    → pravartaka
    → projects and initiates

tatkṣaṇasamutthāna
    → anuvartaka
    → accompanies performance
```

`Ākṣepakatva` names the first condition's projecting capacity.
`Kriyākālānuvartana` names the second condition's accompaniment during the
time of action. These are two causal roles, not two independent substances;
“accompanying” here means present during execution, not merely persisting
afterward.

## 6. The Corpse Comparison and Restraint

The corpse comparison tests whether prior projection alone suffices for
manifest performance. The Bhāṣya says it does not: an accompanying
condition is also relevant. The question about restraint arising without
consciousness then cautions against treating this accompaniment as necessary
in the same way for every kind of action. The reply does not declare
restraint without consciousness impossible.

## 7. Translation and Doctrinal Limits

“Initiating” renders `pravartaka`; “accompanying” renders `anuvartaka`.
“Projects” renders `ākṣip`, preserving the initiating condition's causal
function. “During performance” follows the explicit gloss
`kriyākālānuvartana`.

The unit does not resolve the ethical classification of concentration-born
`avijñapti` or the divine Faculties, and it does not provide a complete
account of restraint without consciousness. The phrase `karttavyo 'tra
yatnaḥ` marks the opening difficulty as requiring further effort.

## 8. Interpretation

VAK 4.10 distinguishes the condition that initiates an action from the
condition accompanying it during performance. This distinction answers the
scriptural-sequence objection without identifying a remote causal
antecedent with the consciousness present at the moment of manifestation.
The Bhāṣya leaves its opening ethical questions and the scope of
contemporaneous origination qualified rather than resolved.

In the Kośa's broader system, Discriminative Cognition (*vijñāna*) governs
Mind (*manas*) and guides Dharma Base; it remains distinct from
consciousness (*citta*) in this causal account. The same `avijñapti` is
classified in Form Base and Dharma Base, with *vijñāna* bearing the stated
*prati* relation to it.
