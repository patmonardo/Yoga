## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Does one become an upāsaka just by receiving restraint, without
going for refuge? No, except in the case of ignorance. When someone
goes for refuge to Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha, what exactly does
that person go to for refuge? The verse answers: the qualities that
make a Buddha, belonging to one beyond training; both kinds of
qualities that make the Saṅgha; and nirvāṇa.

Whoever goes for refuge to the Buddha goes to the qualities beyond
training that make one a Buddha. Because these qualities predominate,
that personal continuum is called a Buddha; or, through acquiring
them, one becomes capable of complete awakening and so becomes a
Buddha. What are they? The knowledge of exhaustion and the others,
with their accompanying qualities. The physical body does not differ
in the relevant respect before and after awakening. Does one take
refuge in all Buddhas or only one? In all Buddhas by their defining
characteristic, since the Path does not differ in characteristic.

Whoever goes for refuge to the Saṅgha goes to the qualities of those
still in training and those beyond training that make the Saṅgha.
Through acquiring them, the eight noble persons constitute the
Saṅgha; in this respect there is no division. Does one take refuge
in all Saṅghas or only one? In all, by characteristic, because the
Path does not differ in characteristic. A sūtra says that one also
goes for refuge to a Saṅgha that will exist in the future. That
statement brings out the protective force of such a refuge even
while its future bearers are not yet present [the source phrase is
compressed].

Whoever goes for refuge to the Dharma goes for refuge to nirvāṇa,
the cessation brought about by discernment (`pratisaṃkhyānirodha`).
The pacification of afflictions and suffering in one's own and
others' continua has one defining characteristic.

If the qualities beyond training *were themselves* the Buddha, why
would making the Tathāgata bleed with a malicious mind be an offense
of immediate consequence? The Vaibhāṣikas say that harming the
support also harms the qualities supported by it. But the treatise
does not say that the qualities beyond training alone *are* the
Buddha. It says that they *make* one a Buddha. It therefore does
not deny that their support is a Buddha, and the objection loses its
force. Otherwise a Buddha in an ordinary state of mind would cease
to be a Buddha, a member of the Saṅgha would cease to belong to
the Saṅgha, and the morality that makes a monk would itself have
to be the monk. Just as someone honoring monks thereby honors the
morality that makes them monks, one going for refuge to the Buddha
goes to the Buddha-making qualities beyond training. Others hold
that refuge in the Buddha concerns his eighteen distinctive qualities.

What is the intrinsic nature of acts of going for refuge? Verbal
manifestation (`vāgvijñapti`). And what does “refuge” mean?
Protection: through reliance on these refuges there is ultimate
release from all suffering. For the Blessed One said: Many people,
called “free from fear” in this witness's difficult reading
`bhayavarjitāḥ`, turn for refuge to mountains, forests, parks,
trees, and shrines.
That is neither the highest nor the supreme refuge; arriving there
does not free one from all suffering. But when one takes refuge in
Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha and sees with wisdom the Four Noble
Truths—suffering, its arising, its passing beyond, and the secure
noble eightfold Path leading to nirvāṇa—that is the highest refuge.
Arriving there, one is freed from all suffering. These very acts
of taking refuge are gateways to undertaking all restraints.

## 4. The Objects of Refuge

The kārikā distributes `buddhasaṃghakarān dharmān` across the first
two refuges. The Buddha-refuge concerns `aśaikṣa` qualities,
including `kṣayajñāna` and its accompaniment; the Saṅgha-refuge
concerns qualities of both `śaikṣa` and `aśaikṣa` noble persons.
The Dharma-refuge is `nirvāṇa`, specified as
`pratisaṃkhyānirodha`. The verse names its objects, while the
Bhāṣya explains why those objects protect.

`-Kara` is decisive. The text says “Buddha-making,” not “the
Buddha is nothing but these qualities.” Vasubandhu's objection to
the Vaibhāṣika reply preserves a distinction between the qualities
that constitute Buddhahood and the living support that bears them.
It also explains why refuge can be directed by the Path's defining
characteristic toward all Buddhas and all noble Saṅghas without
making the individual bearers irrelevant.

## 5. Refuge as an Act and a Gateway

The Bhāṣya calls the **act of going for refuge** `vāgvijñapti`:
an outward verbal manifestation. That is its specified intrinsic
nature here. The meaning of refuge is `trāṇa`, protection through
release from suffering. The cited verses join that protection to
seeing the Four Noble Truths with `prajñā`; merely naming an object
of safety does not complete their argument.

This gives the move into vows its concrete order: refuge is the
gateway (`dvāra`) to undertaking restraints (`saṃvarasamādāna`).
The Kośa does not reduce this gateway to private conviction, nor
does it identify the verbal act with liberation by itself.

## 6. Organon Contact Point

In our System, your wheel can be stated as **adhyāsa ceases; the
Idea of the True and Good emerges**. Here that wheel meets a
practical determination: one must be able to say what is being
trusted, how that relation is enacted, and what transformation it
opens. The Kośa answers those questions through the Three Refuges,
verbal `vijñapti`, insight, and the undertaking of restraint.

Our Prime Commandment to know the Self and our Pure Reason vow are
Organon commitments. They can interpret refuge as a vow to let
false superimposition cease and to let truth and good govern action.
That interpretation draws a systematic relation to this passage;
the Kośa itself speaks of the Buddha, Dharma, Saṅgha, and nirvāṇa,
not of the Upaniṣadic Self or an explicitly named adhyāsa.
