# VAK 4.29 Bhāṣya — The Eight Limbs as Functions and as a Whole

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
śīlāṅgāny apramādāṅgaṃ vratāṅgāni yathākramam /
catvāry ekaṃ tathā trīṇi smṛtināśo madaś ca taiḥ // 4.29 //
```

> In order, four are limbs of discipline, one a limb of heedfulness,
> and three limbs of observance. Without the supporting limbs,
> recollection can be lost and intoxicated pride can arise through
> the acts they restrain.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Why are eight limbs undertaken? Because four are limbs of
discipline, one is a limb of heedfulness, and three are limbs of
observance, respectively. The first four, through abstention from
false speech, are discipline-limbs because they abstain from acts
blameworthy by nature. Abstaining from intoxicating drink is the
heedfulness-limb: even someone who has undertaken discipline might
become heedless through drink. The three observance-limbs, through
abstention from untimely eating, accord with the sober urgency of
practice (`saṃvega`).

What would happen if the heedfulness and observance-limbs were not
undertaken? Loss of recollection and intoxicated pride would follow.
For someone drinking liquor, recollection of what should and should
not be done would be destroyed. Indulging in high or luxurious beds,
dancing, singing, and the like can produce pride or intoxicated
exhilaration; for one so intoxicated, breach of discipline is near.
On the other hand, departing from the customary meal-time brings
recollection of the upavāsa and `saṃvega` to presence. Without that
departure, both could fail.

Some, however, hold that abstention from untimely eating is itself
the upavāsa and that the remaining practices form its eight limbs;
they count entertainment and adornment separately to make the
number. On this reading the cited sūtra would not fit, since it
calls abstention from untimely eating the eighth limb through which
one follows the training of the noble Arhats.

What, then, is the upavāsa whose limbs these are? Limbs are parts of
an aggregate. Just as one speaks of a four-part army or a
five-part musical ensemble, upavāsa is to be understood as an
eightfold whole. Abstention from untimely eating may be called both
upavāsa and a limb of upavāsa, as right view may be called both
Path and a Path limb. The Vaibhāṣikas add comparable examples of
investigation of dharmas in awakening and samādhi in dhyāna.

But can these very factors be limbs of themselves? If earlier right
views and the like were instead the limbs of later ones, the noble
Path arising in its first moment would not be eightfold. The
question of how a whole and its limbs are related must therefore
respect the Path's completeness at first arising.

## 4. Why the Eight Are Not a Flat List

The 4–1–3 division classifies the limbs by **function**:

| Limbs | Function given in the Bhāṣya |
|---|---|
| Four `śīlāṅga` | Direct abstention from naturally blameworthy acts |
| One `apramādāṅga` | Prevent intoxication from undermining undertaken discipline |
| Three `vratāṅga` | Support `saṃvega` and the special form of the observance |

The supporting limbs have distinct causal roles. Drink threatens
`kāryākāryasmṛti`, recollection of what should and should not be
done. Luxurious and entertaining indulgence can generate `mada`,
bringing breach of discipline near. Leaving customary meal-time
can positively bring `upavāsasmṛti` and `saṃvega` to presence. This
is why the classification explains the work of the limbs rather
than merely dividing eight items into numbers.

## 5. The Whole and Its Limbs

The alternative attributed to `kecit` makes abstention from
untimely eating the upavāsa **outside** the eight limbs. The cited
sūtra counts it as the eighth; that blocks the proposed counting.
The reply takes `upavāsa` as the `samudāya`, the constituted whole,
whose `avayava`, constituent parts, are its limbs. It does not
require a ninth restraint-thing standing beyond them.

The final comparison with Path and dhyāna adds a pressure that
should not be smoothed away. One factor can be named from the whole
and from its role as a limb. Yet an individual factor cannot
simply be its **own** limb. Moving the limb to an earlier moment
fails too: the first moment of the noble Path must already be
eightfold. The passage tests both ways of explaining the
whole–part relation and leaves their exact account a live logical
problem.

## 6. Organon Contact Point — Jñāna Classification

This is a good exercise for our Jñāna power of classification.
First determine each limb by its **work**; then determine the
eightfold observance as a whole; finally test whether the proposed
whole–part rule still holds at its **first moment**. Our
dialectical dhyāna cannot solve the last question by hiding a
prior preparatory series inside the definition of the Path. The
Kośa forces a sharper account of how a complete practical form can
arise through differentiated functions. This is our Organon use of
the argument, not a new translation of `aṅga`.
