# VAK_4.13 — Three Kinds of Avijñapti and Restraint

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

```sanskrit
अविज्ञप्तिस्त्रिधा ज्ञेया संवरासंवरेतरा ।
संवरः प्रातिमोक्षाख्यो ध्यानजोऽनास्रवस्तथा ॥ ४.१३ ॥
```

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

```text
avijñaptis tridhā jñeyā saṃvarāsaṃvaretarā /
saṃvaraḥ prātimokṣākhyo dhyānajo 'nāsravas tathā // 4.13 //
```

VAK 4.01–4.12 traced karma through volition, manifestation, causal
continuity, and ethical determination. VAK 4.13 turns to the practical
discipline of action. It first distinguishes three modes of
`avijñapti`; it then differentiates restraint into three forms.

## 3. Lexical Analysis

| Pada | Morphology | Force in this passage |
|---|---|---|
| avijñaptiḥ | nominative feminine singular | non-disclosing continuity |
| tridhā | indeclinable adverb | in three ways |
| jñeyā | nominative feminine singular gerundive | to be understood or classified |
| saṃvara | compound member | restraint |
| asaṃvara | compound member | non-restraint |
| itarā | nominative feminine singular | the class other than either of the first two |
| prātimokṣa-ākhyaḥ | nominative masculine singular compound adjective | called prātimokṣa |
| dhyāna-jaḥ | nominative masculine singular compound adjective | born from meditation |
| anāsravaḥ | nominative masculine singular adjective | uncontaminated |
| tathā | adverb | likewise; also |

The Bhāṣya resolves `saṃvarāsaṃvaretarā` as three classes: restraint,
non-restraint, and that which is neither. It identifies the third class
explicitly as neither `saṃvara` nor `asaṃvara`.

## 4. Grammar

The first pāda predicates `tridhā jñeyā` of `avijñaptiḥ`; the compound `saṃvarāsaṃvaretarā` completes that predicate. The second predicates three coordinated descriptions—`prātimokṣākhyaḥ`, `dhyānajaḥ`, and `anāsravaḥ`—of `saṃvaraḥ`.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation
> Non-disclosing continuity is to be understood as threefold: restraint,
> non-restraint, and that which is neither. Restraint is called
> *prātimokṣa*, is meditation-born, and is likewise uncontaminated.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> Non-disclosing continuity is threefold: restraint, non-restraint, and neither.
> Restraint is prātimokṣa, meditation-born, and likewise uncontaminated.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> Non-disclosing continuity is to be understood as threefold: restraint, non-restraint, and that which is neither. Restraint is called prātimokṣa, is meditation-born, and is likewise uncontaminated.

## 9. Interpretive Note

**Translation note.** The Bhāṣya identifies the three forms as *prātimokṣa*, meditation-born, and uncontaminated restraint, associating them respectively with desire-realm discipline, Form-realm discipline, and uncontaminated discipline. It also glosses restraint as checking the spread of misconduct; these explanations are not stated in the kārikā.

### Textual Notes

The Bhāṣya unit runs from [205|12] through [205|18] in
`Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt`. The next verse, beginning at [205|19],
opens the eightfold division of *prātimokṣa* restraint; it is not included
here.

The witness reads `tābhyāṃ cetaro`; the division `tābhyāṃ ca itaraḥ` is
editorial. The following `naiva saṃvaro nāsaṃvaraḥ` secures the sense
“neither.” The exact force of `ihatyānām` in the commentary's
realm-identification remains provisional.

### Interpretation

The verse gives non-disclosing continuity an expressly practical
classification: restraint, non-restraint, or neither. It then differentiates
restraint by its mode. This marks a shift from the analysis of how action is
initiated and carried through to how conduct is governed over time.

In the Organon reading, *prātimokṣa* can be approached as a **systems vow**:
a practical commitment that gives Cetanā a stable discipline. The Bhāṣya
supports the functional core of this reading by defining restraint as
checking the spread of misconduct. The fuller claim that Avidyā:Asmitā
dies only through this practical realization belongs to Yogavidyā's
systematic interpretation, not to the lexical content of this verse.

This is the Karma plane's objective turn toward disciplined action. It
begins to show how the Faculty's governing force becomes a determinate
practical orientation, while the particular vow and its further
classifications remain for the coming verses.
