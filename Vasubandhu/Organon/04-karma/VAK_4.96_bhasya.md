# VAK 4.96 Bhāṣya — Three Obstructions to the Noble Path

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
ānantaryāṇi karmāṇi tīvrakleśo 'tha durgatiḥ /
kauravāsaṃjñisattvāś ca matam āvaraṇatrayam // 4.96 //
```

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> The five actions of *ānantarya* are action-obstruction. Recurrent
> affliction, not merely affliction of occasional extreme force, is
> affliction-obstruction. The three bad destinies, people of Uttarakuru, and
> non-percipient beings are maturation-obstruction. These conditions obstruct
> the noble Path and the wholesome roots preparatory to it.

## 3. Continuous Scientific English Rendering

The Blessed One has stated three obstructions: action-obstruction,
affliction-obstruction, and maturation-obstruction. What is their nature?

The five actions of *ānantarya* are action-obstruction: killing one's mother,
killing one's father, killing an arhat, causing a division in the Saṅgha,
and, with a hostile mind, causing blood to flow from a Tathāgata's body.

Recurrent affliction is affliction-obstruction. Affliction is of two kinds:
*tīvra*, which is frequent or recurrent, and *tīkṣṇa*, which is very strong.
Of these, the recurrent kind is the obstruction. Even an affliction of
extreme force, if occasional, can be removed; but even a collection that is
continual is not readily removed. When affliction is continual, there is no
time for exertion toward removing it. Depending on a mild affliction, a
moderate one arises; depending on a moderate one, an intense one arises.

The three bad destinies are maturation-obstruction; so also, within a
favorable destiny, are the people of Uttarakuru and non-percipient beings.

What do these obstructions obstruct? The noble Path and the wholesome roots
preparatory to the noble Path. Other actions fixed to limiting conditions
could also have been named. The text names these because they are especially
clear and discriminable under five aspects: support, result, destiny, mode
of birth, and person.

Among these, affliction-obstruction is described as *sarvajaghanya*; next
comes action-obstruction. The Vaibhāṣikas say that through these two a person
can be beyond remedy even in a second birth, because of successive
transmission.

What does *ānantarya* mean? These actions cannot be interrupted with respect
to their maturation by the result, in another birth, of another action;
they cannot be displaced. Alternatively, after the person who performed
such an action departs from this life, there is no interval in proceeding
toward birth in hell. This is called immediacy: through conjunction with the
relevant dharma, one becomes “immediate,” as in the case of monastic
ordination.

## 4. The Three Obstructions

| Obstruction | Specified basis | Functional target |
|---|---|---|
| action-obstruction | five actions of *ānantarya* | noble Path and its preparatory wholesome roots |
| affliction-obstruction | recurrent affliction | noble Path and its preparatory wholesome roots |
| maturation-obstruction | three bad destinies, Uttarakuru, non-percipient existence | noble Path and its preparatory wholesome roots |

The three classes locate the obstruction differently: in a completed
action, in an afflictive continuity, or in the conditions of a resultant
existence. They share a functional target but not a single causal mechanism.

## 5. The Five Actions and the Meaning of `Ānantarya`

The Bhāṣya names five actions:

```text
mātṛvadha
pitṛvadha
arhadvadha
saṃghabheda
tathāgataśarīre duṣṭacittarudhirotpādana
```

These are respectively killing one's mother, killing one's father, killing
an arhat, causing a division in the Saṅgha, and causing blood to flow from a
Tathāgata's body with a hostile mind.

The Bhāṣya gives two explanations of *ānantarya*:

1. Their maturation cannot be displaced by the result of another birth or
   another action.
2. After the agent's death, there is no interval in the movement toward
   birth in hell.

Both accounts concern the ordering of maturation, not mere speed measured
within ordinary clock-time.

## 6. Recurrent and Intense Affliction

The commentary distinguishes two predicates:

```text
tīvra
    = abhīkṣṇika
    = frequent, recurrent, continual

tīkṣṇa
    = adhimātra
    = very strong, intense
```

The obstruction is recurrent affliction, not simply maximum intensity. An
occasional intense episode may still leave a time for effort; continual
affliction consumes the interval needed to strive against it. The sequence
from mild to moderate to intense describes further affliction arising in
dependence on the preceding degree.

## 7. What Is Obstructed and Why These Cases Are Named

The Bhāṣya answers directly: the noble Path and the wholesome roots
preparatory to it. It acknowledges other actions fixed to restrictive
conditions, then explains that the listed cases are especially
recognizable through five aspects:

```text
adhiṣṭhāna  support
phala        result
gati         destiny
upapatti     mode of birth
pudgala      person
```

The running witness's list of further conditions is compressed and
orthographically uncertain. The five-aspect explanation is retained without
claiming that the listed obstructions exhaust every possible limitation.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Ranking

The text calls affliction-obstruction *sarvajaghanya* and then places
action-obstruction next. Because the precise force of *jaghanya* here can
signal a lowest rank without making the scale explicit, this study preserves
the Sanskrit term rather than asserting an unambiguous severity hierarchy.
The Vaibhāṣika account that follows says these two can continue to make a
person beyond remedy even in a second birth through successive transmission.

## 9. Interpretation

VAK 4.95 distinguished projecting a birth-class from completing its
particular features. VAK 4.96 adds a practical criterion: whether action,
affliction, or resultant existence obstructs the noble Path and the
wholesome roots that prepare for it. The three obstructions are not
interchangeable obstacles; their action, temporal, and maturation
mechanisms differ.

The distinction between recurrent and intense affliction is decisive.
Practical counteraction requires an interval in which another response can
be initiated. A powerful but occasional affliction may leave that interval;
recurrence without a break removes it. The passage thus describes
obstruction through the availability of practical transformation, while
keeping its doctrinal claim specific to Path and Path-preparatory roots.
