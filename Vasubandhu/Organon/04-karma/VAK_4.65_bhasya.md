# VAK 4.65 Bhāṣya — Mental Bad Conduct Beyond Karma

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
aśubhaṃ kāyakarmādi mataṃ duścaritatrayam /
akarmāpi tv abhidhyādi manoduścaritaṃ tridhā // 4.65 //
```

> Unwholesome bodily action and the rest are held to be the three
> kinds of bad conduct. Yet mental bad conduct beginning with
> covetousness is threefold, even though it is not itself karma.

The second half widens `manoduścarita` beyond mental karma as
strictly identified with `cetanā`.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Three kinds of bad conduct have been stated. In this connection:
“Unwholesome bodily action and the rest are held to be the three
kinds of bad conduct.” Unwholesome bodily, verbal, and mental
karma are, respectively, bad conduct of body, speech, and mind.

“Yet mental bad conduct beginning with covetousness is threefold,
even though it is not itself karma.” There are also three kinds
of mental bad conduct whose own nature is not karma and which are
distinct from volition: covetousness, hostility, and wrong view.
The Dārṣṭāntikas say that covetousness and the others themselves
are mental karma, citing the wording of the `Saṃcetanīya Sūtra`.

But then karma and affliction would be identical. What would be
wrong if some affliction were also karma? That cannot be so, for
it would conflict with a sūtra. According to the Vaibhāṣikas,
the sūtra indicates volition through these afflictions because
volition proceeds by way of them. Bodily, verbal, and mental
conduct is called bad conduct because, having an undesirable
result, it is blameworthy.

## 4. Three Categories, Two School Voices

The opening classification makes unwholesome `kāya`, `vāk`, and
`manas` karma instances of bad conduct. The second half adds
three mental bad conducts—`abhidhyā`, `vyāpāda`, and
`mithyādṛṣṭi`—that the exposition calls distinct from
`cetanā` and not karma in their own nature. Thus `duścarita`
is wider than karma in this account.

The Dārṣṭāntikas explicitly contest that distinction, invoking
the `Saṃcetanīya Sūtra` to identify the three afflictive states
as mental karma. The ensuing short questions and replies are
unmarked in the running text; their allocation to individual
speakers is inferred from the dispute, not directly labeled at
each sentence. The final explanation is explicitly attributed
to the Vaibhāṣikas: the sūtra can name those states as a way of
indicating the **volition proceeding through them**, without
making affliction and karma identical.

The issue is therefore about the category of mental action, not
about whether covetousness, hostility, or wrong view matters
ethically. The closing definition names undesirable result as
the ground for calling conduct blameworthy.

## 5. Organon Contact Point

Our practical vocabulary can keep an afflictive orientation,
the volition that proceeds through it, and the wider predicate
“bad conduct” distinct. The Bhāṣya shows how a sūtra's naming
may track a mediated relation without making the named
orientation identical to the strict action-kind. This is an
Organon comparison; the Dārṣṭāntika and Vaibhāṣika positions
remain attributed positions within the source.
