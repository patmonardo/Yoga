# VAK_4.68

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)


## 3. Padaccheda and Lexical Analysis

```text
sāmantakāḥ | tu | vijñaptiḥ |
avijñaptiḥ | bhavet | na | vā |
viparyayeṇa | pṛṣṭhāni |
prayogaḥ | tu | tri-mūla-jaḥ
```

| Form | Morphology | Contextual sense |
|---|---|---|
| `sāmantakāḥ` | nominative masculine plural | proximate actions; actions functionally approaching the principal course |
| `tu` | adversative or transitional particle | but; now |
| `vijñaptiḥ` | nominative feminine singular, collective predicate | manifest action |
| `avijñaptiḥ` | nominative feminine singular | non-manifest karmic determination |
| `bhavet` | third-person singular optative of `√bhū` | may occur; may be present |
| `na vā` | negative plus alternative particle | or may not |
| `viparyayeṇa` | instrumental singular used adverbially | conversely; by reversal |
| `pṛṣṭhāni` | nominative neuter plural | subsequent actions; aftermaths |
| `prayogaḥ` | nominative masculine singular | preparatory undertaking |
| `tri-mūla-jaḥ` | nominative masculine singular compound | born from the three roots |

**`Sāmantaka` and `prayoga`**

The Kārikā presents `sāmantaka`, the action proximate to a karmapatha, and
`prayoga`, preparatory undertaking, in the same field. The Bhāṣya treats the
proximate phase as preparation functionally ordered toward principal
completion.

**`Pṛṣṭha`**

Literally associated with what is behind or follows. Here it names the
posterior continuation after the principal karmic course has been completed.

## 4. Grammar

**The proximate or preparatory phase**

```text
sāmantakāḥ tu
    vijñaptiḥ [bhavanti]

avijñaptiḥ
    bhavet vā
    na vā
```

> The proximate actions, however, are manifest action; non-manifest
> determination may or may not occur.

The singular predicates classify the plural actions by mode. `Bhavet na vā`
expresses a real conditional alternative, not uncertainty about the doctrine.

**The posterior phase**

```text
pṛṣṭhāni
    viparyayeṇa [veditavyāni]
```

> The subsequent actions are to be understood conversely.

The Bhāṣya supplies the exact reversal:

```text
preparation
    vijñapti necessary
    avijñapti contingent

aftermath
    avijñapti necessary
    vijñapti contingent
```

**The genesis of preparation**

```text
prayogaḥ tu
    tri-mūla-jaḥ [bhavati]
```

> Preparation, however, is born from the three roots.

`Trimūlajaḥ` means that preparation may arise from any of the three
unwholesome roots, not that all three must occur together.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> The proximate actions, however, are manifest action; non-manifest
> determination may or may not occur. The subsequent actions are conversely.
> Preparation, however, is born from the three roots.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> The preparatory actions approaching a bodily or verbal karmic course are
> necessarily manifest; they generate non-manifest determination only under
> conditions of sufficiently intense afflictive force. After principal
> completion, the distribution is reversed: non-manifest determination
> necessarily continues, while further manifest action occurs only if the
> agent continues to behave in conformity with the completed course.
> Preparation for an unwholesome course may arise from greed, hatred, or
> delusion.

The conditions for `avijñapti`, the exact converse distribution, and the
restriction of the three-root claim to preparatory genesis are supplied by the
Bhāṣya.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> A bodily or verbal course of action is a differentiated practical process.
> Before fulfillment, it exists through action directed toward completion;
> at fulfillment, it becomes the principal karmic course; after fulfillment,
> its determination persists and may again become manifest in conduct that
> conforms to it. Genesis, appearance, completion, and persistence are thus
> related moments, but none is identical with the others.

The three mental courses mark the limit of this temporal schema: according to
the Bhāṣya, their immediate presence itself constitutes the karmapatha, so
they have no separately articulated preparation or aftermath.

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**`Prayoga` — preparatory undertaking**

Action already directed toward the fulfillment of a karmapatha but not yet
constituting its principal completion. It is a functional determination, not
merely anything that happens earlier in time.

**`Sāmantaka` — proximate action**

The bodily or verbal action adjoining and approaching the principal course.
In the Bhāṣya's analysis it substantially designates the preparatory phase.

**`Maula karmapatha` — principal karmic course**

Bhāṣya vocabulary for the phase in which the defining result of a course is
fulfilled. `Maula` does not occur in the Kārikā but is indispensable to the
commentary's three-phase analysis.

**`Pṛṣṭha` — aftermath**

The posterior continuation after principal completion. Moments of
`avijñapti` necessarily belong to it; further manifest actions belong to it
when the agent continues conduct conforming to the completed course.

**`Anudharma` — conforming continuation**

Bhāṣya terminology for behavior that follows in the character of the
completed course. It explains the optional presence of `vijñapti` in the
aftermath.

**`Paryavasthāna` — afflictive domination**

An intense enveloping afflictive condition. The Bhāṣya invokes its force,
together with strongly concentrated heedlessness, to explain when preparation
also produces `avijñapti`.

**`Saṃmukhībhāva` — immediate presence**

The coming into direct presence of covetousness, hostility, or false view.
For these three mental courses, the Bhāṣya says immediate presence itself is
the karmapatha.

**`Trimūlaja` — born from the three roots**

Preparation may be generated by:

```text
lobha     greed
dveṣa    hatred
moha      delusion
```

The claim concerns genetic arising. It does not say that the principal
completion of every unwholesome course is equally determined by each root.

## 8. Logical Determination

**The three-phase form**

```text
prayoga
    action ordered toward fulfillment
        ↓
maula
    fulfillment of the defining result
        ↓
pṛṣṭha
    persistence and possible conforming manifestation
```

This is more exact than a simple before/during/after chronology. The phases
are determined by their relation to fulfillment.

**Converse modal distributions**

```text
PREPARATION
    vijñapti       necessary
    avijñapti      conditional

AFTERMATH
    avijñapti      necessary
    vijñapti       conditional
```

Thus visible activity predominates in approach to completion, while
non-manifest continuity is structurally secured after completion.

**Three irreducible dimensions**

```text
phase
    prayoga / maula / pṛṣṭha

mode
    vijñapti / avijñapti

root
    lobha / dveṣa / moha
```

No dimension substitutes for another. An action's position in the process,
mode of existence, and afflictive genesis must each be determined.

**One course can function within another**

The Bhāṣya says that even another karmapatha can serve as preparation or
aftermath for a given course; in a constructed example, all ten can prepare a
killing. Therefore:

```text
identity of course A
    ≠
functional role of A within the process of course B
```

The ten courses form a relational field rather than ten isolated containers.

**The mental-course exception**

```text
bodily and verbal course
    prayoga → maula → pṛṣṭha

covetousness / hostility / false view
    immediate presence = karmapatha
    no distinct prayoga or pṛṣṭha
```

The process form is therefore differentiated according to the kind of course;
it is not imposed uniformly upon all ten.

## 9. Interpretive Note

**The course as a process of determination**

The ten courses of action now cease to look like a static moral list. A course
has internal articulation:

```text
rooted genesis
    → preparation
    → manifestation
    → result-fulfillment
    → principal completion
    → persistent aftermath
```

Its Meaning emerges through these relations. The terminal event alone does
not disclose what generated it, which activities prepared it, when it became
complete, or what determination continued afterward.

**Delusion can generate organized action**

The Bhāṣya's examples make `moha` philosophically important. Delusion need not
appear as mere confusion or absence of information. A false normative view can
organize deliberate preparation so that harmful action appears righteous,
authoritative, or meritorious.

**Appearance and completion**

The Bhāṣya raises a technical pressure concerning killing: the bodily blow and
the victim's death need not be simultaneous, yet the principal course is
fixed through fulfillment of the result. Its reported Vaibhāṣika resolution
places strain on the exact allocation of `vijñapti` and `avijñapti`. The safe
determination here is limited:

```text
visible execution
    may precede
result-completion
```

The passage should not be forced into a more final harmonization than the
commentarial discussion warrants.

**Organon contact**

```text
Genesis ≠ Appearance ≠ Completion ≠ Persistence
```

These distinctions do not fragment the action. They articulate the mediation
through which it becomes one determinate karmic course. The three-phase model,
modal distribution, mental-course exception, examples, and completion problem
belong to the Bhāṣya; the formulation as a mediated Practical Form is the
project synthesis.
