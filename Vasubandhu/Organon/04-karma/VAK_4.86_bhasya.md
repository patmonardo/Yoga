# VAK 4.86 Bhāṣya — Wrong Livelihood and the Difficulty of Purifying Greed

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
lobhajaṃ kāyavākkarma mithyājīvaḥ pṛthak kṛtaḥ /
duḥśodhatvāt pariṣkāralobhotthaṃ cen na sūtrataḥ // 4.86 //
```

> Bodily or verbal action born from greed is wrong livelihood. It is treated
> separately because it is difficult to purify. If it is said to arise only
> from greed for the requisites, no: that is not so according to the sūtra.

The verse explains why the Noble Eightfold Path names wrong livelihood in
addition to wrong speech and wrong action. The difference is one of causal
root and practical function, not a fourth medium of action.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

The Blessed One speaks of wrong speech, wrong action, and wrong livelihood.
What wrong livelihood is there apart from the first two? There is no other
kind of action. Rather, bodily or verbal action born from greed is wrong
livelihood, whereas bodily and verbal actions born from hatred or delusion
are wrong action. Wrong livelihood is treated separately from those two
because it is difficult to purify.

Greed is a dharma that carries one away, and the mind is not easily guarded
against action arising from it. Livelihood is therefore difficult to purify.
It is separately indicated in order to produce careful attention toward it.

A verse is cited here: “The view of householders, always divided among many
views, is difficult to purify; so too is livelihood for monks, whose way of
living depends upon others.”

Suppose someone holds that only bodily or verbal action arising from greed
for the necessities of life is wrong livelihood, and nothing else. On this
account, dancing, singing, and similar activity undertaken for one's own
enjoyment would have no connection with livelihood. This restriction does
not accord with the sūtra. In the section on ethical conduct, the Blessed One
also places watching elephant fights and similar spectacles under wrong
livelihood. Why? Because they involve enjoyment of an improper object. That
concludes the matter.

## 4. No Additional Medium of Action

The opening question is classificatory. If action is bodily, verbal, or
mental, does wrong livelihood name some fourth kind? Vasubandhu answers no.
The same bodily or verbal action receives a different Path determination
according to its causal root:

```text
bodily or verbal action born from greed
    → wrong livelihood

bodily or verbal action born from hatred or delusion
    → wrong action
```

Wrong livelihood is therefore `pṛthak kṛta`, treated separately, without
being `dravya-antara`, another kind of entity. One action medium supports
different practical classifications.

## 5. Why Greed Requires Separate Attention

The rationale is `duḥśodhatva`, difficulty of purification. Greed “carries
away” the agent, and actions arising from it are difficult to guard against.
Livelihood intensifies this problem because sustenance is continuous,
socially embedded, and easily rationalized as necessity.

Separate classification has a pedagogical purpose:

```text
difficult to detect and purify
    → named independently
    → attention and care are intensified
```

This is a methodological rule of the Path. A determination may deserve its
own practical category because it requires a distinct mode of vigilance,
even when its material substrate belongs to an already established class.

## 6. Householder View and Monastic Livelihood

The cited verse pairs two characteristic purification problems:

| Practical station | Especially difficult object |
|---|---|
| householder | view, amid a plurality of competing views |
| monk | livelihood, because support depends upon others |

This is not a claim that householders lack livelihood problems or monks lack
problems of view. It identifies the structural vulnerability intensified by
each station. Dependence upon donors places the monk's bodily and verbal
conduct in a field where greed can subtly govern presentation, request, and
exchange.

## 7. Why Greed for Requisites Is Too Narrow

The proposed definition restricts wrong livelihood to acquisition of
`jīvitapariṣkāra`, the material requisites supporting life. Its advantage is
clear: livelihood would then mean only conduct performed to obtain
sustenance. The Bhāṣya rejects this extension as too narrow because the sūtra
includes watching elephant fights and related spectacles.

The supplied reason is `mithyāviṣayaparibhoga`, enjoyment or consumption of
an improper object. Wrong livelihood therefore reaches beyond how resources
are acquired. It can include how a life is maintained and occupied through
bodily and verbal participation in an improper field of enjoyment.

The source does not thereby classify every pleasure, song, or spectacle as
wrong livelihood. Its argument is narrower: the proposed requisites-only
definition fails to cover examples that the cited ethical discourse itself
places in the class.

## 8. Classification by Root, Medium, and Function

The analysis requires at least three coordinates:

```text
Medium   = body or speech
Root     = greed, hatred, or delusion
Function = livelihood or another practical end
```

Surface form alone is insufficient. Identical speech can be classified
differently when one instance is organized by greed as a means of life and
another is organized by hatred. Conversely, the separate Path name does not
license invention of a new action substance.

## 9. Organon Contact Point

For the Rational Moral Agent, 4.86 supplies a rule for practical auditing:

```text
ActionClass =
    classify(Medium, Root, PracticalFunction, ConsumptionField)
```

An operation's API shape does not determine its moral class. The same bodily
or verbal operation may serve different governing motives and world
relations. The audit must therefore preserve provenance from root through
function to the object consumed or produced.

The separate warning for livelihood also models risk based observability.
A recurrent, dependency laden operation deserves explicit monitoring when
its errors are unusually hard to detect and purify, even if it reuses ordinary
execution channels.

These are Organon applications. The Path classification, the monastic
dependency claim, and the sūtra examples remain the source doctrine.
