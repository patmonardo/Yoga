# VAK 4.73 Bhāṣya — Killing and Appropriation

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
prāṇātipātaḥ saṃcintya parasyābhrāntimāraṇam /
adattādānamanyasvasvīkriyā balacauryataḥ // 4.73 //
```

> Killing is the deliberate, non-mistaken killing of another. Taking what
> is not given is the appropriation of another's possession by force or
> stealth.

The Bhāṣya begins the `lakṣaṇa`, the constitutive definition, of each
course. The visible event is insufficient: intention, recognition of the
objective basis, mode of action, and fulfilled result must form one
determinate practical relation.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Under what conditions does one who personally kills commit the course of
killing, and under what conditions does each course through false view
become a course of karma? Their defining characteristics must now be
stated.

“Killing is the deliberate, non-mistaken killing of another.” If one forms
the representation, “I shall kill this one,” kills another being, and
kills that very being rather than someone else through confusion, this is
the course of killing.

What if one kills while uncertain whether the target is alive or not, or
whether it is this being or another? Even then, one necessarily reaches a
decision before striking: “Whoever it is, let it be.” A mind willing to
destroy life has thereby been formed.

How can killing occur when the aggregates are momentary? One account calls
`prāṇa` a vital wind supported by body and mind: killing brings that to an
end, as one extinguishes a lamp or stops the sound of a bell. Alternatively,
`prāṇa` is the life faculty, and one brings that to cessation. If one
obstructs the arising of even a single moment of life that otherwise would
arise, one is touched by the fault of killing; otherwise one is not.

Whose life is it? It is the life of the one who dies through its absence.
The force of the genitive “whose” will be examined later in the discussion
of the person. The Blessed One nevertheless said: “When vitality, heat,
and consciousness leave this body, it lies cast away like an insentient
piece of wood.” Therefore a body possessing the faculties is called living;
one without them is called dead.

The Nirgranthas maintain that even an unintended killing of a living being
produces demerit for the causal agent, just as contact with fire produces
burning. The Bhāṣya presses unwanted consequences against this view. Similar
fault would follow in cases such as merely seeing another's spouse; in the
pain connected with a Nirgrantha's hair-plucking; for a teacher prescribing
painful austerity; for a donor when illness and death follow; for physicians
when treatment causes suffering or death; and for mother and fetus because
each can occasion suffering for the other. The witness is compressed in
this list, but the argumentative target is clear: causal contribution alone
would distribute moral fault too broadly.

The intended victim would also become connected with the act, as fire burns
its own support. Yet a mere result does not generate the consequence where
the relevant practical connection is absent—just as someone who causes
another person to touch fire is not thereby burned. Otherwise insentient
wood and similar objects could incur evil when the collapse of a house
kills living beings. An analogy alone does not prove the Nirgrantha thesis.

The definition of killing is complete. “Taking what is not given is the
appropriation of another's possession by force or stealth.” The condition
“without mistake” continues to apply. If one makes another's possession
one's own by force or stealth, and the thing taken is the very object toward
which the forcible or furtive intention was directed rather than another
object through confusion, taking what is not given is complete.

One who removes something from a stūpa takes what belongs to the Buddha,
because, according to one position, the Blessed One accepted all such
property at the time of final nirvāṇa. Others say the offense is against
those charged with protecting it. One who extracts ownerless treasure takes
from the lord of that territory. The witness then gives a compressed and
difficult allocation concerning `parivartaka`, performance of the relevant
act, territorial boundaries, and—in the unperformed case—all Buddhas and
disciples. Its exact institutional application remains uncertain pending
comparison with another witness.

## 4. Killing as a Constituted Course

The definition requires a complete configuration:

```text
living other
    + deliberate lethal determination
    + non-mistaken objective relation
    + effective interruption of life
    + death as fulfilled result
        = prāṇātipāta
```

`Saṃcintya` excludes accidental production of death. `Abhrānti` maintains
the unity between intended and actual objective basis. The doubtful-target
case shows that exact individual identification is not always necessary:
the agent can universalize the destructive intention as “whoever it is.”
What remains decisive is the settled willingness to destroy the life
actually struck.

Momentariness does not dissolve the practical course. Killing is formulated
as obstruction of the conditioned continuation of life:

```text
a life-moment would arise under its conditions
    → deliberate interference prevents its arising
    → living continuation is interrupted
```

The course therefore requires no permanent life-substance. At the same
time, the Bhāṣya refuses to let causal production alone constitute moral
authorship. Its reply to the Nirgranthas insists upon the intentional and
objective practical relation that makes the causal sequence this agent's
killing.

## 5. Appropriation as Relational Transformation

Taking what is not given also has a constituted form:

```text
possession related to another
    + no act of giving
    + recognition of that possession
    + intention directed toward that object
    + seizure by force or stealth
    + making it one's own
        = adattādāna
```

`Svīkriyā`, making one's own, is the center of the definition. A mere change
of location does not yet determine theft. The course transforms a practical
relation: what is recognized as another's is treated as mine without a
legitimate transfer.

The stūpa and treasure cases confirm that an objective basis is never a
bare thing. Classification depends upon the network of possession,
dedication, guardianship, territorial rule, and intended appropriation
within which the thing stands. The uncertain final sentence should not be
used to construct a more exact institutional rule until its readings have
been checked.

## 6. The Argument Against Mere Causal Morality

The Nirgrantha dispute isolates a major logical distinction:

```text
causing an event
    ≠
constituting a karmic course
```

Physical causation belongs to the completed event, but karmic authorship
also requires volitional direction and the relevant object-relation. If
causation alone were sufficient, responsibility would spread to patients,
victims, tools, buildings, wood, and every remote condition of an injury.

The Bhāṣya is therefore defending a precise middle:

```text
intention without completed result
    is not the principal killing-course

completed result without intentional practical relation
    is not the principal killing-course

intention + objective relation + effective completion
    constitutes the course
```

This is the Course of Action as an intelligent Form rather than a visible
event or an inward state considered separately.

## 7. Organon Contact Point

The analysis supplies an executable judgment schema:

```text
<Root,
 Intention,
 Recognition,
 Objective Basis,
 Operation,
 Result,
 Completion>
```

Killing and appropriation differ because their objective relations differ.
Killing negates a living continuation. Taking what is not given negates an
existing relation of possession through unauthorized appropriation. The
same physical movement can consequently belong to different courses or to
no completed course at all.

For applied Science of Knowing, the decisive operation is **constitution**:
the agent must reconstruct how Principle, representation, object, action,
and result become one determinate practical conclusion. This formulation is
the Organon reconstruction; the definitions, momentariness analysis,
Nirgrantha dispute, and property cases belong to the Bhāṣya.
