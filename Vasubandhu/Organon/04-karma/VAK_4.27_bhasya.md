# VAK 4.27 Bhāṣya — The Duration and Purpose of Undertaking

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

For how long should this prātimokṣa restraint be undertaken? “The
undertaking of restraint is for life or for a day and night.” The
seven classes of prātimokṣa restraint are undertaken for life, while
the upavāsa restraint is undertaken for a day and night. That is the
rule. Why? There are two temporal limits: the limit of a day and
night, and the limit of a life. Fortnights and other periods consist
of repeated days and nights. What is this dharma called time? The
word designates conditioned processes: on the continents a state of
light is called day, and a state of darkness night.

It is understandable that restraint does not arise after the end of
life, even if the undertaking projected beyond it: the support is
different, and there is neither application of the practice nor
recollection there. But what prevents a person from undertaking
upavāsa for five or ten nights, so that several upavāsa restraints
would arise? An answer appeals to the Blessed One's teaching of
upavāsa as a day-and-night observance.

This must now be examined. Did the Tathāgata teach a single day and
night because he saw that restraint cannot arise beyond it? Or did
he teach it to engage people of weak faculties in undertaking
restraint even for one day and night? Why ask? Because the arising
of restraint beyond one day and night does not contradict reason.
The Vaibhāṣikas, however, do not accept instruction of a longer
upavāsa for anyone.

What is the temporal rule for non-restraint? “There is no
non-restraint for a day and night.” Non-restraint arises from
undertaking evil action for life, not for a day and night as with
upavāsa. Why? “For, it is said, it is not undertaken in that way.”
Because the act is censured, no one undertakes non-restraint with
the formula “May I be unrestrained for one day and night.” But then
no one undertakes it with the formula “May I be unrestrained for
life” either. On that reasoning, no one would acquire lifelong
non-restraint.

Even without such an explicit undertaking, a person performing the
act with an utterly corrupted intention acquires non-restraint;
someone whose intention is corrupted only for a limited period does
not acquire it in that way. Upavāsa restraint, by contrast, is
acquired through the force of undertaking even when the underlying
intention is not lifelong, because the person seeks restraint. If
someone deliberately sought and undertook non-restraint for a
limited time, that person would indeed acquire it. The passage
declines to establish this as the ordinary rule because no such
case is observed [the source wording here is uncertain].

The Sautrāntikas say that non-restraint, like avijñapti, does not
exist as a distinct substance. Rather, non-restraint is the
intention toward evil action with its continuing connection. Thus a
person is said to possess it even when a wholesome mind arises,
because the intention has not been repudiated.

## 4. Two Limits and the Status of Time

The initial contrast is exact: seven prātimokṣa classes are
undertaken for life; the upavāsa observance is undertaken for a
complete day and night. The explanation of `kāla` is equally
methodological. Time is named through states of conditioned
processes, illustrated by light and darkness. The prose does not
need time as an additional independently specified substance to
state the two practical limits.

The lifelong limit is tied to the bearer. After death the support
is no longer of the same kind, and the prior undertaking is not
applied or recalled there. The argument for a one-day upavāsa limit
is different; it raises the possibility of repeating an observance
over a longer period. These two reasons should not be conflated.

## 5. A Rule Tested Against Its Purpose

The key dialectical turn is `idam idānīṃ saṃpradhāryam`, “this must
now be examined.” The one-day prescription may express an intrinsic
limit on the arising of upavāsa restraint, or it may be a teaching
fitted to persons of weaker faculties. `Yuktyavirodha`, absence of
contradiction in reason, keeps the latter possibility open even as
the Vaibhāṣikas reject teaching a longer observance.

The discussion of non-restraint performs a second test. If an
explicitly stated duration were required, the lack of a lifelong
formula would undermine lifelong non-restraint too. The reply
therefore turns to `āśaya`, the depth and duration of practical
intention, and to the special force conferred by the formal
undertaking of upavāsa. The text even allows in principle that a
deliberately time-limited undertaking of non-restraint would
generate it. Its usual absence is not offered as a strict logical
impossibility.

## 6. The Sautrāntika Alternative

The Sautrāntikas then change the ontological account. They deny
non-restraint as a separate `dravya`, just as they deny a distinct
avijñapti substance. Their proposed bearer of continuity is
`pāpakriyābhisaṃdhi` with an ongoing connection. A wholesome
present mind need not terminate that standing intention while it
remains unrepudiated. This is a reported school position within
the Bhāṣya, not an unqualified restatement of the preceding
Vaibhāṣika temporal scheme.

## 7. Organon Contact Point — Dialectical Dhyāna

For our method, this is an unusually strong instance of dhyāna as
sustained dialectical attention to a practical form. We hold the
rule, test a longer possible undertaking, distinguish the rule's
logical limit from its pedagogical purpose, and return to the
underlying `āśaya` that makes acquisition intelligible. The
teleological question is what the one-day form is **for** and whom
it enables to undertake restraint. This is our Fichte–Hegel Yoga
reading of Vasubandhu's argumentative movement; the Bhāṣya itself
does not call that movement “dialectical dhyāna.”
