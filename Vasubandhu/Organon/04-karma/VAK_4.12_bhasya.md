# VAK_4.12 Bhāṣya — Ethical Variation Within an Action

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> pravartake śubhādau hi syāt tridhāpy anuvartakam /
> tulyaṃ muneḥ śubhaṃ yāvan nobhayaṃ tu vipākajam // 4.12 //

> With a wholesome initiator, and likewise with the other kinds, the
> accompanying cognition may have any of three ethical qualities. In
> the Sage its relation is constrained toward the wholesome.
> Maturation-born cognition performs neither function.

The Bhāṣya gives the general variation, reports a dispute about the
Buddha, and then asks which cognition determines the ethical quality
of manifest action. The Kārikā study is `VAK_4.12.md`.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Does the accompanying cognition necessarily have the same character
as the initiating cognition? It does not invariably do so. “When the
initiator is wholesome and so forth, the accompanying cognition may
be threefold.” With a wholesome initiator, the accompanying cognition
may be wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate. The same applies
when the initiator is unwholesome or indeterminate.

“In the Sage it is corresponding.” For the Blessed Buddha, the
accompanying cognition corresponds to the initiator: wholesome with
wholesome, indeterminate with indeterminate. “As far as the wholesome”:
even where the initiator is indeterminate, the accompanying cognition
may be wholesome; but a wholesome initiator is never followed by an
indeterminate accompanier. This can occur while the Buddhas' teaching
is being recited.

Members of another school say that Buddhas have no indeterminate mind
at all: their continua are exclusively wholesome because they are
always concentrated. A sūtra says that the noble one is concentrated
while walking, standing, sleeping, and sitting. The Vaibhāṣikas reply
that this means the Buddha's mind does not scatter involuntarily; it
does not mean Buddhas lack indeterminate cognitions born from karmic
maturation, connected with bodily deportment, or connected with
magical creation.

It was said that mental cognition to be abandoned through cultivation
can both initiate and accompany action. It should be understood to
occur in each of the three ethical kinds. “Maturation-born cognition,
however, does neither”: it neither initiates nor accompanies because
it proceeds without deliberate constructive activity.

Is the ethical quality of manifestation then determined by the
initiator or by the accompanying cognition? If by the initiator,
obscured-indeterminate manifestation would occur here too, since
views of the personality and of extremes can initiate action. One
would have to specify that not every mind abandoned by seeing is the
relevant initiator. If, instead, manifestation followed the
accompanying cognition, the manifestation involved in undertaking
prātimokṣa restraint could fail to be wholesome when the concurrent
mind is unwholesome or indeterminate.

The answer is that manifestation follows the initiator, but not
simply a mind abandoned by seeing, since a mind abandoned through
cultivation intervenes. An objection says: if manifestation's ethical
quality does not depend on the accompanying cognition, one cannot
simply argue that the sūtra refers to causal rather than
contemporaneous origination and therefore that no
obscured-indeterminate manifestation occurs here. The reply specifies
that the sūtra concerns a causal origin **separated from the action by
another [mind]**. The discussion is concluded.

The final exchange is densely compressed. The relation between the
intervening cultivation-abandoned mind and the status of
obscured-indeterminate manifestation is presented provisionally;
its exact wording requires independent textual comparison.

## 4. Argument and Voices

The unit first denies that initiator and accompanier must share one
ethical quality. It then introduces the Buddha as a special case,
reports another school's denial of indeterminate Buddha-mind, and
gives the Vaibhāṣika reply. It excludes maturation-born cognition
from both action roles. Finally it tests which cognition gives
`vijñapti` its ethical quality and closes with a qualified answer
about the intervening mind. The Vaibhāṣika list contains three
indeterminate types, not just maturation-born and creation-related
cognition.

`Nikāyāntarīyāḥ` explicitly marks the other-school claim; `iti
vaibhāṣikāḥ` marks the response. The final objections are dialectical
and not securely assigned to a named school.

## 5. Variation and the Buddha Case

For ordinary action the accompanier may have any of three ethical
qualities whatever the initiator's quality. Causal continuity therefore
does not entail ethical sameness at each cognitive moment. The
Buddha-case is asymmetric: the commentary permits wholesome
accompaniment after an indeterminate initiator, but rules out
indeterminate accompaniment after a wholesome initiator.

The inter-school dispute must remain visible. “Always concentrated”
is read by the Vaibhāṣikas as freedom from involuntary dispersion,
not as proof that every Buddha-cognition is wholesome. The three
indeterminate types they retain are `vipākaja`, `airyāpathika`, and
`nirmāṇa-citta`; the middle term concerns bodily deportment.

## 6. Resultant Cognition and Present Action

`Vipākaja citta` is neither `pravartaka` nor `anuvartaka`, because
its flow lacks deliberate constructive activity. A cognition may be
a result of past karma without serving as an active condition of
the bodily or verbal action now at issue. This is a functional
distinction, not a claim that maturation-born cognition is causally
unrelated to everything else.

## 7. Which Cognition Gives Manifestation Its Quality?

The restored closing exchange prevents a simple rule such as
“manifest action takes the quality of whatever mind accompanies it.”
The Bhāṣya places ethical attribution with the initiating causal
line, while qualifying which initiator is operative where another
mind intervenes. The prātimokṣa example shows why concurrent
unwholesome or indeterminate cognition cannot by itself erase the
wholesomeness of the manifest undertaking.

This point prepares the move into vows, but the verse still belongs
to the general account of karma's initiation, accompaniment, and
ethical attribution.

## 8. Translation and Doctrinal Limits

“Maturation-born” renders `vipākaja`; it is a product of prior action,
not a fourth ethical quality. `Tulyaṃ muneḥ` is not absolute identity,
because `śubhaṃ yāvat` explicitly allows an asymmetry. The source's
rough Buddha-mind compound and final `anyavyavahitaṃ` need critical
comparison before the precise force of the final qualification is
settled. “While the Buddhas' teaching is being recited” follows the
passive `āmnāyamānā`; the source does not merely say that teaching
is remembered by the commentator. The division of
`vipākajairyā pathikanirmāṇacittāni` is supported by the separate
Chapter 2 list of indeterminate cognitions.

The conventional translation keeps the reported school positions
distinct from the author's running argument. It does not treat
the other school's claim about Buddha-mind as the Vaibhāṣika view.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

**Textual determination.** The first twelve verses have now built
the working principles of karma: volition and expression, manifest
and non-manifest action, momentary configuration, continuing efficacy,
ethical quality, material support, and the two functions of an
originating mind. The last exchange shows why vows cannot simply
be read off from whichever mental episode happens to coincide with
their outward undertaking.

**Organon reconstruction.** Calling 4.01–4.12 a “Principle of Karma”
is a useful section title for our second pass. It names the cluster
of determinations that makes the move to restraint intelligible,
without claiming that Vasubandhu formally titles it so.
