# VAK_4.12 Bhāṣya — Ethical Variation Within an Action

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> pravartake śubha-ādau hi syāt tridhāpy anuvartakam /
> tulyaṃ muneḥ śubhaṃ yāvan nobhayaṃ tu vipāka-jam // 4.12 //


> When the initiator is wholesome and so forth, the accompanier may nevertheless
> be of any of the three ethical kinds. For the Sage, they correspond within the
> wholesome range. Maturation-born Consciousness is neither.

The Bhāṣya specifies the ordinary threefold range, qualifies the
Buddha-case, and completes the final clause by identifying maturation-born
consciousness as neither initiating nor accompanying. The kārikā study is
`VAK_4.12.md`.

## 3. Continuous Translation

Does the accompanying Cognition necessarily have the same ethical quality as
the initiator? “This is not an invariant rule.” When the initiator is
wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate, the accompanying Cognition may be
wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate.

“For the Sage, corresponding.” For the Blessed Buddha, the accompanier
corresponds with the initiator: wholesome with wholesome, indeterminate with
indeterminate. “Within the wholesome limit”: the accompanying Cognition may
also be wholesome when the initiator is indeterminate, but a wholesome
initiator is never followed by an indeterminate accompanier. This occurs
while the Buddhas' teaching is being recited.

Members of another school say that Buddhas have no indeterminate
Consciousness: their continua are exclusively wholesome because they are
always concentrated. A sūtra says that a nāga is concentrated while
walking, standing, sleeping, and sitting. The Vaibhāṣikas explain this as
freedom from involuntary scattering; it does not mean that Buddhas lack
indeterminate maturation-born, posture-related, or creation-related
Cognitions.

Mind-Cognition abandoned through cultivation can both initiate and
accompany, and may be of any of the three ethical classes.
“Maturation-born [Consciousness], however, is neither.” It is neither
initiating nor accompanying because it proceeds without deliberate
constructive activity.

The commentary then asks whether the ethical quality of manifestation
follows the initiator or the accompanier. If it follows the initiator,
obscured-indeterminate manifestation would also occur here, since views of
personality and extremes can initiate action; one would then need to specify
which seeing-abandoned initiator is meant. If it follows the accompanier,
manifestation undertaken with prātimokṣa restraint could fail to be
wholesome when the accompanying Consciousness is unwholesome or
indeterminate.

The reply relates manifestation to its initiating causal sequence, but not
to every seeing-abandoned Consciousness without qualification: a
Consciousness abandoned through cultivation intervenes. The final objection
presses the earlier formulation: if manifestation's ethical quality does not
depend on its accompanier, one should not say simply that the sūtra names
causal rather than contemporaneous origination and therefore there is no
obscured-indeterminate manifestation here. The response says more precisely
that the sūtra refers to causal origination separated by another
[Consciousness]. The discussion is concluded.

The final exchange is tightly compressed. Its exact wording and the force
of the intervening Consciousness remain provisional.

## 4. Argument and Voices

The Bhāṣya first rejects an invariant match between initiator and
accompanier. It then reports a special rule for the Buddha, followed by a
dispute: another school denies indeterminate Buddha-consciousness, while the
Vaibhāṣikas retain three kinds of indeterminate cognition and interpret
concentration as non-scattering rather than as the absence of all
indeterminate states.

The commentary confirms that Mind-Cognition abandoned through cultivation
can perform both action functions, while maturation-born consciousness
performs neither. It then tests how the ethical quality of manifestation
relates to the initiating and accompanying cognitions. `Nikāyāntarīyāḥ`
marks the other-school position; `iti vaibhāṣikāḥ` marks the response.
The final objections are not securely assigned to a named school.

## 5. Variation and the Buddha Case

For ordinary action, the accompanying Cognition may be any of the three
ethical classes, whatever the initiator's class. The Buddha-case is
asymmetric: an indeterminate initiator may have an indeterminate or
wholesome accompanier, but a wholesome initiator is not followed by an
indeterminate one.

The other school's statement that Buddhas have no indeterminate
consciousness must remain distinct from the Vaibhāṣika reply. The latter
allows `vipākaja`, `airyāpathika`, and `nirmāṇa-citta`; the cited continuous
concentration concerns involuntary dispersion, not the absence of every
indeterminate Cognition.

## 6. Maturation-born Cognition

`Vipākaja-citta` is neither initiating nor accompanying because it proceeds
without deliberate constructive activity. This is a functional claim about
present action; it does not say that maturation-born consciousness has no
causal relation to anything.

## 7. Ethical Quality of Manifestation

The final exchange prevents a simple rule that manifestation takes the
ethical quality of whichever consciousness accompanies it. The Bhāṣya
relates it to the initiator, while qualifying which initiating sequence is
relevant: another, cultivation-abandoned consciousness intervenes between
the seeing-abandoned causal mind and the manifestation.

The example of prātimokṣa manifestation shows why a concurrently
unwholesome or indeterminate consciousness cannot by itself erase the
wholesome classification of the undertaking. The final formulation,
`anyavyavahitaṃ hetusamutthānaṃ`, is rendered provisionally as causal
origination separated by another cognition.

## 8. Translation and Doctrinal Limits

`Tulyaṃ muneḥ śubhaṃ yāvat` is not exceptionless identity: the Bhāṣya
specifies an asymmetry and allows wholesome accompaniment with an
indeterminate initiator. “Maturation-born” renders `vipākaja`; it is not a
fourth ethical quality. The three indeterminate Buddha-Cognitions are
provisionally identified from the compressed compound and the Chapter 2
inventory.

`Akukśala` and `vipāpkajam` are rough witness forms. The final
`anyavyavahitaṃ` qualification and the exact relation of the intervening
cultivation-abandoned consciousness need independent witness comparison.
The translation keeps the other-school view distinct from the Vaibhāṣika
reply.

## 9. Interpretation

The Bhāṣya allows ethical variation between the cognition initiating an
action and the cognition accompanying its performance. Its Buddha-case is
constrained, while its final analysis of manifestation relates ethical
classification to the initiating causal sequence without reducing
everything to simple simultaneity. The closing qualification remains
provisional.

The proposed `Dhātu:Indriya :: Loka:Karma` square and Opposition:Relation
framework is recorded separately as a provisional Organon thesis, not as a
claim made by this passage.

In the Kośa's broader system, Discriminative Cognition (*vijñāna*) governs
Mind (*manas*) and guides Dharma Base; keep it distinct from
consciousness (*citta*) and manifestation (*vijñapti*) in this argument.
The same `avijñapti` is classified in Form Base and Dharma Base, with
*vijñāna* bearing the stated *prati* relation to it.
