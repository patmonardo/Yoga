# VAK_4.58 Bhāṣya — Disturbance of Consciousness and Its Conditions

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> cittakṣepo manaścitte sa ca karmavipākajaḥ /
> bhayopaghātavaiṣamyaśokaiś cākurukāminām // 4.58 //

> Disturbance of Consciousness occurs in Mind Cognition; it is born from karmic
> maturation, and also from fear, injury, imbalance, and grief among Desire
> Realm beings other than the Kurus.

The last phrase follows the Kārikā's `a-kuru-kāminām` and its parallel
Chinese translation. The running Sanskrit prose spelling is irregular.

## 3. Continuous Translation

But when beings experience disturbance of Consciousness, in which
Consciousness does it occur, and from what cause? “Disturbance of
Consciousness occurs in Mind Cognition.” That means `manovijñāna`.
Consciousness is not disturbed in the five sensory Cognition-series,
because they do not discriminate. “It also arises from karmic maturation.”
Beings who disturb the Consciousness of others by substances or spells,
make them drink poison or alcohol
against their will, terrify them, set fire in hunting grounds or
forests, throw them from heights, or otherwise cause them to lose
memory later undergo disturbance of Consciousness through the maturation of
that karma.

It can also arise “from fear, injury, imbalance, and grief.” Fear
arises, for example, when nonhuman beings approach humans in a
frightening form. Injury occurs when angry nonhuman beings strike
people at vulnerable points. Imbalance occurs when wind, bile, and
phlegm become aggravated. Grief is illustrated by Vāsiṣṭhī and
others. If Mind is disturbed and disturbance of Consciousness is born
of karmic maturation, why does this not make mental feeling itself
a maturation, contrary to 4.57? We do not say that the disturbed
Consciousness itself is the maturation. The aggravation of the Great Elements is
the maturation; the disturbance arises from that, and is therefore
called *born from* maturation. Through imbalance of a bodily Principle
(`dhātu`) produced by karma, Consciousness becomes confused, helpless,
and deprived of memory; this is what is called disturbed.

Accordingly, a fourfold distinction is possible between `kṣipta` and
`vikṣipta`. For its first case the witness reads “disturbed, not disturbed,
for a distracted Consciousness.” An afflicted Consciousness is distracted
but not disturbed when otherwise healthy; an afflicted, disturbed
Consciousness is both; a healthy, unafflicted Consciousness is neither.

Whose Consciousness is disturbed? “Desire Realm beings other than the
Kurus.” The prose glosses the range as non-Kuru beings in the Desire
Realm. Such disturbance occurs even among gods, and
all the more among humans, animals, and hungry ghosts. Hell beings,
the text says, are constantly disturbed: assaulted by intense pains,
they scarcely attend even to themselves, let alone to what should
or should not be done. A lamenting hell being is offered as an
example. Noble persons other than the Buddha may also undergo mental
disturbance through imbalance of the Elements, but not through karma:
fixed karma has already matured, and indeterminate karma does not
mature in this manner. They are not disturbed through fear, having
passed beyond five fears; nor through injury, since they do not
perform the offensive acts that provoke hostile nonhuman beings;
nor through grief, because they know the nature of things.

## 4. The Causal Distinction

The central reply preserves 4.57: `vipākaja`, *arising from
maturation*, is not identical with `vipāka`, *maturation itself*.
Here the aggravated Great Elements are classed as maturation; the
mental disturbance follows from their imbalance. This is the
passage's technical claim, distinct from any present-day medical
account of mental distress.

The last part differentiates populations and causes, and contains
textual difficulties. The first quadrant is damaged in this Sanskrit
witness and is supported by the parallel Chinese Bhāṣya.
The claims about gods, hell beings, and noble persons belong to the
Kośa's cosmological and path framework.

## 5. Interpretation

For the Organon, the useful distinction is between a result and a
state that arises *because of* that result. A causal chain should
retain this intermediate condition rather than identify every later
state directly with the initial karmic maturation. This is a
project comparison, not a medical classification.

Read beside 4.56, the peace of Consciousness after emergence from
cessation and the disturbance described here can be understood as
conditions of Consciousness rather than classes of sensation, intuition,
or concept. Neither passage states that peace is Consciousness's
invariant natural state.
