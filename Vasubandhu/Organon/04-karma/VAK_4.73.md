# VAK_4.73

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

```sanskrit
प्राणातिपातः संचिन्त्य परस्याभ्रान्तिमारणम् ।
अदत्तादानमन्यस्वस्वीकृया बलचौर्यतः ॥ ४.७३ ॥
```

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

```text
prāṇātipātaḥ saṃcintya parasyābhrānti-māraṇam /

```

## 3. Padaccheda and Lexical Analysis

```text
prāṇa-atipātaḥ | saṃcintya | parasya |
a-bhrānti-māraṇam |
adatta-ādānam | anya-sva-svīkriyā |
bala-cauryataḥ
```

| Form | Morphology | Contextual sense |
|---|---|---|
| `prāṇa-atipātaḥ` | nominative masculine singular compound | destruction of life; killing-course |
| `saṃcintya` | absolutive of `sam-√cint` | having deliberately considered; intentionally |
| `parasya` | genitive singular | of another |
| `a-bhrānti-māraṇam` | nominative neuter singular compound | non-mistaken killing |
| `adatta-ādānam` | nominative neuter singular compound | taking what has not been given |
| `anya-sva` | compound member | another's possession |
| `svīkriyā` | nominative feminine singular | making one's own; appropriation |
| `bala-cauryataḥ` | ablatival compound | by force or stealth |

**Two bodily courses**

The verse begins the formal definition (`lakṣaṇa`) of the ten karmapathas with
the first two bodily courses:

```text
prāṇātipāta
    destruction of life

adattādāna
    taking what is not given
```

**`Abhrānti` carries forward**

The Bhāṣya explicitly states that the non-mistake condition also governs the
definition of taking what is not given. The compact verse voices it in the
first half; the commentary extends its syntactic force into the second.

## 4. Grammar

**Definition of destruction of life**

```text
parasya
    saṃcintya
    abhrānti-māraṇam
        prāṇa-atipātaḥ [bhavati]
```

> Destruction of life is the deliberate, non-mistaken killing of another.

`Saṃcintya` makes prior intentional determination constitutive. `Abhrānti`
requires continuity between the being lethally intended and the being
actually killed.

**Definition of taking what is not given**

```text
anya-svasya
    [abhrānti]
    bala-cauryataḥ
    svīkriyā
        adatta-ādānam [bhavati]
```

> Taking what is not given is the non-mistaken appropriation of another's
> possession by force or stealth.

`Bala-cauryataḥ` gives alternative modes of taking. `Svīkriyā`, rather than
mere movement of an item, supplies the defining act of making another's
possession one's own.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> Destruction of life is the deliberate, non-mistaken killing of another.
> Taking what is not given is the appropriation of another's possession by
> force or stealth.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> The karmapatha of destroying life is completed when one deliberately forms
> the determination to kill a particular living other and kills that very
> being without confusion of the target. Even initial uncertainty does not
> prevent completion when one finally strikes under the settled resolve to
> destroy life regardless of which being it is. Taking what is not given is
> completed when one non-mistakenly makes another's possession one's own by
> overt force or covert theft. Mere production of death or displacement of
> property does not by itself establish either karmapatha.

The doubtful-target case, the extension of `abhrānti` to theft, and the
distinction between physical causation and karmic authorship are supplied by
the Bhāṣya.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> A bodily event becomes a determinate karmic course through the unity of
> intentional direction, objective relation, execution, and fulfilled result.
> Killing is not death considered merely as a physical occurrence; it is the
> deliberate interruption of another conditioned life-series under a
> non-mistaken lethal determination. Theft is not an object's spatial
> displacement; it is the practical transformation of another's ungiven
> possession into what the taker treats as their own. Moral form lies in the
> complete relation, not in the visible movement alone.

The Bhāṣya joins this intentional account to momentariness without requiring
the annihilation of an enduring life-substance.

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**`Prāṇa` — life**

Within `prāṇātipāta`, life or living vitality, not physiological breath alone.
The Bhāṣya offers vital wind supported by body and mind as one formulation and
`jīvitendriya`, the life faculty, as the more exact Abhidharma determination.

**`Atipāta` — striking down or destruction**

The interruption of living continuation. VAK 4.72 already located principal
completion at the death of the victim.

**`Saṃcintya` — deliberately**

Having formed the lethal representation, “I shall kill this one.” It excludes
purely accidental causal production of death from the completed karmapatha.

**`Abhrānti` — non-mistake in the objective relation**

The being or possession intended must be the one actually affected. This is
more precise than generic absence of error: it preserves the linkage between
intended basis and completed result.

**`Tyāgacitta` — destructive relinquishing mind**

Bhāṣya terminology explaining the doubtful case. A person initially unsure
whether the target is alive or which being it is can nevertheless settle upon
“whoever it is” and strike with a formed willingness to relinquish or destroy
that life.

**`Jīvitendriya` and `jīvitakṣaṇa`**

The life faculty and a moment of life. The Bhāṣya states that obstructing even
one life-moment that otherwise would arise incurs the fault of destruction of
life.

**`Adatta-ādāna` — taking what is not given**

The compound retains the relational condition: no giving or legitimate
transfer has occurred.

**`Anya-sva` — another's possession**

What stands in a recognized possessive relation to another. The Bhāṣya's
special cases concerning stūpa property and treasure show that determining
whose possession is violated matters to classification.

**`Svīkriyā` — appropriation**

Making another's possession one's own. It is the practical center of the
theft-definition.

**`Bala` and `caurya` — force and stealth**

Open coercive seizure and concealed taking are alternative modes realizing
the same appropriative course.

## 8. Logical Determination

**Constitutive conditions of killing**

```text
living other
    + deliberate lethal determination
    + non-mistaken target relation
    + causal interruption of life
    + death as completion
        = prāṇātipāta
```

Accordingly:

```text
intention without death
    ≠ completed killing-course

death without deliberate lethal determination
    ≠ completed killing-course

mere causal contribution
    ≠ karmic authorship
```

**Life under momentariness**

The Bhāṣya asks how killing is possible if the aggregates are momentary. Its
answer does not posit a permanent living substance:

```text
conditioned life-moment would arise
    ↓ deliberate obstruction
that moment does not arise
    ↓
fault of prāṇātipāta
```

The ordinary genitive question “whose life?” is explicitly postponed to the
later discussion of the person. It should not be used here to smuggle a
permanent subject into the definition.

**The Nirgrantha dispute**

The Bhāṣya reports a Nirgrantha position according to which unintended killing
produces demerit through mere causation, as contact with fire produces burning.
Vasubandhu rejects the analogy: if physical causation alone sufficed, even
insentient objects could become bearers of moral fault.

```text
physical causation
    necessary in the completed event
    but not sufficient for karmic authorship
```

**Constitutive conditions of theft**

```text
possession belonging to another
    + absence of giving
    + non-mistaken object relation
    + appropriative determination
    + taking by force or stealth
        = adattādāna
```

Movement and appropriation remain distinct:

```text
moving an item
    ≠ necessarily theft

making another's ungiven possession one's own
    = defining relational transformation
```

## 9. Interpretive Note

**From event to action to karmapatha**

```text
causal event
    becomes intentional action
    through practical determination and object-reference

intentional action
    becomes completed karmapatha
    through realization of its constitutive result
```

This is not the addition of a moral predicate after the event. The intention,
recognized basis, mode of execution, and completion-event constitute what the
action is.

**Two violated relations**

```text
prāṇātipāta
    intentional interruption of living continuation

adattādāna
    appropriation that negates another's possessive relation
```

The objective basis of VAK 4.71 now receives exact practical determination.
`Sattva` is not a generic object but another living continuum whose life is
interrupted; `bhoga` is not an isolated thing but a possession whose relation
to another is knowingly overridden.

**Textual restraint**

The Bhāṣya uses ordinary possessive grammar and then postpones the question of
the person. The study therefore affirms the practical reality of killing and
theft without turning a grammatical possessor into an enduring metaphysical
subject.

This is the Organon contact. The constitutive definitions, momentary-life
analysis, Nirgrantha dispute, property cases, and postponed person-question
belong to the Bhāṣya; the event-action-karmapatha sequence is the project
synthesis.
