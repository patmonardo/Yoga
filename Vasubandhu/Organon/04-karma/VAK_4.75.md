# VAK_4.75 — Seen, Heard, Thought, and Known

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

**The sixfold field**

The kārikā names consciousness associated with the eye, ear, and Mind, then
three further consciousnesses. The Bhāṣya interprets this compact structure
through six kinds of Cognition (`vijñāna`):

```text
visual Cognition
auditory Cognition
Mind-Cognition
olfactory Cognition
gustatory Cognition
tactile Cognition
```

The verse distributes these through four conventional expressions. The
distinction between the kārikā's `citta` and the Bhāṣya's `vijñāna` is
preserved: consciousness is not silently rendered as Cognition.

## 4. Grammar

**The correlative construction**

```text
yat
    through consciousness associated with the eye, ear, and Mind
    through the three remaining consciousnesses

tat
    dṛṣṭam
    śrutam
    vijñātam
    matam ca
    yathākramam uktam
```

> What is experienced through consciousness associated with the eye, ear,
> and Mind, and through the three other consciousnesses, is called seen,
> heard, known, and thought, respectively.

`Yathākramam` requires an ordered correspondence. The Bhāṣya states the
Vaibhāṣika allocation in terms of six Cognitions:

```text
visual Cognition                         → seen
auditory Cognition                       → heard
Mind-Cognition                           → known
olfactory, gustatory, tactile Cognition  → thought
```

The last collective allocation is explicitly identified as the Vaibhāṣika
account in the prose.

## 5. Translation

### Scientific English Rendering

> What is experienced through consciousness associated with the eye, ear,
> and Mind, and through the three other consciousnesses, is called seen,
> heard, known, and thought, respectively.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> What is experienced through consciousness associated with the eye, ear,
> and Mind, and through the three other consciousnesses, is called seen,
> heard, known, and thought, respectively.
>
> The Bhāṣya interprets this through the Vaibhāṣika allocation: visual
> Cognition is called seen; auditory Cognition, heard; Mind-Cognition, known;
> and olfactory, gustatory, or tactile Cognition, thought. It then challenges the
> scriptural and classificatory proof offered for this table and records
> alternative accounts in which the four expressions are determined by the
> mode of access: direct sensory presence, report from another, reasoning or
> consideration, and inward realization.

The attribution of the table, its offered proof, the criticism, and the
alternative definitions all belong to the Bhāṣya.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> Truthful speech requires a determination of the sense in which a matter is
> seen, heard, thought, or known. One account fixes these expressions by the
> cognitive basis or Condition-field; competing accounts fix them by how the
> speaker has gained access to the matter. The passage therefore does not
> deliver a single unchallenged epistemic table. It exposes a question about
> the ground of differentiation: are the predicates licensed by Faculty and
> Condition, or by the agent's mode of access?

The logical movement is:

```text
received allocation
    → explanatory proof
    → contextual criticism
    → alternative classificatory principle
```

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**`Dṛṣṭa`, `śruta`, `mata`, and `vijñāta`**

Four conventional expressions operative in truthful and false assertion:

```text
dṛṣṭa      seen
śruta      heard
mata       thought or considered
vijñāta    known
```

Their presence in the preceding scriptural classification makes this an
ethical-semantic question, not abstract epistemology detached from speech.

**`Anubhūta` — experienced**

What has been encountered through a determinate cognitive route. The dispute
concerns how that route licenses one of the four expressions.

**`Ṣoḍaśa vyavahārāḥ` — sixteen modes of assertion**

The Bhāṣya's surrounding context distinguishes eight non-noble and eight noble
usages according to whether one speaks falsely or truthfully of what was seen,
heard, thought, or known.

**The Vaibhāṣika allocation**

```text
cakṣur-vijñāna      → dṛṣṭa
śrotra-vijñāna      → śruta
mano-vijñāna        → vijñāta
ghrāṇa-vijñāna
jihvā-vijñāna       → mata
kāya-vijñāna
```

The Vaibhāṣikas argue that odor, taste, and tangibles must receive `mata`
because the other three Conditions have been assigned to seen, heard, and
known.

**`Pratyakṣa` / `pratyakṣīkṛta` — directly apprehended**

Used by alternative accounts to define what is seen more broadly through
direct sensory presence, or by the earlier teachers specifically through the
eye.

**`Yuktyanumāna` — reasoning and inference**

One alternative account calls what the agent accepts through reasoning and
inference `mata`, thought.

**`Pratyātmavedya` — inwardly experienced**

What is directly realized within one's own continuum; an alternative
definition of `vijñāta`, known.

**`Pūrvācārya` — earlier teacher**

The earlier teachers give a related but not identical access-based account:
eye-directness as seen, reception through ear or another as heard,
self-consideration as thought, and inward experience as known.

## 8. Logical Determination

**The immediate Kārikā model**

```text
MODEL A — FACULTY / OBJECT INDEXED

visual field       → seen
auditory field     → heard
mental field       → known
remaining sensory fields
                   → thought
```

This is the mapping the verse states and the Bhāṣya attributes to the
Vaibhāṣikas.

**The alternative model**

```text
MODEL B — MODE OF ACCESS INDEXED

direct apprehension       → seen
received from another     → heard
reasoned consideration    → thought
inward direct realization → known
```

Under this account, one sensory object can receive different predicates
according to how the speaker stands in relation to it. The sixth Condition
is excluded only from direct visual “seen,” not from all four expressions.

**The earlier-teacher variant**

```text
eye-directness       → seen
ear / report         → heard
self-consideration   → thought
inward experience    → known
```

This resembles Model B but should not be silently identified with it.

**Context governs proof from scripture**

The Vaibhāṣikas cite a sūtra that speaks of merely the seen in the seen,
merely the heard in the heard, and so forth. The Bhāṣya's criticism is that
the passage has another purpose: preventing the superimposition of agreeable
and disagreeable signs upon the sixfold Condition field, not defining a technical
four-term table.

```text
same vocabulary
    ≠ same doctrinal function

scriptural citation
    requires determination of contextual purpose
```

**Ethical-semantic consequence**

The practical problem remains stable across the disputed definitions:

```text
actual standing under the governing account
    must correspond to
asserted status
```

But which account governs that status is precisely what the Bhāṣya keeps open.

## 9. Interpretive Note

**Do not flatten dispute into ontology**

The first-pass witness treated the verse as a settled provenance taxonomy.
The Bhāṣya shows that this is too quick. Provenance becomes a legitimate
interpretive development only under the alternative access-based positions;
it is not the immediate and uncontested meaning of the Kārikā.

**Ground of differentiation**

The decisive philosophical question is not which table is easiest to encode,
but what relation generates the four distinctions:

```text
fixed cognitive basis and Condition?

or

mode by which the speaker acquires or realizes the matter?
```

Meaning emerges through the systematic principle governing the terms, not
through four isolated glosses.

**Use before abstraction**

The scriptural criticism gives a general discipline: identical words can
perform different work in different arguments. The sūtra's practical aim must
be determined before its terms are extracted as technical definitions.

This is the project's interpretive synthesis. The Vaibhāṣika table, scriptural rationale,
criticism, alternative accounts, and earlier-teacher account belong to the
Bhāṣya; the explicit contrast between two grounds of differentiation is the
project synthesis.
