## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

The restraint called prātimokṣa is eightfold: the restraints of monks,
nuns, women in training, male novices, female novices, male lay followers,
female lay followers, and those undertaking the fasting observance. These
eight are called prātimokṣa restraint. It is eightfold by name.

As a distinct real type, however, it is fourfold: the restraints of a monk,
a novice, a lay follower, and one observing the fast. These are four types
because each has a specifically fixed characteristic. Nun's restraint is
not different from monk's restraint; the restraints of women in training
and female novices are not different from novice restraint; and female lay
follower's restraint is not different from male lay follower's restraint.
How is this known? From the transfer of names according to `liṅga`.

Here `liṅga` names the bodily sex-characteristic by which female and male
are distinguished. Disciplinary names such as monk and nun change with
that characteristic. When the characteristic changes, someone called a
monk is called a nun, or conversely; corresponding changes occur with the
novice and lay names. Yet such a change gives no reason to abandon the
earlier restraint or acquire a new one. Therefore the natures of these
four restraints do not differ from the corresponding three named classes.

Suppose someone undertakes novice restraint after lay restraint and then
monastic restraint. Are those restraints merely larger quantities of the
same abstentions, as in the text's compressed numerical comparison, or do
they arise separately as complete restraints? The answer is: separately.
They arise unmixed, each with its own characteristic. In the three
restraints there are three abstentions from killing, and likewise through
abstention from drinking intoxicants; the same applies to the others.
What distinguishes these abstentions? Their different causal grounds.

As a person undertakes more training rules, they withdraw from more
occasions of intoxication and heedlessness, and from more causes of killing
and the other prohibited acts, while entering more conditions for
abstention. Thus differences in causal grounds distinguish the abstentions.
If the restraints were merely included one within another, relinquishing
monastic restraint would also discard all three, because the other two
would be contained in it. That is not accepted. The restraints therefore
arise separately.

They are also mutually compatible: all three can be present together.
Undertaking a later restraint does not abandon an earlier one. Otherwise,
giving up monastic restraint would leave one without even lay restraint.

## 4. The Two Tests of Difference

The first test separates **designation** from **restraint type**. The
Bhāṣya's historical example depends on its classification of bodily sex
characteristics. Its argument is that changed designation, without loss or
fresh acquisition of restraint, does not establish a new restraint nature.
The eight and four counts belong to this Abhidharma taxonomy; they are not
a general claim about every contemporary religious status or identity.

The second test separates **a larger number of rules** from **a distinct
undertaking**. Lay, novice, and monastic restraint are not just increasing
quantities of one vow. The text says each arises separately and locates the
difference in `nidāna`: the occasions of misconduct excluded and the
conditions of abstention taken up. Separate restraint does not imply
conflict; `avirodhin` says the three can coexist.

## 5. Karma and Practical Philosophy

The Kośa defines karma at 4.01 as `cetanā` and what is produced by it;
the cited sūtra speaks of volition as karma and karma after having willed.
That account is broader than this taxonomy of vows. Here the inquiry is
specific: when does a continuing restraint
count as the same determination under another name, and when does a new
undertaking establish a distinct determination? The answer needs both
identity across a name change and real difference across distinct causal
grounds.

For the Organon, this is a useful model of one Program Feature under
several designations and several compatible commitments. That comparison
is our reconstruction. The Bhāṣya itself argues about prātimokṣa
restraints, their names, acquisition, causal conditions, and coexistence.
