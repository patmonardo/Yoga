# VAK 4.75 Bhāṣya — Seen, Heard, Thought, and Known

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
cakṣuḥ śrotramanaścittairanubhūtaṃ tribhiśca yat /
taddṛṣṭaśrutavijñātaṃ mataṃ coktaṃ yathākramam // 4.75 //
```

> What is experienced by eye-, ear-, and mind-knowledge, and by the three
> remaining knowledges, is called respectively seen, heard, known, and
> thought.

The verse states a Vaibhāṣika allocation of four modes of assertion to the
six kinds of sensory and mental knowledge. The Bhāṣya presents its scriptural
and rational defense, rejects both arguments as conclusive, and records two
access-based alternatives.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

The sūtra teaches sixteen modes of assertion. To say of what was not seen
that it was seen, of what was not heard that it was heard, of what was not
thought that it was thought, or of what was not known that it was known;
and conversely, to say of what was seen that it was not seen, through saying
of what was known that it was not known—these are the eight non-noble modes
of assertion. To say that the seen was seen through saying that the known
was known, and to say that the unseen was unseen through saying that the
unknown was unknown—these are the eight noble modes.

What, then, are the defining characteristics of the seen, heard, thought,
and known? The verse says: “What is experienced by eye-, ear-, and
mind-knowledge, and by the three remaining knowledges, is called respectively
seen, heard, known, and thought.” What is experienced by visual knowledge
is called seen; what is experienced by auditory knowledge, heard; what is
experienced by mental knowledge, known; and what is experienced by
olfactory, gustatory, and bodily knowledge, thought.

Why are the last three called thought? The Vaibhāṣikas say that odors,
tastes, and tangibles are indeterminate and as though inert; therefore the
designation “thought” applies to them. What establishes this? Scripture and
reasoning.

The cited sūtra asks Māhakīmātṛ whether forms never seen before, not now
seen, and not even wished to be seen give rise to desire, passion,
affection, attachment, inclination, or resolve. The answer is no. It asks
the same concerning sounds not heard and, through the full series, dharmas
not known by mind. It then teaches that for this practitioner there will be
merely the seen in the seen and likewise merely the heard, thought, and
known.

The Vaibhāṣika inference is that because the sūtra applies “seen,” “heard,”
and “known” to three object fields, odors and the remaining fields receive
the designation “thought.” Their reasoning adds that otherwise odors,
tastes, and tangibles would fall outside all four expressions and could not
be spoken of through them.

The sūtra does not establish that technical allocation, because it has a
different purpose. The Blessed One is not teaching definitions of the four
modes of assertion there. Its apparent meaning is that within the sixfold
object field and these four conventional expressions, there should be only
the seen and so forth, without superimposing signs of agreeable and
disagreeable upon them.

What, then, are the seen, heard, thought, and known? Some say: what is
directly apprehended through the five faculties is seen; what is received
from another is heard; what one accepts through one's own reasoning and
inference is thought; and what is established as mental direct apprehension
and inwardly experienced is known.

On this account, each of the five sensory fields can be spoken of as seen,
heard, thought, or known, depending upon the relevant mode. Only the sixth,
the mental field, is excluded from being visually seen. It therefore does
not follow that odors and the other fields would fall outside all four
expressions. The Vaibhāṣika reasoning is consequently not compelling either.

The earlier teachers say: what is made directly present through the eye is
seen; what is received through the ear or reported by another is heard;
what one has oneself considered is thought; and what is inwardly experienced,
realized, and arisen is known.

Extending the treatise by association, can false speech occur when someone
produces an otherwise-state through the body? It can. For this reason it is
said that one may not act bodily yet be touched by the fault of killing;
one may act through speech or not act through speech and still be touched
by the fault of false speech; one may act bodily, or act neither bodily nor
verbally, and be touched by both faults. The text points to the corruption
of the sages' minds and to the `poṣadha` example.

But if one acts in neither manner, and if one lacking non-manifest action
has no desire-realm non-manifest action, how are the two courses established?
Effort must be made on this point. The discussion of false speech is complete.
The Bhāṣya thus records the difficulty without resolving it in this unit.

## 4. Sixteen Modes of Assertion

The sixteen modes arise by crossing four epistemic predicates with truthful
and inverted positive or negative assertion:

```text
seen / unseen
heard / unheard
thought / unthought
known / unknown

× correspondence or inversion in speech

= eight noble and eight non-noble modes
```

Truthfulness therefore includes the speaker's epistemic standing. A claim
can misrepresent not only what occurred but also whether it was seen,
heard, thought, or known. The course of false speech preserves provenance
as part of semantic integrity.

## 5. Two Grounds of Classification

The immediate Kārikā and Vaibhāṣika explanation use a faculty-indexed table:

```text
visual knowledge                  → seen
auditory knowledge                → heard
mental knowledge                  → known
olfactory, gustatory, bodily knowledge
                                  → thought
```

The alternative uses modes of access:

```text
direct sensory presence           → seen
report received from another      → heard
reasoning and inference           → thought
inward mental realization         → known
```

These are distinct principles of classification. The first assigns a fixed
predicate according to cognitive basis and field. The second classifies how
the agent obtained or realized the content. Under the second account, one
object field can support several predicates because access can vary.

The earlier teachers give a related, narrower form:

```text
eye-directness                    → seen
ear or another's report           → heard
self-consideration                → thought
inward experience and realization → known
```

The Bhāṣya preserves the disagreement. It does not replace the verse's table
with one unanimously accepted provenance theory.

## 6. Scriptural Context as Logical Constraint

The criticism of the scriptural proof is methodologically important. Shared
terms do not guarantee shared function:

```text
same words
    + different argumentative purpose
        ≠ same technical classification
```

The cited sūtra uses “seen,” “heard,” “thought,” and “known” to restrain the
superimposition of attractive and repulsive signs upon experience. The
Vaibhāṣika table uses them to divide six forms of knowledge into four
assertible statuses. A quotation cannot establish the second use until the
relation between those purposes is demonstrated.

This is powerful thinking rather than dictionary thinking: determine what
operation a term performs inside its argument before extracting it as a
definition.


A mismatch can constitute false speech when it is knowingly asserted and

The unresolved bodily-indication problem adds a warning: semantic action may

fully explain every combination. We preserve that open problem rather than
