# VAK 4.95 Bhāṣya — Projecting and Completing a Birth

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> ekaṃ janmākṣipaty ekam anekaṃ paripūrakam /
> nākṣepike samāpattī acitte prāptayo na ca // 4.95 //

> One action projects one birth; many actions complete it. The two non-mental
> attainments do not project birth, nor do acquisitions.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Does one action project one birth only, or not even one? And does one action
project one birth, or many? The established position is this:

> One [action] projects one birth.

One action projects one birth only, not many births. “Birth” here is the
designation of `nikāyasabhāga`; when that has been acquired, one is said to
have been born in that class.

But what of the elder Aniruddha's statement: “Through the maturation of that
single alms-food offering, I was born seven times among the Thirty-three
gods, and eventually born in a wealthy Śākya family”? Having gained
prosperity and remembered former births, he then performed further
wholesome action; he was indicating the originating occasion. It is like a
person who, beginning with one dinar and later acquiring a thousand, says:
“Through one dinar I obtained this prosperity.”

Others say that, taking that alms-food offering as a basis, a great stream of
intentions to give arose, with each intention appropriating some result.

Even by many actions one birth-class is projected, so that projection of
`nikāyasabhāga` does not occur piecemeal. The next sentence concerning one
action and an afflicted `nikāyasabhāga` is difficult in the witness and is
not expanded beyond its wording.

Completing action is multiple. Just as a painter outlines a form with one
stroke and completes it with many, so among people of the same class one
person may possess all faculties, major and minor limbs, and excel in color,
shape, stature, and strength, while another is deficient in one or another
of these.

Action alone is not the projector of birth; something else that has
maturation may also project it. In every case, however, the two non-mental
attainments are not projectors. Even though they are without maturation,
they do not project `nikāyasabhāga` because they are not coexistent with
action. Nor do acquisitions project it; the Bhāṣya gives the reason:
“because action has multiple results.”

## 4. Birth as `Nikāyasabhāga`

The Bhāṣya specifies the force of `janma`: it names the acquisition of a
`nikāyasabhāga`, the common class-membership through which the continuum is
said to have been born as a member of that class.

```text
projected:
    one unified class-membership

not thereby specified:
    every feature and endowment of the resulting life
```

The painter analogy addresses the latter: multiple completing actions
differentiate the particular features within a unified class-membership.

## 5. The Aniruddha Example

The reported statement seems to attribute multiple births to the maturation
of one alms-food offering. The first explanation treats the offering as the
originating occasion: after attaining prosperity and recalling former
births, Aniruddha performed further wholesome action. The dinar analogy
distinguishes an initial source from the later accumulation it occasions.

A second account, explicitly introduced by `apare`, treats the offering as
the support for a great stream of giving-volitions. Different intentions
appropriate different results. This account analyzes the apparent singular
act into a plurality of causal moments without making the offering itself a
single projector of many births.

These are two reported explanations, not one merged account.

## 6. One-to-many and Many-to-one Projection

The passage states two different cardinality claims:

```text
one action does not project many births

many actions may project one birth-class
```

The stated reason against many-to-one projection being fragmented is that
`nikāyasabhāga` is projected as a unity. Accordingly, the formula is
asymmetric; it is not a strict one-to-one bijection between individual
actions and births. The difficult sentence immediately following may
qualify the discussion for an afflicted class-membership, but the witness
does not support a more determinate reconstruction here.

## 7. Projecting and Completing Action

The painter analogy distinguishes two functions:

```text
one outline
    → establishes the unified form

many strokes
    → complete its particular features
```

Likewise, projecting action determines the unified birth-class, while
multiple completing actions account for features such as faculties, limbs,
color, form, stature, strength, and their deficiencies. Projection does not
exhaust the causal constitution of a life.

The Bhāṣya further states that action is not the only possible projector:
another factor possessing maturation may also project birth. The verse's
distinction between projecting and completing therefore does not turn
action into the sole cause of birth.

## 8. The Two Attainments and Acquisitions

The syntax and prose keep the exclusions distinct:

```text
acitte samāpattī
    the two non-mental attainments; not projectors

prāptayaḥ
    acquisitions; separately not projectors
```

The Bhāṣya specifies the attainments as the two `acitta-samāpattis`. They
lack maturation and do not coexist with action, which is the stated reason
they do not project `nikāyasabhāga`. The `prāptis` are excluded separately;
the compact explanation `karmaṇo 'nekaphalatvāt` is rendered “because action
has multiple results” without adding a further causal theory.

## 9. Interpretation

The argument distinguishes causal unity from causal simplicity. Birth as
class-membership is projected as one determination, yet the Bhāṣya allows
many actions to contribute to that unity and many further actions to complete
its particulars. The Aniruddha example tests whether ordinary singular
attribution conceals a longer sequence or a stream of volitions. Projection
and result are therefore not rendered transparently identical by naming
one initiating act: the passage itself differentiates originating occasion,
projecting function, completion, maturation, and possession.

Across the Kośa's wider cognitive movement, Discriminative Cognition joins
and governs Perception and Conception. Here, the causal account is practical:
it distinguishes what establishes a birth-class from what completes its
particularity, without making acquisition itself an act of projection.
