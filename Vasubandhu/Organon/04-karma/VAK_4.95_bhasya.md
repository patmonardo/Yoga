# VAK 4.95 Bhāṣya — Projecting and Completing a Birth

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> ekaṃ janmākṣipaty ekam anekaṃ paripūrakam /
> nākṣepike samāpattī acitte prāptayo na ca // 4.95 //

> One action projects one birth; many actions complete it. The two non-mental
> attainments do not project birth, nor do acquisitions.

## 2. Continuous Sanskrit

karma ekam janmākṣipati, athānekam? eṣa hi siddhāntaḥ:

ekaṃ janmākṣipaty ekam

nikāyasabhāgasyākhyā. tatra hi labdhe jāta ity ucyate.

yat tarhi sthavirāniruddhenoktam: "so 'haṃ tasyaikapiṇḍapātasya vipākena
saptakṛtvas trāyastriṃśeṣu deveṣūpapanno yāvad etarhy ādye śākyakule

tena hy asau samṛddhiṃ labdhvā jātismaraḥ punar anyat puṇyaṃ kṛtavān.
tata utthānaṃ darśayati sma. yathā manuṣyo dīnārottho 'nena sahasraṃ
nirviśyāha: "ekena dīnāreṇāham etad īśvaryaṃ prāptaḥ" iti.

apare tv āhuḥ: tasya taṃ piṇḍapātam adhiṣṭhānaṃ kṛtvā dānacetanānāṃ
pravāho mahān utpannaḥ; kayācit kiñcit phalaṃ parigṛhītam iti.

anekenāpy ekam ākṣipyate? mā bhūt khaṇḍaśo nikāyasabhāgasyākṣepa iti.
ekena tu karmaṇā kliṣṭasya nikāyasabhāgasyeṣyate karma.

anekaṃ paripūrakam /

yathā citrakara ekayā vartyā rūpam ālikhya bahvībhiḥ paripūrayati,

anyad api savipākam. sarvathā tu

nākṣepike samāpattī acitte prāptayo na ca // 4.95 //

savipākābhyām apy acittasamāpattibhyāṃ nikāyasabhāgo nākṣipyate,

The unit follows the source from `[258|11]` through `[259|03]`. Several
forms in the working witness are damaged. The transcription emends its
second question's `ekam` to `anekam` at `[258|11–12]` (otherwise the two
questions repeat), and its `avipākābhyām` to `savipākābhyām` at `[259|02]`.
Both readings are attested or adopted in [O’Brien-Kop's Sanskrit citations](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/ws/files/358745842/Ethical_Causality_and_Rebirth_OBRIEN-KOP_Publishedonline17September2024_GOLD_VoR_CC_BY_.pdf)
(notes 21 and 25). The witness has no explicit `na` before
`anekenāpy ekam ākṣipyate`; the interrogative punctuation above follows
the `mā bhūt` warning against
piecemeal projection, without silently inserting a negation. The text also
normalizes `etarhy ādye` (the witness has `etahyrādye`), `samṛddhiṃ`,
`etad īśvaryaṃ prāptaḥ`, and `bahvībhiḥ`. The prior `āḍhye` in this study
was an incorrect repair of the witness's `ādye`. The difficult sentence
concerning an afflicted `nikāyasabhāga` is retained closely.

## 3. Continuous Translation

Does one action project one birth only, or not even one? And do many actions
project one birth, or many? The established position is this:

> One [action] projects one birth.

One action projects one birth only, not many births. “Birth” here is the
designation of `nikāyasabhāga`; when that has been acquired, one is said to
have been born in that class.

But what of the elder Aniruddha's statement: “Through the maturation of that
single alms-food offering, I was born seven times among the Thirty-three
gods, and eventually born in a distinguished Śākya family”? Having gained
prosperity and remembered former births, he then performed further
wholesome action; he was indicating the originating occasion. It is like a
person who, beginning with one dinar and later acquiring a thousand, says:
“Through one dinar I obtained this prosperity.”

Others say that, taking that alms-food offering as a basis, a great stream of
intentions to give arose, with a certain intention appropriating some result.

Can one birth-class be projected by many actions? That would make projection
of `nikāyasabhāga` occur piecemeal. But a single action is admitted as the
action [projecting] an afflicted `nikāyasabhāga`; the final `karma` in this
sentence is syntactically difficult.

Completing action is multiple. Just as a painter outlines a form with one
brush and completes it with many, so among people of the same class one
person may possess all faculties, major and minor limbs, and excel in color,
shape, stature, and strength, while another is deficient in one or another
of these.

Action alone is not the projector of birth; something else that has
maturation may also project it. In every case, however, the two non-mental
attainments are not projectors. Even though they have maturation,
they do not project `nikāyasabhāga` because they are not coexistent with
action. Nor do acquisitions project it; the Bhāṣya gives the reason:
“because action has multiple results.”

## 4. Birth as `Nikāyasabhāga`

The Bhāṣya specifies the force of `janma`: it names the acquisition of a
`nikāyasabhāga`, the common class-membership through which the continuum is
said to have been born as a member of that class.

```text
projected:
    one unified class-membership

not thereby specified:
    every feature and endowment of the resulting life
```

The painter analogy addresses the latter: multiple completing actions
differentiate the particular features within a unified class-membership.

## 5. The Aniruddha Example

The reported statement seems to attribute multiple births to the maturation
of one alms-food offering. The first explanation treats the offering as the
originating occasion: after attaining prosperity and recalling former
births, Aniruddha performed further wholesome action. The dinar analogy
distinguishes an initial source from the later accumulation it occasions.

A second account, explicitly introduced by `apare`, takes the offering as
the basis for a great stream of giving-volitions. The witness says that a
certain volition appropriated some result (`kayācit kiñcit phalam`); it does
not assign a separate result to every volition. This account analyzes the
apparent singular act into a plurality of causal moments without making the
offering itself a single projector of many births.

These are two reported explanations, not one merged account.

## 6. The Limits on Projection

The passage imposes two limits on the projecting action:

```text
one action does not project many births
many actions do not jointly project one birth-class
```

The second limit follows from `mā bhūt khaṇḍaśaḥ`: joint projection by many
would divide the projection of a single `nikāyasabhāga` into parts. The
working witness lacks an explicit negative in the preceding clause, so this
reading depends on the force of that warning and the context. The next
sentence admits one action for the projection of an afflicted class-membership,
though its final `karma` is syntactically difficult. These restrictions
concern projection; many actions still complete the resulting birth.

## 7. Projecting and Completing Action

The painter analogy distinguishes two functions:

```text
one brush outlines the form
    → establishes its general configuration

many brushes
    → complete its particular features
```

Likewise, projecting action determines the unified birth-class, while
multiple completing actions account for features such as faculties, limbs,
color, form, stature, strength, and their deficiencies. Projection does not
exhaust the causal constitution of a life.

The Bhāṣya further states that action is not the only possible projector:
another factor possessing maturation may also project birth. The verse's
distinction between projecting and completing therefore does not turn
action into the sole cause of birth.

## 8. The Two Attainments and Acquisitions

The syntax and prose keep the exclusions distinct:

```text
acitte samāpattī
    the two non-mental attainments; not projectors

prāptayaḥ
    acquisitions; separately not projectors
```

The Bhāṣya specifies the attainments as the two `acitta-samāpattis`. On the
emended `savipākābhyām` reading, they have maturation yet do not coexist
with action; this lack of coexistence is the stated reason they do not
project `nikāyasabhāga`. The `prāptis` are excluded separately;
the compact explanation `karmaṇo 'nekaphalatvāt` is rendered “because action
has multiple results” without adding a further causal theory.

## 9. Interpretation

The argument distinguishes projection of a birth-class from completion of
its particulars. It rejects both one action projecting many births and many
actions jointly projecting one birth. The Aniruddha example tests whether
ordinary singular attribution conceals a longer sequence or a stream of
volitions. Projection
and result are therefore not rendered transparently identical by naming
one initiating act: the passage itself differentiates originating occasion,
projecting function, completion, maturation, and possession.

Across the Kośa's wider cognitive movement, Discriminative Cognition joins
and governs Perception and Conception. Here, the causal account is practical:
it distinguishes what establishes a birth-class from what completes its
particularity, without making acquisition itself an act of projection.
