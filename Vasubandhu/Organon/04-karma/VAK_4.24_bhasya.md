## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“Nor is one endowed with past obscured or unobscured [manifest
actions].” No one is endowed even with past manifest actions that are
obscured-indeterminate or unobscured-indeterminate. For the
acquisition of a weak dharma is itself weak and does not continue to
adhere. What makes this manifest action weak? Its dependence on mind.
Then, one might ask, would the same not be true of an
obscured-indeterminate mind? No. Manifest action is inert and
dependent, whereas mind is not so. A manifest action brought forth by
a weak mind is weaker still.

The expression “one established in non-restraint” has been used. What
is this non-restraint? “Non-restraint is bad conduct, bad discipline,
action, and an action-course.” These are alternative names for
non-restraint. It is called non-restraint because body and speech are
not restrained. It is called bad conduct because good people censure
it and because it has undesirable results. It is called bad
discipline because it is opposed to discipline. It is called action
because it is bodily and verbal action. It is called an action-course
because it is included in the fundamental phase.

## 4. Why These Past Manifest Actions Are Not Possessed

The first half qualifies the general rule about past vijñapti in
4.23. `Nivṛta` and `anivṛta` are not restraint and non-restraint here:
the Bhāṣya expands them as the two `avyākṛta`, ethically
indeterminate, kinds of manifest action. It denies being **endowed
with them as past**. It does not deny that an indeterminate action
occurred, nor does it state that every consequence of such an action
has vanished.

The explanation uses `prāpti`, acquisition or possession. A weak
dharma's acquisition is also weak and does not continue in the way
required for the past-possession claim. Vasubandhu gives a relational
reason for the weakness: vijñapti is `jaḍā`, inert, and `paratantrā`,
dependent. Arising from a weak mind, it is weaker still.

The interlocutor tests the reason: if weakness is mind-caused, why
would past possession not also fail for the corresponding
obscured-indeterminate mind? The answer is that **mind is not inert
and dependent in the way manifest action is**. The argument thus
distinguishes cause and dependent expression; it does not infer equal
possession rules merely from their association.

## 5. Five Grounds for Naming Non-Restraint

The second half changes question to `asaṃvara`, already used as a
standing category. The five names are `paryāyaśabdāḥ`, alternative
expressions, but each has its own explanatory ground:

| Name | Ground supplied in the Bhāṣya |
|---|---|
| `asaṃvara` | Body and speech are not restrained |
| `duścarita` | Censured by the good and bearing undesirable fruit |
| `dauḥśīlya` | Opposed to `śīla`, discipline |
| `karma` | Bodily and verbal action |
| `karmapatha` | Included in the fundamental action-course |

This is not a mere list of insults for bad behavior. One
determination is named from its restraint-status, moral appraisal,
opposition to discipline, action-nature, and position in a
`karmapatha`. The positive standing of `asaṃvara` was already
distinguished from the mere absence of restraint by the
`madhyastha` case in 4.21.

## 6. Organon Contact Point

The first movement gives our System a check on **expression and
retention**: an occurrence can be manifest without being possessed
as a past manifest dharma in every ethical mode. The second gives a
check on **naming**: several predicates can identify one practical
determination while drawing their validity from different grounds.
Neither check permits us to treat a stored record, a present act, a
standing vow, and its moral evaluation as one undifferentiated
Feature. This is a proposed Organon use, kept distinct from the
Kośa's own categories.
