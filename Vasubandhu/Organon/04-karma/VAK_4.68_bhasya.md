# VAK 4.68 Bhāṣya — Preparation, Completion, and Aftermath

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
sāmantakās tu vijñaptir avijñaptir bhaven na vā /
viparyayeṇa pṛṣṭhāni prayogas tu trimūlajaḥ // 4.68 //
```

> The proximate actions are manifest action; non-manifest action may or
> may not arise. The subsequent actions are the converse. Preparation,
> however, is born from the three roots.

The Bhāṣya articulates the bodily and verbal course as preparation
(`prayoga` or `sāmantaka`), principal completion (`maula`), and aftermath
(`pṛṣṭha`). It then asks how these phases are related to manifestation,
result, other courses, and the three unwholesome roots.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“The proximate actions are manifest action.” The actions proximate to a
course of karma necessarily have manifest action as their nature.
“Non-manifest action may or may not arise.” If one begins the preparation
under an intense afflictive domination, or through heedlessness having a
strong savor, non-manifest action arises; otherwise it does not.

“The subsequent actions are the converse.” The aftermaths of the courses
are to be understood conversely to their proximate actions. They
necessarily have non-manifest action as their nature, while manifest
action may or may not occur. If, after completing a course, one again acts
in conformity with it, manifest action occurs; otherwise it does not.

Where do the preparation, principal course, and aftermath begin and end?
Suppose someone wishing to kill an animal rises from bed, takes money,
goes out, examines the animal, buys it, brings it home, feeds it, confines
it, takes up a weapon to kill it, and strikes it once or twice. Until the
animal is deprived of life, all this is preparation. The manifestation
belonging to the blow by which it is deprived of life, together with the
non-manifest action of that moment, is the principal course of karma. One
becomes connected with the fault of killing for two reasons: through the
preparation and, once the animal has died, through completion of the
result.

The moments of non-manifest action after that constitute the aftermath.
As long as one cuts up the animal, cleans it, sells it, cooks it, feeds it
to others, or recounts the deed, the corresponding moments of manifest
action also constitute the aftermath. The other courses are to be
analyzed similarly wherever applicable. Covetousness and the other mental
courses have neither preparation nor aftermath, because their mere
immediate presence is the course of karma.

A difficulty must be considered. Do the manifest and non-manifest actions
become the course while the animal is in the state of dying, or after it
has died? What follows from this question? The witness's statement of the
first consequence is damaged, but it rejects completion while the animal
is merely dying because that would produce an unacceptable allocation of
the fault of killing. If the course occurs only once the animal has died,
however, the preceding statement that the manifestation of the fatal blow
is the principal course requires qualification.

The treatise also says that there can be an animal already killed while
the killing has not ceased, as when the animal has been deprived of life
but the undertaking has not yet subsided. One proposed explanation takes
“undertaking” there to mean aftermath. The Vaibhāṣika objects that this
conflicts with the meaning because the principal course itself has not yet
ceased. Let the interpretation avoid the fault. How does it do so? By
taking “undertaking” there to mean the principal course itself.

How, then, can the manifestation be the principal course at that later
time? Why should it not be? Because it no longer possesses causal power.
But how can the non-manifest action occur then? The resulting
determination is that at the time when the result of the preparation is
completed, both are the course of karma. The discussion is compressed and
textually difficult; it seeks to coordinate the earlier manifestation
with completion of the result rather than identify the visible blow alone
as the entire course.

A course of karma can itself function as the preparation or aftermath of
another course. All ten courses can serve as preparation for killing. For
example, one arranging a rite to kill an enemy might sacrifice an animal,
steal another's property, violate that person's partner, use false,
divisive, harsh, and disordered speech to separate the person from friends
who could provide protection, covet the person's property, entertain
hostility toward the person, and strengthen wrong view for the sake of the
killing. The other courses should be connected in corresponding ways.

Yet covetousness and the other mental courses are not properly called
preparatory actions merely as mental occurrences. The arising of a
thought alone does not constitute practical engagement, because action
has not begun.

The sūtra says: “Monks, killing is threefold: born from greed, born from
hatred, and born from delusion,” and continues through wrong view. One
must therefore explain what killing born from greed is, and likewise for
the others. The point is not that every principal course is completed by
each of greed, hatred, and delusion. Rather, “preparation is born from the
three roots”: the preparation for these courses can arise from any of the
three unwholesome roots. This explains the sūtra's statement about their
initial arising.

Killing born from greed includes depriving a living being of life for a
part of its body, for money, because one has been paid, or to protect
oneself or a friend. Killing born from hatred is undertaken to injure an
enemy. Killing born from delusion includes violence committed by
sacrificers who believe it dharma, or by kings relying upon the authority
of interpreters of law and believing that rulers gain merit by suppressing
the wicked. The commentary also attributes examples to Persians and to
others who hold that aged or sick parents, dangerous creatures, or animals
regarded as existing for consumption should be killed. Killing initiated
by wrong view is likewise born from delusion.

Taking what is not given is born from greed when one takes what one wants,
or acts for gain, honor, reputation, or protection of oneself or a friend.
It is born from hatred when directed toward injuring an enemy. It is born
from delusion when a ruler acts upon supposed religious authority to
punish the wicked. The Bhāṣya also quotes a Brahmanical claim that Brahmā
gave everything to Brahmins, so that a Brahmin who takes something merely
takes what is already his. Those holding such a view do not conceive the
property as belonging to another. Theft initiated by wrong view belongs
to the same category.

Sexual misconduct is born from greed through passion for another's
partner and similar objects, or when undertaken for gain, honor, or
protection. It is born from hatred when intended to injure an enemy. The
Bhāṣya assigns to delusion practices it attributes to Persians and a cited
ritual prescription associated with the `gosava`, as well as sayings that
represent women as objects available like public facilities. These are
polemical examples used to show action organized by false normative
belief, not neutral ethnographic descriptions.

False speech and the remaining verbal courses are born from greed or
hatred in the ways already explained. As an example of false speech born
from delusion, the Bhāṣya cites a verse claiming that five kinds of lies—
including lies in jest, concerning women, at marriage, when life is at
risk, or when all one's wealth is being taken—are not transgressions.
False speech initiated by wrong view is also born from delusion. Divisive
speech and the remaining verbal courses are born from delusion when
initiated by wrong view, as is the recitation of false treatises such as
the Vedas according to the Bhāṣya's polemical judgment.

## 4. The Course as a Three-Phase Practical Form

The passage replaces a punctual model of action with a functionally
articulated course:

```text
prayoga / sāmantaka
    action ordered toward fulfillment
        ↓
maula
    fulfillment of the defining result
        ↓
pṛṣṭha
    retained efficacy and possible conforming action
```

These phases are determined by their relation to completion, not merely by
clock time. Preparation may contain many deeds and even other complete
courses. The principal phase is fixed by fulfillment of the defining
result. The aftermath is the continuation governed by that completed
course.

The modal distribution is reciprocal:

```text
PREPARATION
    vijñapti      necessary
    avijñapti     conditional

AFTERMATH
    avijñapti     necessary
    vijñapti      conditional
```

Before completion, the course advances through manifest activity while
its non-manifest efficacy depends upon intensity. After completion, the
non-manifest determination necessarily continues, while further
manifestation depends upon whether the agent acts in conformity with what
has been completed.

The killing discussion exposes a pressure inside the analysis. The fatal
movement, its causal efficacy, the victim's death, and the principal
completion need not occupy one moment. The source does not permit these
determinations to collapse into a single visible event. Its local textual
difficulties also prevent a cleaner reconstruction from being attributed
to Vasubandhu without further witness comparison.

The statement that one karmapatha can become another's preparation or
aftermath makes the courses a relational system. A course retains its own
ethical identity while acquiring a functional position inside a more
comprehensive course. The ten are consequently neither isolated classes
nor merely consecutive events.

The mental courses establish the limit of the three-phase schema. Their
immediate presence is their principal course; a mere thought is not, for
that reason, a preparatory bodily or verbal undertaking. The Bhāṣya thus
distinguishes the constitution of a mental course from the role a mental
root plays in generating practical engagement.

## 5. Roots as Genetic Powers

`Trimūlaja` classifies preparation by its generative root rather than by
its outward shape:

```text
lobha     appropriative attraction
dveṣa     hostile repulsion
moha      deluded normative determination
```

The extended examples give `moha` special practical force. Delusion is
not depicted as an empty lack of information. A false view of dharma,
law, sacrifice, property, or permissible conduct can organize a coherent
undertaking and confer apparent legitimacy upon it. The examples are
historically polemical, but the theoretical determination is exact: an
intelligible and organized course can be generated from a false governing
principle.

This answers why the courses read as practical intelligence. The same
visible action can be generated by distinct ruling roots, and one course
can be subordinated to another as its means. Classification must therefore
determine both the act's place within a course and the Power that generates
that placement.

## 6. Organon Contact Point

For the Organon, VAK 4.68 gives the Course of Action an executable
configuration:

```text
Root
    → Preparation
    → Principal Completion
    → Aftermath
```

Across that progression, three independent coordinates must be retained:

```text
phase       preparation / principal / aftermath
mode        manifest / non-manifest
root        greed / hatred / delusion
```

This is already matrix-like. A practical event acquires meaning from its
position across all three axes, while the governing course gives the
configuration unity. A complete course may also occupy a phase inside a
higher course, so the practical Form can recursively enclose another Form.
That is the immediate contact with our `3^3` Dialectical Cube; the cube is
an Organon reconstruction rather than an explicit Abhidharma doctrine.

The governing determination can now be stated more exactly:

> `Indriya` is the dominant efficacy of a domain; `karmapatha` is the
> dominant practical determination that orders genesis, completion, and
> persistence as one course.
