# VAK_4.01 Bhāṣya — Volition and the Differentiated World

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> karmajaṃ lokavaicitryaṃ cetanā tatkṛtaṃ ca tat /
> cetanā mānasaṃ karma tajjaṃ vākkāyakarmaṇī // 4.01 //

> The differentiation of the world is born from karma. That karma is
> volition and what is produced by it. Volition is mental action; action
> born from it is verbal and bodily action.

The Kārikā study is `VAK_4.01.md`. The Bhāṣya specifies that the world at
issue includes beings and their receptacle-world, and that the karma at
issue is the karma of beings.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Homage to the Buddha.

Now, the manifold differentiation of the world of beings and the
receptacle-world has been described. By what was it made? It was certainly
not made by anyone acting with prior intelligence. By what, then? The
differentiation of the world is born from the karma of beings.

If it is born from karma, why do the karmas of beings produce attractive
things such as saffron and sandalwood, but not correspondingly attractive
bodies for those beings? [For beings whose karma is mixed, karmas of this
kind produce] bodily supports that are like wounds, and attractive
enjoyments that serve as remedies for them. For gods whose action is
unmixed, both are attractive.

What, then, is this karma? He says: “It is volition and what is produced by
it.” The sūtra says, “There are two karmas: volition as karma, and karma
after having willed.” What is described as action after having willed is
also what is produced by volition. These two karmas become three: bodily,
verbal, and mental action.

How are these actions classified? Is it by support, by intrinsic nature, or
by origination? If by support alone, only bodily action would result, since
all depend on the body. If by intrinsic nature alone, only verbal action
would result, since speech has the nature of action. If by origination
alone, only mental action would result, since all arise from mind. The
Vaibhāṣikas say that the three are classified, respectively, by these
three grounds.

Here, further, “volition is mental action”: volition is to be understood
as mental action. “Verbal and bodily actions are born from it”: what was
called action born from volition, action after having willed, is to be
understood as bodily and verbal action.

The bracketed subject and causal link in the mixed-karma reply are a
provisional construal, not a recovered reading. “Like wounds” preserves
`vraṇabhūtāḥ`; “remedies” preserves `pratīkāra`. A
[separate English translation excerpt](https://abhidharmakosa.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/09/kosa-karika-study.pdf)
also reads a contrast between mixed action and gods' unmixed action
(Chapter 4, p. 70). It is a translation check, not an independent Sanskrit
witness. Its additional account of bodily impurities and sense objects is
not present in this Sanskrit e-text and has not been imported here.
The passage does not require a one-to-one correspondence between the
pleasantness of a body and that of its enjoyments.

## 4. Argument and Voices

The opening question takes up the differentiated world described in
Lokanirdeśa and asks for its producing condition. The negative answer
excludes production by a deliberating maker; the positive answer names
the karma of beings. An objector then presses the asymmetry between
attractive enjoyments and unattractive bodies. The reply invokes mixed
and unmixed karma.

The next question changes level: what is karma itself? A cited sūtra
distinguishes volition from action after having willed. The Bhāṣya relates
that pair to bodily, verbal, and mental action, tests three possible
grounds of classification, and attributes the distributive answer to the
Vaibhāṣikas. Its closing gloss identifies volition as mental action and
volition-produced action as bodily and verbal action.

The objection at `yadi karmajaṃ kasmāt` is an argumentative voice, not the
author's settled conclusion. `iti vaibhāṣikāḥ` explicitly attributes the
three-ground solution. The sūtra citation is another distinct voice.

## 5. From Loka to Karma

`Sattva-bhājana-loka` makes the scope of `loka-vaicitrya` explicit: beings
and their environmental receptacle. The Bhāṣya's `sattvānām` makes beings'
karma the causal term. `Na khalu kenacid buddhi-pūrvakaṃ kṛtam` rules out a
world deliberately fashioned by someone. It does not deny the efficacy of
intelligence or intention within conditioned action; the subsequent
analysis makes volition central to karma.

The saffron-and-sandalwood objection matters because embodiment and
enjoyment can differ in quality. The reply distinguishes mixed from
unmixed karma across those results. This contrast is supported by the
clear continuation, especially `avyāmiśrakāriṇāṃ tu devānām`, while the
damaged preceding Sanskrit cannot support a more exact account of the
mixed agent or the mechanism of each result.

## 6. Two Karmas and Three Modes of Action

The sūtra's pair is `cetanā karma` and `cetayitvā karma`: volition itself,
and action after having willed. The Kārikā compresses the second as
`tatkṛta`, what is produced by that volition. The Bhāṣya then correlates
the pair with three domains:

```text
cetanā                         mental action
cetanā-produced action          bodily and verbal action
```

This is a genetic relation, not simply a list of three equal kinds.
`Tajjaṃ` is singular while `vākkāyakarmaṇī` is dual; the Bhāṣya makes
the compressed relation clear by supplying the sense of action generated
by volition and identifying its two bodily and verbal forms.

## 7. Grounds of Classification

The Bhāṣya asks whether bodily, verbal, and mental action are named by
`āśraya` (support), `svabhāva` (intrinsic nature), or `samutthāna`
(origination). Applied indiscriminately, each criterion would erase the
threefold distinction: all depend on body, speech has karmic nature, and
all arise from mind. The Vaibhāṣika answer distributes the criteria
respectively across bodily, verbal, and mental action.

The passage thus distinguishes a condition common to all actions from the
ground by which each class is specified. Origination from mind does not
make every action merely mental. Bodily and verbal action remain distinct
while depending on volition.

## 8. Translation and Doctrinal Limits

“Volition” renders `cetanā`; it should not be narrowed to a conscious wish
or an isolated decision. “Action after having willed” keeps the temporal
and causal force of `cetayitvā` without deciding yet how the efficacy of
an action persists. That problem opens with `vijñapti` and `avijñapti` in
4.02.

The mixed-karma reply cannot be treated as a settled translation of every
word. The exact form `cetanākṛṭaṃ` and the apparent break around
`tānyevañjātīyāti` require comparison with independent Sanskrit witnesses
and critical editions. Word division and the displayed expansion of the
sūtra citation are editorial; no lexical repair is built into the
continuous Sanskrit above.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

**Textual determination.** The Bhāṣya asks for the production of a
conditioned, differentiated world, then follows karma inward to volition
and outward to bodily and verbal action. Its first move forbids an appeal
to a prior world-designing intelligence. Its classificatory discussion
requires attention to support, nature, and origin as distinct relations.

**Organon reconstruction.** For a Rational Moral Agent, the relevant
question is how volition becomes bodily or verbal action and how such
action participates in a differentiated world. The mixed-karma objection
requires the account to distinguish embodiment from enjoyment rather
than infer one directly from the other. This is an Organon application
of the passage, not Vasubandhu's own account of a Rational Moral Agent.

The sequence can guide further work without pretending that 4.01 already
settles karmic continuity:

```text
volition → bodily and verbal action → differentiated results
                 ↑
       mode of continued efficacy: still to be examined
```
