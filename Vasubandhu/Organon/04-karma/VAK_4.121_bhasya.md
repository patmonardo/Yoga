# VAK 4.121 Bhāṣya — Relinquishment, Reception, and the Gift-Seed

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> caitye tyāgānvayaṃ puṇyaṃ maitryādivad agṛhṇati /
> kukṣetre 'pīṣṭaphalatā phalabījāviparyayāt // 4.121 //

> At a shrine, merit connected with relinquishment arises as with loving-kindness,
> even without a recipient. Even in a deficient field, there is desirable fruit
> because fruit does not invert the seed.

The Bhāṣya explains the difficult `agṛhṇati` through merit arising without
a recipient who accepts or uses the offering.

## 3. Continuous Translation

It was said that a passion-bearing person gives at a shrine for the
person's own sake. When there is no one who uses the offering, how can
there be merit? For merit is twofold: merit connected with relinquishment,
which arises from relinquishment itself, and merit connected with use,
which arises from the recipient's use of the thing given.

At a shrine there is merit connected with relinquishment; there is no
merit connected with use. How can there be merit there when no one
receives? What reason is there that a recipient must be present? “Because
there is no benefit to anyone” is no reason. If merit arose only through
benefit to another, there would be no merit in cultivating loving-kindness
and the other immeasurables or right view. Therefore merit must be
acknowledged at a shrine as well:

“As with loving-kindness, even without a recipient.”

Just as in loving-kindness and related practices merit arises from one's
own mind, even without a recipient or benefit to another, so merit arises
from one's own mind when one acts from devotion to a virtuous Teacher of
the past.

“Then acts of giving and honoring would be useless.” No: devotion that
gives rise to those actions is more intense. Just as bodily and verbal
action arising from an intention to kill an enemy produces greater
demerit when performed under the conception that the person is an enemy,
even if that person is already dead—not through intention alone—so one
who performs acts of giving and honoring arising from devotion to a
Teacher of the past produces greater merit, not through devotion alone.

“If a seed of giving-action yields desirable fruit in a good field, then
in that field [a deficient field] it will yield undesirable fruit.”

“Even in a deficient field, there is desirable fruit because fruit does
not invert the seed.”

Even in a deficient field, fruit is seen not to invert the seed: from a
grape seed, a grape fruit arises, sweet; from a neem seed, a neem fruit
arises, bitter. Likewise, even in a deficient field, the seed of giving
that proceeds from a resolve for another's welfare produces desirable
fruit, not undesirable fruit. Because of the field's defect, however,
that seed may produce little fruit or no fruit. Thus the discussion of
the giving-based basis of meritorious action is concluded.

## 4. The Bhāṣya's Causal Distinctions

| Distinction | Result |
|---|---|
| Relinquishment-connected merit | Arises from giving up the thing, even without recipient-use |
| Use-connected merit | Arises from the recipient's use of what is given |
| Mental cultivation | Merit can arise from the practitioner's own mind without recipient or benefit to another |
| Enacted devotion | Giving and honoring actions arising from devotion produce more merit than devotion alone |
| Deficient field | Can reduce the desirable fruit or prevent it, but does not invert it into undesirable fruit |

The hostile-intention comparison explains the difference between intention
alone and bodily or verbal action arising from intention. It is an
illustration of the Bhāṣya's causal argument, not practical advice.

## 5. Interpretation

The passage rejects a single-source account of merit. Actual recipient
benefit is not necessary for every wholesome result: relinquishment can
itself be meritorious, and the Bhāṣya treats loving-kindness and right-view
cultivation as practices whose merit arises from the practitioner's own
mind. Yet this does not render outward action pointless. Bodily and verbal
acts of giving and honoring make devotion more intense than devotion
remaining at intention alone.

The seed-and-field comparison assigns distinct functions to orientation
and context. A giving-seed directed toward another's welfare retains
desirable fruit even in a deficient field; the field can diminish the
amount or prevent fruit from occurring, but cannot turn it into
undesirable fruit. This is a claim about the Bhāṣya's ethical causality,
not a universal empirical law about every gift or recipient.

The source's `svacitta` names the giver's own mental contribution; it
does not make the whole action identical with Mind. The passage keeps
relinquishment, devotion, bodily and verbal enactment, recipient-use,
and field-condition distinct.
