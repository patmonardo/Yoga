# VAK 5.42 Bhāṣya — Envy, Avarice, and a Counterexample

## Kārikā Anchor

> ekāntākuśalaṃ yasmāt svatantraṃ cobhayaṃ yataḥ |
> īrṣyā-mātsaryam eṣūktaṃ pṛthak saṃyojana-dvayam || 5.42 ||

> Because both are invariably unwholesome and independent,
> envy and avarice are stated separately among these as two
> fetters.

## Continuous Sanskrit

```iast
atha kasmād īṣryāmātsarye saṃyojane pṛthak

tasyaivaṃ syāt. yasya punar daśa tasya
krodhamrakṣāv apy ubhayaprakārau. tasmān na
bhavaty ayaṃ parihāra ity apare.
```

## Continuous Conventional Translation

Why are envy and avarice each stated separately as a fetter,
but no other enveloping affliction is? “Because both are
invariably unwholesome and independent, envy and avarice
are stated separately among these as two fetters.” For no
other enveloping affliction of this kind has both traits:
invariable unwholesomeness and independence.

That would work for someone who counts eight enveloping
afflictions. But for someone who counts ten, anger and
concealment also have both traits. Therefore, others say,
this is not a sufficient answer.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The question follows the nine fetters of 5.41. Its
comparison class is the *paryavasthānas*: why do envy and
avarice alone receive separate fetter headings? The verse
proposes two jointly distinguishing marks,
`ekāntākuśalatva` and `svatantratva`. The prose does not
further define the latter as autonomous causal operation;
“independent” remains the local classificatory term.

The answer is immediately tested against two enumerations.
On an eight-member list, the proposed marks single out envy
and avarice. On a ten-member list, anger (`krodha`) and
concealment (`mrakṣa`) also satisfy them. The objection
attributed to `apare`, “others,” therefore challenges the
**sufficiency of the distinction**, not the claim that envy
and avarice are unwholesome or independent. The text leaves
this challenge in place; no replacement criterion is given
within the 5.42 unit.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Ekānta` gives the force of
“invariably” in the first mark. `Svatantra` is kept as
“independent,” without importing an unstated theory of
independent arising. `Parihāra` is an answer meant to
resolve the stated question; the critics say this one fails
under the larger comparison class. The source's `īṣryā`
is retained in the continuous Sanskrit rather than silently
normalized to the official verse's `īrṣyā`.

**Organon reconstruction.** A classificatory rule must
carry its comparison domain. The two predicates may be
true of their intended examples while failing to pick out
only those examples when the domain includes further
members. This is a project-level formulation of the
Bhāṣya's actual counterexample, separate from a settled
source doctrine about which enumeration is final.
