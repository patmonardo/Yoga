# VAK 5.34 Bhāṣya — Conditions for an Affliction to Arise

## Kārikā Anchor

> aprahīṇād anuśayād viṣayāt pratyupasthitāt |
> ayoniśo manaskārāt kleśaḥ saṃpūrṇakāraṇaḥ || 5.34 ||

> An affliction arises with its causal conditions complete:
> from an unabandoned anuśaya, a present object, and
> unwise attention.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

When an affliction arises, it arises through three conditions:
an unabandoned anuśaya, an object that has appeared, and
unwise attention. For example, an attachment-anuśaya remains
unabandoned and not fully comprehended; dharmas liable to
occasion sensual attachment come into view; and unwise
attention attends to them. In that way sensual attachment
arises. These are, respectively, the powers of cause,
object, and application. Any other affliction is understood
to arise in the same manner when its causal conditions
are complete.

Sometimes, however, afflictions are said to arise by the
power of the object alone rather than by the power of
the cause. The example given is an arhat liable to fall
away. The transmitted particle in this exception is damaged,
so the force of the report remains provisional.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

Verses 5.32–5.33 gave an order of development among the ten
afflictions. This verse asks what makes a particular affliction
arise on an occasion. Its example separates three contributions:
the unabandoned `anuśaya` as `hetu-bala`; the appearing
`viṣaya` as `viṣaya-bala`; and `ayoniśo manaskāra` as
`prayoga-bala`. The source thus makes the way an object is
attended to a causal factor, alongside the disposition and
the object itself.

The exception prevents turning the triad into a universal
identity between anuśaya and manifest kleśa. The Bhāṣya
reports a case in which object power is sufficient without
the ordinary cause power. The status of the fallible arhat
and the force of the damaged particle require later review;
the important point here is that the text itself qualifies
the general rule. `Anuśaya` and an affliction in operation
are related in this causal account, but their exact ontological
relation should not be settled by a simple latent-versus-manifest
equation.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Pratyupasthita` describes an
object appearing before cognition, while `ābhāsagata`
describes the appearance of the particular dharmas in
the example. `Aparijñāta` adds incomplete comprehension
to the unabandoned condition. `Ayoniśo manaskāra`
is unwise attention, and the prose's `prayoga-bala`
identifies the force of this application. `Saṃpūrṇakāraṇa`
names a complete causal complex for the ordinary case,
with the following sentence registering a qualification.

**Organon reconstruction.** The passage gives a concrete
way to hold disposition, presented object, and attentive
activity apart while explaining their conjunction in an
event. For the Science of Citta this is more informative
than storing a single “afflicted” flag. The causal schema
is a project-level use of the Kośa and retains the text's
exception.
