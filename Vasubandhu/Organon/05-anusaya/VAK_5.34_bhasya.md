# VAK 5.34 Bhāṣya — Conditions for an Affliction to Arise

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> aprahīṇād anuśayād viṣayāt pratyupasthitāt |
> ayoniśo manaskārāt kleśaḥ saṃpūrṇakāraṇaḥ || 5.34 ||

> An affliction arises with its causal complex complete: from an
> unabandoned anuśaya, a Condition now present, and unwise attention.

## 3. Continuous Translation

An affliction, when arising, arises through three causes:
an unabandoned anuśaya, a Condition that has become present,
and unwise attention. For example, an attachment-anuśaya
remains unabandoned and not fully comprehended; Dharmas
conducive to envelopment by sensual attachment come into
appearance; and there is unwise attention to them. In this
way sensual attachment arises. These are, respectively, the
powers of cause, Condition, and application. Any other
affliction whose causes are complete should be understood
to arise in the same way.

Sometimes, however, afflictions arise by the power of the
Condition alone, not by the power of the cause, as in the
case of an arhat liable to fall away. The particle following
“sometimes” is damaged in the Sanskrit witness.

## 4. Argument and Doctrinal Determination

Verses 5.32–5.33 gave an order of development among the ten
afflictions. This verse asks what makes a particular affliction
arise on an occasion. Its example separates three contributions:
the unabandoned `anuśaya` as `hetu-bala`; the appearing
Condition (`viṣaya`) as `viṣaya-bala`; and `ayoniśo manaskāra` as
`prayoga-bala`. The source thus makes the mode of attention
a causal factor alongside the anuśaya and the Condition.

The post-verse qualification prevents treating the three
causes as an exceptionless requirement. It reports an
arising through Condition power without cause power in the
case of an arhat liable to fall away. The arhat example is
brief, and the damaged particle does not settle its reportive
force. The passage relates anuśaya to arising kleśa without
here defining their full ontological relation.

## 5. Translation and Interpretation

**Translation decisions.** `Pratyupasthita` describes a
Condition that has become present, while `ābhāsagata`
describes the appearance of the particular Dharmas in
the example. `Aparijñāta` adds incomplete comprehension
to the unabandoned condition. `Ayoniśo manaskāra`
is unwise attention, and the prose's `prayoga-bala`
identifies the power of this application. `Saṃpūrṇakāraṇa`
names the complete causal configuration in the ordinary
account. The Sanskrit uses `viṣaya` here, rendered
Condition; it does not use `ālambana`, Support.

**Organon reconstruction.** The passage gives a concrete
way to hold anuśaya, presented Condition, and attentive
activity apart while explaining their conjunction in an
event. Basis and Support may be added as distinct project
relations, but the verse does not itself identify either
with `viṣaya`. The causal schema retains the text's exception.
