# VAK 5.62 Bhāṣya — Four Grounds of Distance

## Kārikā Anchor

> vailakṣaṇyād vipakṣatvād deśaviccheda-kālataḥ |
> bhūta-śīla-pradeśādhvadvayānām iva dūratā || 5.62 ||

> Distance arises through unlike characteristics, opposition,
> separation in place, or time: as among the great elements,
> between discipline and misconduct, between distant places,
> and between the past and future.

The anchor follows the running verse's singular `dūratā`;
the separate kārikā has plural `dūratāḥ`. The Bhāṣya's
examples determine the compact second half. Neither
reading makes these four grounds one temporal sequence.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

This is called distancing. How many kinds of distance are
there? Distance is said to be fourfold: through unlike
characteristics, opposition, separation in place, and time.
Distance through unlike characteristics is exemplified by
the great elements: even when they arise together, their
distinct characteristics make them distant. Distance
through opposition is exemplified by misconduct in relation
to discipline. Distance through separation in place is
exemplified by widely separated regions, such as the eastern
and western oceans. Temporal distance is exemplified by
calling the past and future distant.

From what are the past and future distant? From the present.
But how is what occurred immediately before, or what is
just coming into being, distant? Perhaps by belonging to
different temporal courses, rather than by a long interval.
Then the present too would count as distant in this way.
Perhaps the past and future are distant through lack of
present activity. How, then, is the unconditioned established
as near? Because it is attained everywhere. The same would
follow for past and future; and how would this account work
for space (`ākāśa`)?

Perhaps instead past and future are distant from each other
because the present intervenes. The present would be near
both, and the unconditioned near because nothing intervenes.
But this would also make past and future near to one another
through their nearness to the present. A more fitting
account is that the future is distant from a dharma's own
characteristic because it has not yet attained it, while
the past is distant because it has fallen away from it.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The first three cases show why `dūrībhāva` cannot be read
as physical spacing alone. Co-arisen great elements are
unlike in *svalakṣaṇa* despite proximity. Discipline and
misconduct are opposed, a stronger relation than simple
difference. The two oceans supply literal spatial
separation. These examples explain the verse's compressed
`bhūta-śīla-pradeśa` series without converting one ground
into another.

The temporal case receives a dialectical test. Mere elapsed
duration fails because an immediately past or imminent
occurrence can still be called distant. Distinct temporal
courses threaten to make the present distant too. Lack of
activity raises the unconditioned case. Interposition of
the present between past and future produces the opposite
problem: nearness to the present may make both count as
near. The final proposal, introduced by `evaṃ tu yuktaṃ
syāt`, shifts to the dharma's own characteristic: the
future has not attained it; the past has passed from it.
This is the local preferred account, not a proof that all
four kinds of distance reduce to temporal status.

The dialogue also tests the word “near” against the
unconditioned and `ākāśa`. Its compressed replies should
remain provisional; their role in the argument is to block
an easy equation of nearness with present activity or lack
of spatial interval. The source does not here identify
`ākāśa` with the spatial separation of the two oceans.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Vailakṣaṇya` means difference
of characteristic; `vipakṣatva` means opposition, not mere
non-identity. `Adhvadvaya` points to past and future in the
Bhāṣya's temporal example. The source's damaged `dutaḥ`
in `dutastaddūram` is rendered through the immediately
following `vartamānāt` as the question “from what?”; this
is an interpretive repair, not a claim that the witness
prints a secure form.

**Organon reconstruction.** Distance is a relation indexed
to its ground. In the Anuśaya:Mārga study, a counteragent
may distance an afflictive acquisition through opposition
and path activity, without placing the affliction somewhere
else in space. The fourfold source classification disciplines
that project reading; it does not itself present a universal
theory of every kind of otherness.
