# VAK 5.33 Bhāṣya — From Self-View to Attachment and Hostility

## Kārikā Anchor

> tato 'ntagrahaṇaṃ tasmāc chīlāmarśas tato dṛśaḥ |
> rāgaḥ svadṛṣṭau mānaś ca dveṣo 'nyatrety anukramaḥ || 5.33 ||

> From self-view comes grasping an extreme; from that comes
> overvaluation of discipline and observance, then of view.
> Attachment and conceit arise toward one's own view, and
> hostility toward what opposes it. Such is the sequence.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

“From that comes grasping an extreme.” From self-view comes
extreme-view: one grasps the self as permanent or as subject
to annihilation. “From that comes overvaluation of discipline
and observance.” One expects purification through precisely
the extreme one has grasped.

“Then of view”: the word “overvaluation” carries forward.
From overvaluation of discipline and observance comes
overvaluation of view, because one treats as supreme what
one expects will purify. “Attachment and conceit arise
regarding one's own view”: attachment through clinging
to it, conceit through being elevated by it.

“Hostility elsewhere.” A person settled in their own view
becomes hostile to an opposed view. Others instead locate
the hostility with respect to one's own view when it has
been taken in another way and then abandoned [the syntax
is compressed]. Their reason is that attachment and related
afflictions abandoned through seeing take a view within
one's own continuum as object. “Such is the sequence”:
this is the order of operation of the ten afflictions.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The verse completes the sequence opened in 5.32. Its transitions
are determinate. Self-view supplies the supposed subject
whose fate is then grasped under the alternatives of
permanence and annihilation. The chosen extreme is made
into a claimed means of purification. That means or view
is then elevated as supreme. At this point a view has become
an object of `rāga` and a ground for `māna`; opposition
to it becomes an object of `dveṣa`.

The main explanation of `anyatra` points to a rival or
opposed view. The `apare` explanation brings the object
back into one's own continuum, on the technical ground
that seeing-abandonable attachment and related afflictions
take such a view as their object. The two construals should
not be collapsed into a single claim about hostility toward
other people. The source locates the relation at the level
of views and their object range.

The final `pravṛttikrama` calls this an order of operation
of the ten kleśas. It is a reported doctrinal sequence,
not a claim that every person always passes through these
stages in a fixed psychological chronology.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** The Bhāṣya expands `antagrahaṇa`
to `antagrāhadṛṣṭi` and `śīlāmarśa` to
`śīlavrataparāmarśa`. It explicitly supplies the carried
word `āmarśa` after `tato dṛśaḥ`, yielding
`dṛṣṭiparāmarśa`. `Abhiṣvaṅga` grounds attachment,
while `unnatigamana` grounds conceit. The scope of
`anyatra` is contested by the unnamed `apare` and
remains visibly plural in the translation.

**Organon reconstruction.** The sequence shows how an
unexamined self-determination can generate a view about
permanence or annihilation, then a claim of purification,
then attachment to the claim and opposition to other views.
This gives a precise point of contact for the project's
concern about clinging to view and clinging to death. That
contact is an Organon reading of the Kośa's sequence, not
an attribution of our framework to Vasubandhu.
