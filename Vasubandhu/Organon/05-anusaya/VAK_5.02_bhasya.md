# VAK 5.02 Bhāṣya — Seven Anuśayas and Attachment to Existence

## Kārikā Anchor

> ṣaḍ rāgabhedāt saptoktāḥ bhavarāgo dvidhātujaḥ |
> antarmukhatvāt tanmokṣasaṃjñāvyāvṛttaye kṛtaḥ || 5.02 ||

> Because attachment is divided, the six are stated as seven. Attachment
> to existence arises in two domains. It is so designated because it
> turns inward and to exclude the conception that this is liberation.

The first words complete the suspended `te punaḥ` of 5.01. The full
commentary unit includes a substantial debate before the prose reaches
`bhavarāga`.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

“Because attachment is divided, the six are stated as seven.” The six
anuśayas are spoken of as seven in the sūtra by dividing attachment in
two: the anuśayas of sensual attachment, resistance, attachment to
existence, conceit, ignorance, view, and doubt.

How should “anuśaya of sensual attachment” be understood? Is sensual
attachment itself the anuśaya, or does sensual attachment have an
anuśaya? What follows from the choice? If sensual attachment itself
is the anuśaya, a sūtra appears to contradict it. The sūtra says that
someone does not dwell much with a mind possessed by sensual
attachment; they know as it really is an escape beyond the arisen
possession by sensual attachment; and that possession, with its
anuśaya, is abandoned. If instead attachment has an anuśaya, an
anuśaya separate from associated mind would follow, contradicting
the Abhidharma statement that it is associated with three faculties.

The Vaibhāṣikas say that sensual attachment itself is the anuśaya,
and similarly down to doubt. Asked about the sūtra, they answer that
“with anuśaya” means “with an accompanying connection”; alternatively,
the sūtra uses “anuśaya” figuratively for acquisition or possession,
as in calling fire painful. In Abhidharma's proper technical usage,
the kleśa itself is the anuśaya. Thus anuśayas are associated with
mind. They argue that anuśayas afflict citta, obstruct it, and oppose
the wholesome: they make citta afflicted, prevent a new wholesome
state from arising, and cause decline from an arisen one. Hence, they
say, anuśayas cannot be separate from associated mind. A separate
anuśaya always present would make wholesome states impossible, yet
wholesome states are observed.

The reply finds that proof inconclusive. Someone who accepts a
separate anuśaya need not ascribe those active effects to the anuśaya;
they may ascribe them to kleśa. The Sautrāntika account is preferable:
the expression means an anuśaya *of* sensual attachment. Yet that
anuśaya is neither associated nor dissociated as a distinct entity,
because it is no separate substance. A dormant kleśa is called
`anuśaya`; when awakened it is called `paryavasthāna`. Dormancy is the
persistence of a seed condition when the kleśa is not present; waking
is its becoming present. This “seed condition” is a capacity of the
existing continuum, generated by kleśa, to produce kleśa again. It is
compared to the capacity produced by experience to yield memory and
the capacity in sprouts to yield a rice crop. If someone posits a
separate, dissociated entity as the seed of kleśa, they must likewise
posit a separate entity as the seed of memory.

But does a sūtra not call kleśa itself an anuśaya when it says that
someone “has an anuśaya of attachment toward pleasant feeling”?
The wording “comes to have” does not require the active kleśa to be
anuśaya at that very moment. It is called that when dormant; or the
name of the cause is applied figuratively to its effect. Enough of
the digression: let the treatise proceed.

The sūtra thus divides attachment into sensual attachment and
attachment to existence. What is the latter? “Attachment to existence
arises in two domains”: the form and formless domains. Why is it
designated so? “Because it turns inward, and to exclude the conception
of that as liberation.” There attachment is predominantly attachment
to samāpatti, and it proceeds inwardly. According to some, the name
prevents these two domains from being conceived as liberation. But
`bhava` here means one's mode of existence. Beings free of sensual
attachment savor samāpatti together with its support, and so savor
their own mode of existence. That attachment is therefore called
`bhavarāga`.

## Argument and Voices

The long opening dispute is indispensable to 5.02. The Vaibhāṣikas
identify anuśaya with the associated kleśa and interpret the sūtra's
`sānuśaya` figuratively. Vasubandhu rejects their proposed proof and
prefers the Sautrāntika account: dormancy and manifestation are
conditions of an afflictive continuum, not two distinct substances.
The seed analogy explains a capacity to produce another kleśa; it
does not make the seed a hidden thing inside citta.

The second movement defines `bhavarāga` through its two domains and
its predominance as `samāpattirāga`. The phrase `ekeṣām iti` marks the
liberation-conception explanation as a reported view. The ensuing
`ātmabhāva eva tu bhavaḥ` offers a more exact explanation of what is
enjoyed. The source identifies **attachment to samāpatti**, rather
than samāpatti itself, as the continuing rāga.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Citta` remains visible at the first
occurrence because it is the stated locus of affliction in the
Vaibhāṣika argument; “mind” would obscure the technical question.
`Saṃprayukta` concerns association with faculties and citta;
`viprayukta` concerns a purportedly dissociated item. Neither term
is equivalent to the simple contrast between conscious and
unconscious. `Ātmabhāva` here denotes the being's constituted mode of
existence, not a metaphysical self. The precise wording of the sūtra
quotation and several rough e-text forms need witness comparison.

**Organon reconstruction.** The textual debate sets a constraint on
our Science of Citta: citta, the associated caitta factors, the
continuum's acquired capacity, and a factor's present operation must
remain discriminable. `Cetanā` is not named in this unit, so its role
as karma from Chapter 4 should not be imported into the literal
translation. The `bhavarāga` discussion adds a path criterion:
inward refinement can still be appropriated as one's mode of
existence. That criterion concerns the relation of rāga to samāpatti,
not the worth of samāpatti as such.
