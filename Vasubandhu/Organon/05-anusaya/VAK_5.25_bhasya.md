# VAK 5.25 Bhāṣya — The Vaibhāṣika Case for Existence in Three Times

## Kārikā Anchor

> sarvakālāstitā uktatvād dvayāt sadviṣayāt phalāt |
> tadastivādāt sarvāstivādā iṣṭāś caturvidhāḥ || 5.25 ||

> Existence in all times is maintained because it has been stated,
> because of the cognitive pair, because cognition has an existent
> object, and because of fruit. Those asserting that all exists
> are recognized as Sarvāstivādins, in four forms.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

Does this past and future that we speak of exist or not? If it
exists, conditioned formations seem to become permanent through
existence in all times. If it does not, how could one be bound
or released with respect to it or through it? The Vaibhāṣikas
do not assert the permanence of conditioned formations, since
these have the marks of the conditioned. They do, however,
plainly assert “existence in all times.”

Why? First, “because it has been stated.” The cited teaching
says that, if past form did not exist, a learned noble disciple
could not be without longing toward past form; because it exists,
that disciple can be without longing. Likewise, if future form
did not exist, the disciple could not refrain from delighting
in it; because future form exists, this is possible.

Second, “because of the pair.” Consciousness is said to arise
depending on two: eye and forms, and so on through mind and
dharmas. Without past and future objects, a consciousness
taking them as support could not arise depending on that pair.
These are the scriptural grounds. There are also rational ones.

Third, “because of an existent object.” Consciousness operates
with an existent `viṣaya`, not a nonexistent one. If past and
future did not exist, a cognition of them would have a
nonexistent support; without a support, that cognition would
not arise. Fourth, “because of fruit.” If the past did not
exist, how could wholesome or unwholesome karma bear fruit
later? When the fruit arises, its maturation cause is not
present. Therefore, say the Vaibhāṣikas, past and future do exist.

The text then says that one asserting `sarvam asti` must accept
this. Those who affirm the existence of past, future, and
present are Sarvāstivādins [the transmitted enumeration is
damaged]. Those who distinguish an existing present and past
karma that has not yet borne fruit from past karma that has
borne fruit and from the future, which they deny, are called
Vibhajyavādins. How many forms of Sarvāstivādin are there?
“Four.” Their enumeration follows in 5.26.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The question is generated by 5.23–5.24: bondage was said to
involve past and future afflictions and objects. The opening
dilemma presses both sides. Sheer nonexistence seems to remove
the terms of binding and release; existence through all times
seems to threaten the impermanence of conditioned dharmas.
The Vaibhāṣika answer asserts all-time existence while denying
that conditioned dharmas are thereby eternal.

The four grounds have different functions. `Uktatvāt` appeals
to a cited teaching about the disciple's relation to past and
future form. `Dvayāt` appeals to the scriptural formula that
consciousness arises from a faculty-object pair. The Bhāṣya
then names these two grounds `āgama`. `Sadviṣayāt` is a
claim about cognition requiring an existent object; `phalāt`
is a causal claim about past karma and later maturation.
Those latter two are given as `yukti`. This is the
Vaibhāṣika argument as reported here, not an uncontested
proof that every opponent must accept its account of objects
or karmic causation.

The final contrast specifies the doctrinal boundary. The
Sarvāstivādin asserts all three times. The Vibhajyavādin
distinguishes present existence and a qualified past from
past karma whose fruit has already been given and from the
future. The text announces four Sarvāstivādin accounts but
does not yet describe them.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Sarvakālāstitā` is the thesis of
existence in all times, not an assertion here that conditioned
things are immutable; the prose explicitly rejects
`śāśvatatva`. `Dvaya` is the faculty-object pair. `Viṣaya`
and `ālambana` occur in a connected argument, but their
lexical difference is preserved. `Adattaphala` and
`dattaphala` distinguish past karma that has not yet yielded
fruit from past karma that has. The damaged enumeration is
translated according to the immediate contrast and remains
marked as such.

**Organon reconstruction.** The chapter now asks what temporal
status a term must have to enter a real relation of cognition,
bondage, or fruition. The issue for the Science of Citta is
the validity of those relations across time, not simply a
dictionary meaning of “exists.” This is a project-level
question drawn from the Vaibhāṣika argument; it does not
settle the metaphysics on Vasubandhu's behalf.
