# VAK 5.20 Bhāṣya — Roots of the Unwholesome and Indeterminate

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> kāme 'kuśala-mūlāni rāga-pratigha-mūḍhayaḥ |
> trīṇy avyākṛta-mūlāni tṛṣṇāvidyā matiś ca sā || 5.20 ||

> In the Desire Realm, attachment, hostility, and delusion are roots of the
> unwholesome; the three roots of the ethically indeterminate are craving,
> ignorance, and discriminative comprehension in its indeterminate form.

The third pāda here is the official study's contextual reading;
the running IAST text differs, as noted below.

## 2. Continuous Sanskrit

## 3. Continuous Translation

How many are roots of the unwholesome, and how many are not?
“In the Desire Realm, attachment, hostility, and delusion are
roots of the unwholesome.” In the Desire Realm, all attachment,
all hostility, and all delusion are included, except the delusion
associated with self-view and extreme-view, respectively.

“Three are roots of the unwholesome.” Greed is a root of the
unwholesome; hatred and delusion are roots of the unwholesome.
Only what is itself unwholesome and is a root of the unwholesome
is accepted as an unwholesome root. Thus the remaining anuśayas
are established as not being roots of the unwholesome.

How many are roots of the indeterminate, and how many are not?
There are three roots of the indeterminate. Which three?
“Craving, ignorance, and that cognition.” The word “that”
indicates indeterminate status. Any craving, ignorance, or
discriminative cognition that is indeterminate, even if born
of maturation, is an indeterminate root, according to the
Kāśmīra teachers.

## 4. Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The question has changed from the **ethical status** of an
anuśaya in 5.19 to its status as a **root**. For an
`akuśalamūla`, the Bhāṣya requires both conditions: the factor
is itself `akuśala`, and it roots something `akuśala`. The
ignorance associated with the two indeterminate views fails
the first condition. Other anuśayas may be unwholesome without
being among the three unwholesome roots.

The next question asks after roots of the indeterminate. The
explanation makes `sā` restrict the triad to its indeterminate
instances: not craving, ignorance, and `mati` under every
ethical condition. It also glosses the verse's `mati` as
`prajñā`. The inclusion of even `vipākaja` instances is the
Kāśmīra position explicitly marked by `iti kāśmīrāḥ`.
Thus neither `prajñā` nor `mati` is intrinsically a liberating
or wholesome act merely because it is a form of cognition.

The running text's `trīṇy akuśalamūlāni` is coherent with the
preceding definition of the three unwholesome roots, but it
leaves the subsequent question about three indeterminate roots
to supply that category in prose. The official Kārikā study
adopts `avyākṛtamūlāni` for its third pāda. Both textual facts
must remain visible; the doctrinal argument does not by itself
establish the history of the verse's wording.

## 5. Translation and Interpretation

**Translation decisions.** `Mūla` is a root relation, not a
synonym for having the same ethical status. The prose names
`lobha`, `dveṣa`, and `moha` for the verse's attachment,
hostility, and delusion. `Mati` becomes `prajñā` in the prose;
this is an explicit local gloss, while Prajñā must remain
distinct from Jñāna in the wider study. The `yathākramam`
clause is compressed; the practical exclusion at issue is the
ignorance associated with the two views classified as
indeterminate in 5.19.

**Organon reconstruction.** For a Science of Citta, the passage
separates a factor's own status from its role in generating
other factors. That distinction can guide later formalization
of citta and caitta relations. It is a project-level use of
the Bhāṣya, separate from the Kāśmīra attribution and from
any claim about implemented software.
