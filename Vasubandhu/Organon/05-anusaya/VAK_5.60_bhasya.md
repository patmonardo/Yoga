# VAK 5.60 Bhāṣya — Four Grounds of Affliction's Exhaustion

## Kārikā Anchor

> ālambanaparijñānāt tadālambanasaṃkṣayāt |
> ālambanaprahāṇāc ca pratipakṣodayāt kṣayaḥ || 5.60 ||

> Affliction is exhausted through complete knowledge of its
> object, through the exhaustion of what takes it as object,
> through abandonment of its object, or through the arising
> of its counteragent.

The third member follows the separate kārikā's
`ālambanaprahāṇāc`. The running IAST verse at [319|25]
reads `ālambanagrahāṇācca`; its subsequent prose has
`ālambanaprahāṇāt`. The divergence is retained below.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

This is to be examined. Consider universal afflictions of
a different domain and contaminated-object afflictions
abandoned through seeing cessation or the path. When their
object is completely known, they are not then abandoned;
when they are abandoned, their object is not then being
completely known. How, therefore, are they abandoned?
Afflictions do not necessarily cease through complete
knowledge of their own object. They cease in four ways.

For afflictions abandoned through seeing, first: through
complete knowledge of the object, through exhaustion of
what takes that as object, and, as the running verse reads,
through “taking the object.” The subsequent prose explains
the third as **abandonment** of the object. The first way
applies to afflictions abandoned through seeing suffering
and origin that take objects on their own level, and to
those taking uncontaminated objects. The second applies to
universal afflictions of a different domain: universal
afflictions of the same domain take them as objects; when
the latter are abandoned, the former are abandoned too.
The third applies to contaminated-object afflictions
abandoned through seeing cessation or the path. Afflictions
that take uncontaminated objects are themselves the objects
of these; when those are abandoned, these too are abandoned.

For afflictions abandoned through cultivation, exhaustion
occurs when the counteragent arises. When the path opposing
a particular type of affliction arises, that type is
abandoned. Which counteragent opposes which type? The
graded relation from the strongest to the weakest will be
explained later.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The opening problem rejects a universal rule that the
object of each affliction must be directly known at the
moment that affliction is abandoned. `Ālambana` names the
object taken by an affliction; it is not interchangeable
with a cognitive faculty or with the affliction itself.
Knowing an object and abandoning an affliction are thus
different relations, with different timing in the cases
that motivate the question.

The first three grounds are assigned to **darśana-heya**
cases. In the direct case, complete knowledge of the
relevant object is the ground. The next two cases are
mediated: the target affliction is removed when a different
affliction that takes it as object, or an affliction serving
as its own object, is abandoned. The local prose names the
same-domain and different-domain universal factors in the
second case, and contaminated-object and
uncontaminated-object factors in the third. Those directions
must be kept; “the object is abandoned” does not license a
general claim that material things disappear when known.

The fourth ground changes the class to **bhāvanā-heya**.
Its counteragent is a *mārga* specific to a type of kleśa.
The verse's four ablatives therefore mark different grounds
for different cases, not four consecutive stages every
affliction undergoes. The following question about the
graded matching of counteragents is deferred by the prose;
the *kinds* of counteragent become the topic of 5.61.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** The anchor uses
`ālambanaprahāṇāc` because the prose explicitly says
`ālambanaprahāṇāt`; the continuous Sanskrit preserves the
running verse's `ālambanagrahāṇācca`. The conventional
translation states that conflict rather than disguising it.
`Tadālambana` is relational: in the second case the
same-domain universals take the different-domain universals
as object. The third case likewise turns on which affliction
is object for which. “Or” in the anchor makes the
Bhāṣya's case allocation readable; the verse itself uses
coordinated ablatives.

**Organon reconstruction.** In our Anuśaya:Mārga reading,
the Path does not apply one abstract deletion rule to the
kleśa world. It determines which relation sustains a given
affliction and which path operation can end it. This is a
project-level inference from the Kośa's fourfold account;
it neither replaces the source's specific cases nor makes
every instance of seeing an afflictive appropriation.
