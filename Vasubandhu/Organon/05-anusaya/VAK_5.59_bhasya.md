# VAK 5.59 Bhāṣya — Five Hindrances and Their Grounds

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> kāme nivaraṇāny ekavipakṣāhārakṛtyataḥ |
> dvayakaitāḥ pañcatā skandhavighātavicikitsanāt || 5.59 ||

> The five hindrances belong to the Desire Realm. Torpor with sleepiness and
> restlessness with remorse each count as one because each pair shares a
> counteragent, nourishment, and function. They are five because they obstruct
> the Path-Bases of discipline, concentration, and
> discriminative comprehension, and leave doubt concerning the Truths.

## 3. Continuous Translation

The sūtra teaches five hindrances: sensual desire, ill will,
torpor and sleepiness, restlessness and remorse, and doubt.
Does it include torpor, restlessness, and doubt occurring in
all three domains, or only those connected with the desire
domain? The sūtra calls the five hindrances a complete mass
of the unwholesome. Since they are declared exclusively
unwholesome, the hindrances belong to the Desire Realm,
not another domain.

Why are torpor and sleepiness, though two, called one
hindrance, and likewise restlessness and remorse? Each pair
counts as one because its members have one counteragent,
one nourishment, and one function. “Unity of two” explains
`dvyekatā`; in this context `vipakṣa`, `pratipakṣa`, and
`anāhāra` express the same opposing relation.

For torpor and sleepiness, the sūtra gives one nourishment
and one non-nourishment. Their nourishment is five factors:
languor, discontent, yawning, imbalance regarding food, and
sinking of Consciousness. Their non-nourishment is the perception of
light. They also have one function: both drive Consciousness
toward collapse. Restlessness and remorse likewise have one
nourishment and one non-nourishment. Their nourishment is
four factors: thought about relatives, thought about a
locality, the transmitted `amara-vitarka`, and recalling
former laughter, play, and enjoyment. Their non-nourishment
is calming (`śamatha`). Both keep Consciousness unpacified.
Thus each pair counts as one through its common counteragent,
nourishment, and function.

If all afflictions can hinder, why name only five? Because
they obstruct the Path-Bases and occasion doubt.
Sensual desire and ill will obstruct the Base of ethical
discipline; torpor and sleepiness obstruct discernment;
restlessness and remorse obstruct concentration. Without
concentration and discernment, one doubts the truths. Yet
this explanation would require restlessness and remorse,
which oppose concentration, to be listed before torpor and
sleepiness. Others therefore say that, in list order, the
two pairs impair concentration and discernment respectively:
the one engaged in concentration fears torpor and sleepiness;
the one engaged in examining dharmas fears restlessness and
remorse.

Still others explain it differently. How? When someone
has been moving about and grasping signs in agreeable and
disagreeable Conditions, sensual desire and ill will arise
from that prior activity when the person returns to the
dwelling; they obstruct entry into concentration at the outset.
For one who has entered concentration, nonapplication in
cultivating calm and insight is followed by torpor with
sleepiness, restlessness with remorse, and doubt as obstacles
to calm and insight “in order.” The mapping in this compressed
sentence is not fully explicit. Even after one emerges, doubt
obstructs contemplation of Dharma. Thus five hindrances are taught.

## 4. Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The first question fixes **domain**, not the possible range
of each factor under every classification. The reason given
is the cited sūtra's designation of the five hindrances as
exclusively unwholesome. “Hindrance” here is the technical
fivefold set in the Desire Realm; it does not deny that a
factor bearing the same name may be discussed elsewhere.

The paired factors retain their distinct names and
identities. Their **count as one hindrance** arises from
shared nourishment (`āhāra`), opposing or non-nourishing
condition (`vipakṣa` / `pratipakṣa` / `anāhāra`), and effect
(`kṛtya`). The text supplies the concrete conditions and
effects for each pair. Torpor and sleepiness tend toward
`citta-laya`; restlessness and remorse keep Consciousness
`avyupaśānta`. These are different failures of Consciousness's
readiness, not one generic “distraction.”

The fivefold explanation concerns **śīla-, samādhi-, and
prajñā-skandhas**, not the five appropriated Bases.
The first proposed assignment places
torpor/sleepiness against discernment and
restlessness/remorse against concentration. The prose then
objects on grounds of **list order** and reports `apare`,
who reverse those two assignments. `Anye` introduce a
third, practice-sequence account. The text presents these
as competing explanations; it does not explicitly erase
their disagreement or settle every mapping among
`śamatha`, `vipaśyanā`, and doubt in the last account.

## 5. Translation and Interpretation

**Translation decisions.** The running verse's `dvyekatā`
is translated through its explicit prose gloss, “unity of
two.” `Skandhavidhāta` is rendered “obstruction of the
Path-Bases” in light of the three named skandhas; the
separate kārikā's `vighāta` would make the obstructive sense
more transparent. The reading and exact sense of
`'maravitarkaḥ` remain open, so no conjectural English
equivalent is inserted into the translation. The final
practice-sequence account lists three later hindrances beside
two named practices and then singles out doubt after emergence;
its `yathākramam` does not by itself secure a one-to-one mapping.

**Organon reconstruction.** A factor's identity and the
count of operative hindrances answer different questions.
The Bhāṣya generates a relational criterion for grouping:
common maintenance, common countercondition, common effect.
The three explanations of fivefoldness then test that group
against ethical, contemplative, and investigative
functions. This is a project-level account of systematic
classification; the source itself supplies the concrete
pairings and preserves rival analyses.
