# VAK 5.27 Bhāṣya — Testing Existence Across the Three Times

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> kiṃ vighnaṃ tat kathaṃ nānyad adhva-yogas tathā sataḥ |
> ajāta-naṣṭatā kena gambhīrā khalu dharmatā || 5.27 ||

> What obstructs that activity? How is it not distinct from the dharma? How
> can something that exists in the same way belong to a particular time, and
> by what is it unborn or destroyed? “The nature of dharmas is indeed
> profound,” replies the Vaibhāṣika.

## 3. Continuous Translation

The earlier account has been traversed. A further question
remains: if past and future exist substantially, why are they
called past and future? The defender repeats that temporal
courses are distinguished by activity. Then what is the
activity of a presently occurring eye belonging to the same
class? “Giving and receiving a fruit.” But past homogeneous
causes also give fruit. They would therefore have activity,
or at least half an activity, and the temporal marks would
become confused.

What prevents a dharma existing in the same manner from always
performing its activity? If the answer is that the relevant
conditions have not assembled, the critic replies that the
dharma's perpetual existence has been admitted. What of
activity itself, which is called past, future, and present?
Does activity have a further activity? If it exists yet is
in none of the three times, it would be unconditioned and
permanent. One could no longer say simply that the dharma is
future when it does not perform activity. The defender replies
that this fault would arise only if activity differed from the
dharma; it does not. The critic answers that the original
problem returns: why is this same existing dharma sometimes
called past and sometimes future?

The defender defines future as not yet arisen, present as
arisen and not destroyed, and past as destroyed. But if past
and future exist substantially just as the present does, by
what is the same dharma unborn or destroyed? What was absent
before it arose, and what is absent after it ceased? Unless
something can come to be after not being and cease after
being, the three times cannot be established. The earlier
claim that conditioned marks prevent permanence is only
words if arising and destruction do not apply. To say both
that a dharma always exists and that it is not permanent is,
the critic says, an unprecedented way of speaking. A further
quoted verse says: “Own-nature always exists, yet the entity
is not accepted as permanent; nor is the entity other than
own-nature: plainly, a Lord's contrivance.” The quoted line's
final expression is preserved as a polemical formulation,
without assigning it an independent doctrinal voice.

The critic turns to the first ground of 5.25, “because it has
been stated.” We too say that past and future “exist”: past
means what formerly occurred; future means what will occur
when a cause is present. This is a qualified use of “exists,”
not substantial existence like the present. If asked in what
other way they exist, the answer is: in the manner of what
was past or is future. It is the opponent who must explain
how something eternally existent is past or future. The
Blessed One's saying “the past exists, the future exists”
makes known a former cause as former and a future result as
future, and counters the view denying cause and fruit. Here
`asti` can function as a particle: one may say “there is a
lamp's prior absence” and “there is its later absence,” or
“that lamp has ceased, though I did not extinguish it.”
Without such usage, even the statuses past and future would
not be intelligible.

Another cited saying, addressed to the Laguḍaśikhīyaka
mendicants, says that a deed already past, exhausted, ceased,
gone, and transformed “exists.” Did they deny that the deed
had formerly occurred? In that case “exists” refers to the
capacity for giving fruit implanted by that deed in its
continuum. A past deed existing in its own nature would not
be past in this sense.

The teaching on ultimate emptiness likewise says that an eye
arising comes from nowhere and, ceasing, goes into no store:
having not been, it comes to be; having been, it passes away.
If the future eye already existed substantially, the latter
account of arising and ceasing would fail. The reply “it comes
to be in the present time” does not help, since time is not a
separate entity from its contents. If the reply is that the
eye comes to be in its own nature, its future nonexistence
has been conceded.

The second and third grounds, concerning the pair and an
existent Condition, now come under scrutiny. When Mind and
dharmas condition Mind-Cognition, are those dharmas a
producing condition like Mind, or merely its Support? If
they produce Cognition, how could something that may or may
not occur a thousand aeons hence produce Cognition now?
Nor can nirvāṇa, as cessation of all activity, be such a
producer. If dharmas are only Supports, the critic accepts
that past and future can serve as Supports. Asked how a
nonexistent thing can do so, the critic asks in return: if
that Support exists just as it is now taken, how can it be
remembered as having been or anticipated as yet to be? One
remembering past Form or Feeling does not see “it exists”
but “it was.” A past item is remembered as it was experienced;
a future one is grasped by understanding as what will become
present. If it existed in that same way now, it would be
present. If it does not, something not presently existent
can nonetheless be a Support.

Suppose the opponent says that the very same past Form still
exists, merely dispersed. What is dispersed is not what is
remembered. If the same Form has only been divided into atoms,
the atoms would be permanent, with mere rearrangement in place
of arising and cessation. This would approach the Ājīvika
account and discard the teaching that the eye arises without
coming from elsewhere. How could Feelings and other things
not compounded of atoms be dispersed? They too are remembered
as they were experienced. If they still exist in just that
way, they are permanent; if not, a nonexistent item can be
a Support.

An objection says that, if a nonexistent item can be a
Support, a thirteenth Essence should also be possible. But
what is the Support of Cognition that a thirteenth Essence
does not exist? “That very name.” Then the name itself would
seem not to exist. What is the Support when one attends to a
sound's prior absence? “The sound itself.” Then someone
seeking the sound's absence would have to make the sound.
If the answer is “the sound is in a future state,” how can
there be a Cognition that it does not exist while it exists?
If the answer is “it does not exist now,” the critic replies
that the sound is one; the temporal qualification establishes
the absence in question. This last inference is compressed
in the working witness. Thus both being and absence can
serve as Supports of Cognition.

The Bodhisattva's saying, “I could know or see what does not
exist in the world: that is impossible,” is then raised. Its
intention, the critic replies, is that conceited others see
even an unreal appearance as real, whereas he sees as existent
only what is existent. If all Cognitions had existent
Supports, what distinction would this saying make? Another
teaching says that a disciple instructed in the morning will
seek distinction by evening, and one instructed in the
evening will do so by morning; he knows the existent as
existent, the nonexistent as nonexistent, what has a higher
as having a higher, and the unsurpassed as unsurpassed. The
received wording of this quotation is difficult, but its
contrast of existent and nonexistent is legible. Therefore
the claim that Cognition always has an existent Support is
not a sufficient ground for all-time existence.

The fruit argument is also answered. The Sautrāntikas do not
say that fruit arises directly from a past deed that has
ceased. Rather, it arises from a special determination of
the continuum produced by that deed; the text promises a
fuller explanation in the discussion refuting self-theory.
If past and future already exist substantially, however,
the result always exists. What causal power does the deed
have? If it produces the result, production after prior
nonexistence has been admitted. If everything already exists,
causal power loses its target. That would disclose
Vārṣagaṇya's position: “What exists, exists indeed; what does
not exist, does not exist; the nonexistent has no arising;
the existent has no destruction.”

Perhaps the deed's power is to make the result present. What
is “making present”? If it draws the result from another
place, permanence follows, and such transport cannot explain
nonmaterial dharmas; moreover, the drawing itself comes to
be after not being. If it differentiates the result's own
nature, this too concedes coming to be after not being. Thus
the assertion that past and future exist substantially is,
in this critique, unsuitable for the teaching. “All exists”
may instead be said as the sūtra says it: “All exists, brahmin,
to the extent of the twelve Essences.” The passage also
allows speech about the three times, provided the manner of
their existence is stated as above.

The opponent returns to 5.23–5.24: if past and future do not
exist substantially, how is someone bound through an
affliction or with respect to its `vastu`? A person can remain
bound through an anuśaya born from a past affliction or one
that will cause a future affliction. With respect to the
`vastu`, the bond is an anuśaya of an affliction that has had
or will have that thing as its Support. The Vaibhāṣikas still
insist that past and future exist. An intervening instruction
about leading the argument and understanding it for oneself
is textually compressed; its exact predication remains
unresolved. “The nature of dharmas is indeed profound” is
their stated rejoinder. The next
sentence is disputed: the working Sanskrit permits “Then it
is not necessarily impossible to establish,” whereas a
parallel Sanskrit reading and Xuanzang's translation yield
“It need not be demonstrable by reasoning” as a continuation
of the Vaibhāṣika reply.

There is a way of saying that what arises is what ceases:
Form arises and Form ceases. There is also a way of saying
that something else arises and something else ceases: the
future arises and the present ceases. Even a temporal course
can be said to arise, because what arises is included in one;
a course can also arise in the sense that future time comprises
many moments. The digression then closes.

## 4. Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The Kārikā's questions are not free-standing puzzles.
They test the strongest of the four temporal theories from
5.26. If `kāritra` is other than the dharma, its own temporal
status must be explained. If it is identical, the identity
of the dharma across times makes the difference between
unborn, operative, and destroyed hard to ground. The critic's
central demand is for a real account of arising and ceasing,
not merely three names assigned to one continuously existing
substance.

The four grounds of 5.25 are answered in sequence. Against the
scriptural ground, the critic distinguishes the qualified
`asti` of what formerly occurred or will occur from
substantial existence, using the lamp's prior and later
absence and the cited deed whose potency remains in a
continuum. The appeal to `paramārthaśūnyatā` is tied here
to the eye's actual arising and cessation; it does not
settle every meaning of ultimate reality.

Against the cognitive grounds, the exchange distinguishes a
producing condition (`janaka pratyaya`) from a Support
(`ālambana`) taken by Cognition. The memory, atom, thirteenth
Essence, sound-absence, and quoted-teaching cases test whether
every Support must presently exist. The critic's conclusion
is that both being and absence can serve as Supports. Against
the fruit ground, the Sautrāntika side invokes a specially
determined continuum rather than a substantially persistent
past deed. The Vārṣagaṇya quotation exposes the contrary
implication of denying new arising and cessation. These are
positions in a dispute; the Vaibhāṣika objections remain
visible.

In the final bond exchange, the reply distinguishes an
anuśaya born from a past affliction from one that can produce
a future affliction. Its relation to the `vastu` runs through
the affliction's Support. This explains bondage without
granting substantial present existence to either temporal
object.

`Gambhīrā khalu dharmatā` belongs to the Vaibhāṣika rejoinder

## 5. Translation and Interpretation

**Translation decisions.** `Dravyataḥ` and `svabhāva` name
the substantial-existence claim under dispute. `Kāritra`
is operative activity, whose relation to the dharma is
explicitly tested. `Ālambana` is Support taken by Cognition,
distinguished here from a producing condition (`janaka pratyaya`).
`Saṃtānaviśeṣa` is a special determination of the continuum,
offered as an account of delayed fruition. The long source
contains several quotations and compressed exchanges. The
translation follows their order, retaining uncertainty in
the morning-and-evening quotation, the sound-absence exchange,
and the instruction before `gambhīrā khalu dharmatā`.
The reading and speaker of the sentence after that appeal
remain uncertain in the working Sanskrit witness.

**Organon reconstruction.** The key theoretical issue is
whether continuity requires a thing to remain substantially
identical across all times. The counterproposal traces a
genetic continuity through actual arising, cessation, and
transformation of the continuum. This is a project-level
comparison prompted by the Bhāṣya, separate from either
school's doctrinal voice and from implemented architecture.
