# VAK 5.27 Bhāṣya — Testing Existence Across the Three Times

## Kārikā Anchor

> kiṃ vighnaṃ tat kathaṃ nānyad adhvāyogas tathā sataḥ |
> ajātanaṣṭatā kena gambhīrā khalu dharmatā || 5.27 ||

> What prevents its activity? How is activity not other than
> the dharma? How can one existing in that way belong to a
> definite time, and by what can it be unborn or destroyed?
> The nature of dharmas is indeed profound.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

After the state-and-function account of 5.26, the questioner
asks: if past and future dharmas exist substantially, why are
they called past and future? The reply repeats that the times
are distinguished by activity. But what is the activity of a
present eye of the same kind? If it gives and receives a
fruit, past homogeneous causes also give fruit; the criterion
would mix temporal marks.

The next objection asks what prevents an always-existing dharma
from always acting. A proposed answer invokes the lack of a
complete set of conditions, but the opponent objects that
permanent existence has been admitted. Activity itself must
then be explained. If it differs from the dharma, does it have
another activity of its own? If it exists without being past,
present, or future, it looks unconditioned and permanent.
The defender says that activity is not other than the dharma.
The questioner replies that this returns us to the original
problem: how does the very same existing dharma acquire
different temporal assignments?

The defender defines future as not yet arisen, present as
arisen and not yet destroyed, and past as destroyed. The
questioner presses the point: if past and future exist
substantially just as the present does, what makes the same
dharma unborn or destroyed? What was absent before arising,
and what is absent after destruction? Without genuine coming
to be and ceasing, the three times cannot be established.
The earlier appeal to the marks of conditioned things is
called verbal if arising and destruction cannot apply.

The critic then revisits the four grounds from 5.25. Past
means what formerly occurred; future means what will occur
given a cause. In that qualified sense one can say they
“exist,” without saying that they exist substantially like
the present. The cited teaching about past and future blocks
denial of former causes and future results. Under the heading
`paramārthaśūnyatā`, the critic also quotes a teaching that
the eye comes from nowhere when it
arises and is stored nowhere when it ceases: it comes to be
having not been, and passes away having been.

The cognition argument is tested next. The opponent's formula
has consciousness arising from a pair, faculty and object.
Are past and future dharmas a producing condition like the
faculty, or merely objects? A future object far removed in
time, or one that may never arise, cannot straightforwardly
produce present cognition; nirvāṇa is likewise no productive
cause of ordinary arising. If these are only objects, the
critic asks how an object must exist. Memory cognizes what
*was*, and anticipation what *will be*, rather than seeing
each as presently existent. If the object exists just as it
is remembered or anticipated, it would be present; if it
does not, something nonexistent can still be an object.

The suggestion that a past form survives merely dispersed
into atoms is rejected: it would make the atoms permanent
and leave only rearrangement, without real arising or
cessation. The same move cannot explain remembered feelings
and other non-atomic dharmas. Further examples concern the
notion of a thirteenth Essence and the absence of a sound.
They press the conclusion that consciousness may take both
being and absence as its object. The quoted Bodhisattva
statement about knowing only what exists is read as rejecting
false projection, not as proving that every cognition has a
substantially existent object.

The fruit argument is also answered. The Sautrāntikas do not
make a presently nonexistent past deed itself produce the
future fruit; they appeal to a special condition of the
continuum produced by that deed. If both cause and result
already exist substantially, the critic asks what causal
power remains. “Making the result arise” concedes a new
arising; “making it present” either suggests transfer across
places, inapplicable to nonmaterial dharmas, or again a new
determination. On this analysis, the Sarvāstivāda assertion
that past and future exist as substances is rejected. A
scriptural assertion that “all exists” can instead be read
within the twelve Essences.

The opponent returns to the question from 5.23–5.24: without
substantial past and future, how is someone bound to an
affliction or its object? The critic answers through the
presence of a disposition (`anuśaya`) arising from that
affliction and directed toward that object. The Vaibhāṣikas
still insist that past and future exist and invoke the depth
of dharma-nature. The reply says that profundity does not
make analysis impossible. It distinguishes ways of speaking:
form arises and ceases; future becomes present and present
ceases; even a temporal course may be said to arise in a
specified sense. The discussion then returns to its main
sequence.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The Kārikā's questions are not free-standing puzzles.
They test the strongest of the four temporal theories from
5.26. If `kāritra` is other than the dharma, its own temporal
status must be explained. If it is identical, the identity
of the dharma across times makes the difference between
unborn, operative, and destroyed hard to ground. The critic's
central demand is for a real account of arising and ceasing,
not merely three names assigned to one continuously existing
substance.

The subsequent answers preserve several distinctions.
An object of cognition is not automatically a producing
condition of cognition. Remembering a past object does not
present it as presently existing. A past deed can condition
a later result through a transformed continuum without the
past deed itself persisting as a substance. These are
arguments advanced in the Sautrāntika side of the exchange;
the Vaibhāṣika objection is reported, not silently erased.
The appeal to `paramārthaśūnyatā` is tied here to the eye's
actual arising and cessation; it should not be expanded into
a claim that the passage settles every meaning of ultimate
reality.

`Gambhīrā khalu dharmatā` belongs to the Vaibhāṣika rejoinder
at this point in the dialogue. The immediate reply denies
that calling dharma-nature profound settles the specific
objections. The study therefore keeps the line's dramatic
place rather than treating it as Vasubandhu's final appeal
to mystery.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Dravyataḥ` and `svabhāva` name
the substantial-existence claim under dispute. `Kāritra`
is operative activity, whose relation to the dharma is
explicitly tested. `Ālambana` is a cognitive object or
support and must be distinguished from a producing condition.
`Saṃtānaviśeṣa` is the special condition of a continuum,
offered as an account of delayed fruition. The long source
contains several quotations and compressed exchanges; the
conventional translation follows their argumentative order
without presenting damaged quoted words as certain.

**Organon reconstruction.** The key theoretical issue is
whether continuity requires a thing to remain substantially
identical across all times. The counterproposal traces a
genetic continuity through actual arising, cessation, and
transformation of the continuum. This is a project-level
comparison prompted by the Bhāṣya, separate from either
school's doctrinal voice and from implemented architecture.
