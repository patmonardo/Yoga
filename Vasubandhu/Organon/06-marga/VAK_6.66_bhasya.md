# VAK_6.66 Bhāṣya — Ease of Course and Speed of Realization

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> dhyāneṣu mārgaḥ pratipat sukhā duḥkhānyabhūmiṣu |
> dhandhābhijñā mṛdumateḥ kṣiprābhijñetarasya tu || 6.66 ||

> In the four dhyānas, where calm and insight are balanced, the Path is an easy course; on the other grounds their imbalance makes it difficult. Whether realization is slow or quick depends on faculty: it is slow for one of mild faculty and quick for one of sharp faculty.

Ground determines ease of the course; faculty determines the speed of realization.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

The Path itself is also called a course of progress, *pratipad*, because it leads to Nirvāṇa. There are four courses: difficult with slow realization, difficult with quick realization, and the corresponding two easy courses.

In the four dhyānas the Path is an easy course. Its requisite factors are present, calm and insight are balanced, and it proceeds without strenuous effort. In the other grounds—the access concentration, intermediate concentration, and formless grounds—the Path is difficult. Its factors are not fully present, calm or insight is deficient, and it proceeds through effort. Calm is deficient in the access and intermediate concentrations; insight is deficient in the formless grounds.

Either easy or difficult course is further distinguished by realization. For one of mild faculty it is slow, and for one of sharp faculty it is quick. The commentary explains the expression for slow realization as a course on which realization is slow, and alternatively as a course belonging to a slow person; quick realization is understood correspondingly. This same Path then receives the name *bodhipakṣya*, aligned with Awakening.

## 4. Movement of the Commentary

The commentary first renames Mārga as the course that leads to Nirvāṇa and sets out the four combinations. It then explains why the four dhyānas give an easy course and the other grounds a difficult one. A separate faculty contrast determines the pace of realization. The final sentence carries the same Path into the Bodhi-aligned classification of 6.67.

## 5. Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Sukhā` and `duḥkhā` describe structural ease and difficulty of the Path-course, supported or hindered by meditative conditions; they are not identical with pleasant or painful feeling. `Mṛdumati` is explained through mild faculty, `mṛdvindriya`, and its counterpart through sharp faculty, `tīkṣṇendriya`. The kārikā's `dhanyābhijñā` is retained in the continuous witness, while `dhandhābhijñā` is used in the study on the repeated prose evidence. The alternative construal of the slow compound does not collapse the two axes.

**Organon determination.** The concrete course depends on both contemplative ground and practitioner faculty. Ground conditions ease; faculty conditions speed. This two-axis account is an interpretation of the passage's explicit combinations, and it preserves the possibility of an easy but slow or a difficult but quick course.

## 6. Four Courses of Progress

| Contemplative ground | Mild faculty | Sharp faculty |
|---|---|---|
| Four dhyānas | Easy, slow realization | Easy, quick realization |
| Access, intermediate, formless | Difficult, slow realization | Difficult, quick realization |
