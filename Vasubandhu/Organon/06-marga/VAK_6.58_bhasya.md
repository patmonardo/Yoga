# VAK_6.58 Bhāṣya — Regression, First Attainment, and Seeing

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> gotrāc caturṇāṃ pañcānāṃ phalād dhānir na pūrvakāt |
> śaikṣānāryāś ca ṣaḍgotrāḥ saṃcāro nāsti darśane || 6.58 ||

> Four can fall from an acquired disposition and five from the fruit, but none from the original disposition or first noble fruit. Trainees and ordinary persons also have six dispositions; faculty transition does not occur during the Path of Seeing.

The commentary distinguishes a later acquired disposition from an
original one, and records opposing accounts of regression from
arhat fruit.

## 3. Continuous Translation

From what do these arhats fall away: from fruit or from disposition?
Four, beginning with the volition-characterized type, may fall from
their disposition; the regression-susceptible type cannot fall from
its own. Five, beginning with that type, may fall from fruit. Yet
they do not fall from what was first. One does not lose one's first
disposition, since it was made firm by the trainee and beyond-training
Paths. A trainee likewise does not lose their own disposition, made
firm by mundane and supramundane Paths. One can lose a disposition
subsequently gained through sharpening the faculties. Nor does one
lose one's first fruit; one can lose another. Thus stream-entry fruit
is never lost.

On this reckoning the regression-susceptible arhat has three possible
courses: attaining final Nirvāṇa while remaining in that state,
changing faculties, or falling back to trainee status. The
volition-characterized arhat has four: those three and a return to
the regression-susceptible disposition. The other three types have,
in order, five, six, and seven courses, each adding one possibility.
If one falls back and becomes a trainee, one remains in one's first
disposition, not another; otherwise gaining that distinct disposition
would be an advance rather than a loss.

Why is there no loss from what was first? Because what is abandoned
by Seeing is without a real basis (*avastuka*). Those afflictions
proceed as though grounded in a self, since they have self-view as
their root; but that self does not exist. Does this make their
cognitive Support nonexistent? No: they take the real Truths as
Support, but take them falsely. Is there any affliction that does not do so?
There is a distinction. Self-view falsely superimposes a self as
agent, experiencer, and controller upon form and the other real
aggregates. Views of the extremes and the rest follow that superimposition,
and are therefore called baseless. Passion, aversion, conceit, and
ignorance abandoned by Cultivation instead operate as attachment,
hostility, self-elevation, and failure to discern with respect to
form and the other aggregates. There is some real feature in their
range, though the witness's `tacdhrātādimātraṃ` is hard to construe;
there is not even a trace of self. More precisely, Cultivation-abandoned
afflictions have a determinate agreeable or disagreeable feature in
their Support, whereas Seeing-abandoned afflictions have
no corresponding self-feature; this is another reason for calling
the latter baseless.

An affliction may arise in a noble person through a lapse of
mindfulness when they do not examine carefully, but not when they
do—like mistaking a rope for a snake. Self-view and its companions
require deliberate examination, so their arising without it is
incoherent. There is therefore no regression from the abandonment
of afflictions effected by Seeing.

The Sautrāntikas say there is no regression from arhatship either;
the same reasoning applies. How is this known? From scripture and
reason. Scripture says, “What noble insight has abandoned, monks,
is abandoned.” Both the first and last fruits are attained by noble
insight. It also says, “For a trainee, I speak of vigilance in what
requires vigilance,” but does not say this of an arhat. In the
teaching, “Ānanda, I say that gain and honor can obstruct even an
arhat,” the loss concerns only pleasant abiding in this life. And
it says of the unshakeable liberation of consciousness directly
realized by that person, “From this I do not declare a falling away
in any manner.”

If one objects that occasion-dependent liberation can be lost, that
is also admitted; but one must ask whether it means arhatship or the
concentrations. A fundamental concentration is called
occasion-dependent liberation because it comes into presence when
conditions allow. It is called desirable because one repeatedly
seeks it for pleasant abiding in this life; others say because it is
enjoyable. Arhat liberation, which accompanies the person constantly,
cannot be called occasion-dependent, nor desirable in the sense of
requiring renewed pursuit. If loss of arhatship were possible, why
would the Blessed One mention only loss of the superior mental
pleasant abidings? It follows, on this argument, that every arhat's
liberation is unshakeable, although pleasant abiding may be lost.

A person with mild faculties may lose mastery over such abiding when
gain and honor distract them; the damaged witness leaves the exact
wording uncertain. One with sharp faculties may not. The former
is called regression-susceptible and the latter non-regressing.
The other named types are to be understood in the same way. What,
then, distinguishes a non-regressing person, a steady one, and an
immovable one? The non-regressing person reached that state without
sharpening the faculties; the immovable one reached it through such
sharpening. Neither loses the particular meditative attainments
they produce. The steady person merely does not lose the quality in
which they already abide, but does not produce another. Or, if they
produce another, they may be shaken from it. This is the stated
distinction.

The Venerable Bhautika, while still a trainee, repeatedly fell from
occasion-dependent liberation because he was excessively attached
to its enjoyment and had mild faculties. Disheartened, he took up
a knife; unconcerned with body and life, he attained arhatship at
the time of death and entered final Nirvāṇa. Thus even he did not
fall from arhatship.

The *Daśottara* also says, “One Dharma is to be brought forth: desirable,
occasion-dependent liberation of consciousness. One Dharma is to be
directly realized: unshakeable liberation of consciousness.” If the
former were arhatship, why would that tenfold text mention arhatship
twice? Nor is arhatship anywhere simply called something “to be
brought forth”; rather, it is “to be directly realized.” But one
might say that arhatship associated with mild faculties is “to be
brought forth.” What does this indicate? If it means that it can be
brought forth, that is even more possible for the other [case]. If
it means that it ought to be brought forth, the other [case] ought
still more. Thus occasion-dependent liberation is not arhatship.
Why, then, call an arhat
“occasion-liberated”? Because, owing to mild faculties, that person's
concentration comes into presence only when conditions allow. The
occasion-independent arhat is the converse.

The Abhidharma also states three conditions for the arising of the
latent tendency toward desire: it has not been abandoned or fully
known; Dharmas that provoke desire are present; and there is unwise
attention to them. If one says this describes only its full arising,
what other arising has a complete set of causes? That is the
scriptural case. What is the reasoning? If the antidote has arisen
in an arhat and has made the afflictions incapable of arising again,
how could that arhat fall away? If it has not arisen, how are the
influxes exhausted? If the seed-character of affliction has not
been wholly uprooted, how can one with unexhausted influxes be an
arhat?

One then raises the *Ember-Pit Simile* discourse: an instructed noble
disciple living in a certain way may sometimes, through a lapse of
mindfulness, have harmful unwholesome thoughts arise. The objection
claims the disciple there must be an arhat, because the discourse
says that person's Consciousness has long inclined toward seclusion
and tends toward Nirvāṇa, a strength elsewhere assigned to an arhat;
it also says the person has cooled and cast out all Dharmas that
occasion influxes. The reply grants those descriptions, but says
afflictions can still arise in a trainee whose course is not yet
securely fixed. Thus the discourse refers to the trainee stage and
does not establish arhat-regression. Loss of the two fruits attained
by a mundane Path is admitted. The Vaibhāṣikas, however, also
describe loss of arhatship.

Finally, are only arhats of six dispositions, or others too?
Trainees and ordinary persons have these six dispositions as well;
the arhat dispositions continue those earlier ones. Yet there is
no transition of faculties during Seeing. It occurs elsewhere,
because the preparatory practice needed for it is impossible in
the Path of Seeing. One person changes faculties as an ordinary
person; another does so as a faith-resolved trainee.

## 4. Movement of the Commentary

The unit first limits loss by distinguishing acquired from first
disposition and later from first fruit. It explains why what Seeing
abandons does not return, then records a lengthy Sautrāntika
argument against loss of arhatship alongside the Vaibhāṣika
classification that allows it. The closing lines extend the six
dispositions to earlier practitioner stages and exclude faculty
transition during Seeing.

## 5. Translation and Interpretation

**Translation decisions.** `Na pūrvakāt` governs both first
disposition and first noble fruit. `Avastuka` does not mean without
a cognitive Support: the Truths and the real aggregates are Supports,
but self-apprehension of them is false (`vitathālambana`). The
Vaibhāṣika fruit-regression thesis and the Sautrāntika denial remain
attributed. In the latter account, `sāmayikī vimukti` concerns
conditionally manifest meditative attainment, not intermittent
arhatship. `Saṃcāra` is change of faculties, requiring `prayoga`.
The *Daśottara* reply's `anyat` (“other”) is left indefinite in
the continuous English; Xuanzang's parallel reads the contrast as
one with sharper faculties.

**Organon determination.** Later gains can be locally lost without
undoing the first attained disposition or fruit. Treating these as
layers of a Path-history is project synthesis; it does not settle
the school's disagreement about loss of arhatship.
