# VAK_6.58 Bhāṣya — Regression, First Attainment, and Seeing

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> gotrāc caturṇāṃ pañcānāṃ phalād dhānir na pūrvakāt |
> śaikṣānāryāś ca ṣaḍgotrāḥ saṃcāro nāsti darśane || 6.58 ||

> Four can fall from an acquired disposition and five from the fruit, but none from the original disposition or first noble fruit. Trainees and ordinary persons also have six dispositions; faculty transition does not occur during the Path of Seeing.

The commentary distinguishes a later acquired disposition from an
original one, and records opposing accounts of regression from
arhat fruit.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

From what can these arhats fall: fruit or disposition? Four types,
beginning with the volition-characterized type, may fall from a
disposition. The lowest, regression-susceptible type cannot fall
from its own disposition. Five, beginning with that lowest type, may
fall from fruit in the received classification. Yet not from what
was first: the first disposition does not fall away, made firm by the
trainee and beyond-training Paths; a trainee's own disposition is
made firm by mundane and supramundane Paths. A disposition gained
later through sharpened faculties may be lost. Likewise one does
not lose one's first noble fruit, though one may lose a later fruit.
This is why stream-entry fruit is not lost. The commentary then
counts possible courses: the lowest type may remain so until final
Nirvāṇa, change faculties, or fall back to trainee status; the next
type adds return to the lowest disposition. The following types have
successively more such possibilities. Someone falling to trainee
status remains in the first disposition, or the event would be an
increase rather than a loss.

Why are the first abandonments not lost? What Seeing abandons is
called `avastuka`: the self on which these afflictions rely is not
there. This does not mean there is no cognitive Condition. The real
Bases are taken as Conditions, but falsely: self-view projects
an agent, experiencer, or controller onto form and the rest; other
Seeing-abandoned views follow it. Cultivation-abandoned passion,
aversion, pride, and ignorance relate instead to given features of
the Bases, such as agreeable and disagreeable qualities. Their
basis is real in that respect, though no self is present. An
unreflective lapse of mindfulness can permit such an affliction to
arise, as a snake is mistakenly perceived in a rope. A self-view
requiring deliberate construction cannot return in this way after
Seeing has abandoned it. Thus there is no regression from the
abandonment effected by Seeing.

The Sautrāntikas say there is no regression from arhatship either.
They support this from scripture and reason. A cited teaching says
what Noble insight has abandoned remains abandoned; the earliest
and final fruits are gained by Noble insight. The admonition to
practice vigilance is addressed to trainees. Another teaching says
gain and honor may disturb an arhat's pleasant abiding in this life,
while unshakeable liberation of consciousness cannot be lost. If
occasion-dependent liberation can be lost, what is it: arhatship
or the dhyānas? The Sautrāntika answer is that a fundamental dhyāna,
manifest when conditions allow and sought again for pleasant abiding,
is what is occasion-dependent and desirable. Arhat liberation
continues; it is not newly sought in that manner.

The argument then separates arhat status from meditative ease. Gain
and honor can disrupt a mild-faculty person's pleasant abiding;
a sharp-faculty person's may remain undisturbed. The commentary
compares arhat types by whether and how particular meditative
attainments are lost, including the distinction between one who
became stable without faculty-sharpening and one who became
immovable through it. A cited case of a trainee who repeatedly lost
an occasion-dependent attainment ends in arhatship at death, not
loss from arhatship. A further sūtra distinguishes a desirable
occasion-dependent liberation to be developed from unshakeable
liberation of consciousness to be directly realized. If the first
were arhatship, the scriptural distinction would be confused; the
name `samayavimukta` instead marks an arhat whose concentration
becomes manifest only under suitable conditions.

Scripture on the arising of desire-affliction likewise gives
conditions including an unabandoned latent tendency, a present
provoking condition, and unwise attention. The Sautrāntikas then
state the reasoned dilemma: if the arhat has acquired the antidote
that makes afflictions incapable of arising, how could arhatship be
lost? If no such antidote has arisen and the seeds remain, how can
that person be an arhat whose influxes are exhausted? An opposing
citation about harmful thoughts after a lapse of mindfulness is
answered by reading it as applying to a trainee, whose conduct is
not yet securely bound. Regression from the two fruits reached by
a mundane Path is admitted; the Vaibhāṣikas nevertheless describe
regression even from arhatship.

Finally, are these six dispositions found only among arhats? No;
trainees and ordinary persons also have them, and arhat dispositions
continue those earlier types. Faculty change can occur outside the
Path of Seeing, including in an ordinary person or a faith-resolved
trainee. It cannot occur during Seeing, because the preparatory
practice required for faculty change is not possible there.

## 4. Movement of the Commentary

The unit first limits loss by distinguishing acquired from first
disposition and later from first fruit. It explains why what Seeing
abandons does not return, then records a lengthy Sautrāntika
argument against loss of arhatship alongside the Vaibhāṣika
classification that allows it. The closing lines extend the six
dispositions to earlier practitioner stages and exclude faculty
transition during Seeing.

## 5. Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Na pūrvakāt` governs both first
disposition and first noble fruit. `Avastuka` does not mean without
a cognitive Condition: the Bases are real Conditions but
self-apprehension of them is false (`vitathālambana`). The
Vaibhāṣika fruit-regression thesis and the Sautrāntika denial remain
attributed. In the latter account, `sāmayikī vimukti` concerns
conditionally manifest meditative attainment, not intermittent
arhatship. `Saṃcāra` is change of faculties, requiring `prayoga`.

**Organon determination.** Later gains can be locally lost without
undoing the first attained disposition or fruit. Treating these as
layers of a Path-history is project synthesis; it does not settle
the school's disagreement about loss of arhatship.
