# VAK 6.78 — Three Principles of One Unconditioned Liberation

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

```text
असंस्कृतैव धात्वाख्या विरागो रागसंक्षयः ।
प्रहाणधातुरन्येषां निरोधाख्यस्तु वस्तुनः ॥ ६.७८ ॥
```

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

```text
asaṃskṛtaiva dhātvākhyā virāgo rāgasaṃkṣayaḥ |
prahāṇadhātur anyeṣāṃ nirodhākhyas tu vastunaḥ || 6.78 ||
```

The Bhāṣya asks what distinguishes the three liberation Principles and answers that they are three names of the same unconditioned liberation, differentiated according to the terminus of cessation.

## 3. Lexical Analysis

| Pada | Morphology | Force in this passage |
|---|---|---|
| asaṃskṛtā | feminine nominative singular | unconditioned [liberation] |
| eva | restrictive particle | precisely, exclusively |
| dhātu-ākhyā | feminine nominative singular compound | bearing the designation “Principle” |
| virāgaḥ | masculine nominative singular | dispassion; Principle named from abandonment of rāga |
| rāga-saṃkṣayaḥ | masculine nominative singular | complete exhaustion of attachment |
| prahāṇa-dhātuḥ | masculine nominative singular | Principle of abandonment |
| anyeṣām | masculine genitive plural | of the others, namely afflictions other than rāga |
| nirodha-ākhyaḥ | masculine nominative singular | bearing the name cessation |
| tu | adversative particle | whereas, distinguishing the third designation |
| vastunaḥ | neuter genitive singular | of the basis already freed from affliction, according to the Bhāṣya |

The sense of **saṃkṣaya**, complete exhaustion, carries through the compact clauses. The Bhāṣya supplies **kleśanirmuktasya** as the decisive restriction on **vastunaḥ**.

## 4. Grammar

The opening identifies the Principle-designated member with the same **asaṃskṛta vimukti** discussed in 6.75–6.76. The three following expressions distribute its names by genitive relation:

```text
rāgasya
    abandonment of rāga

rāgād anyeṣāṃ kleśānām
    abandonment of the other afflictions

kleśanirmuktasya vastunaḥ
    cessation of the basis already freed from affliction
```

The terse **nirodhākhyas tu vastunaḥ** cannot be translated adequately as generic “cessation of a thing.” The Bhāṣya’s qualifier determines which basis is meant.

The verse classifies one unconditioned liberation under three relations. It does not grammatically require three independent entities or a strictly narrated temporal sequence.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

The [liberation] named as a Principle is exclusively unconditioned. Dispassion is the complete exhaustion of attachment; the abandonment-Principle concerns the others, whereas what is called cessation concerns the basis.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> The designation “Principle” is exclusively unconditioned. Dispassion is the exhaustion of attachment; the Principle of abandonment concerns the other afflictions, while the Principle called cessation concerns the basis.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

One accomplished freedom receives three determinations according to what is brought to an end. As attachment ceases it is dispassion; as the other afflictions cease it is abandonment; as the already purified conditioned basis itself ceases it is cessation.

The plurality belongs to the relation and terminus of freedom, not to a multiplication of unconditioned realities. Liberation is one while its Meaning is articulated through three distinct endings.

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**`Virāga-dhātu` / `prahāṇa-dhātu` / `nirodha-dhātu` — three Principle-names**

The Bhāṣya enumerates three Principle-names: dispassion, abandonment, and cessation. All three designate one *asaṃskṛta-vimukti*, one unconditioned liberation. The difference is relational: each name specifies what the cessation is considered in relation to, and each fixes a distinct terminus. The names are not redundant.

**`Dhātu` — Principle, in this verse**

Not the eighteen cognitive Principles of Chapter 1, and not Kāmadhātu, Rūpadhātu and Ārūpyadhātu as existential Principles. It names a Principle or Principle determination of liberation, namely unconditioned cessation classified by its terminus.

**`Virāga-dhātu` — Principle of dispassion**

The Bhāṣya: *rāgasya prahāṇam*, abandonment of rāga. Virāga is therefore not an emotional attitude of detachment but the complete exhaustion (*saṃkṣaya*) of appropriating attachment.

**`Prahāṇa-dhātu` — Principle of abandonment**

Abandonment of the “others”, *rāgād anye kleśāḥ*, the afflictions other than rāga. The first two names divide afflictive abandonment according to the affliction serving as terminus.

**`Nirodha-dhātu` — Principle of cessation**

The decisive Bhāṣya phrase is *kleśanirmuktasya vastunaḥ prahāṇam*, cessation of the basis already freed from the afflictions. **`Vastu`** is not an unspecified metaphysical thing and not the afflictions themselves; it is the conditioned basis or continuum after afflictive liberation. Virāga and prahāṇa concern the cessation of afflictions; nirodha concerns the cessation of the affliction-free conditioned basis. This is aligned with final cessation, but the immediate passage does not elaborate its temporal or cosmological mechanics.

**`Eka-artha` — one reality, three names**

The chapter's recurring semantic form: the same underlying reality receives different names under different determinate relations. Unlike the thirty-seven-to-ten reduction, the relation here is three Principle-names to one unconditioned liberation.

**`Saṃskṛta-vimukti` / `asaṃskṛta-vimukti` — conditioned and unconditioned liberation**

6.76 distinguished liberated mental actuality (conditioned) from abandonment of the afflictions (unconditioned). 6.78 articulates the unconditioned side under three terminus-relations. The Aśaikṣa consciousness of 6.77 remains conditioned; the Principles named here are unconditioned.

## 8. Logical Determination

Let **U** be the one unconditioned liberation. Then:

```text
U consideredAs AbandonmentOfRāga
    → DispassionPrinciple

U consideredAs AbandonmentOfOtherAfflictions
    → AbandonmentPrinciple

U consideredAs CessationOfAfflictionFreeBasis
    → CessationPrinciple
```

Therefore:

```text
ThreeDesignations
    do not entail
ThreeUnconditionedSubstances
```

Nor does one underlying reality erase the distinctions:

```text
Terminus(DispassionPrinciple) = Rāga
Terminus(AbandonmentPrinciple) = OtherKleśas
Terminus(CessationPrinciple) = AfflictionFreeBasis
```

The classification is one-to-many by relation:

```text
1 unconditioned liberation
    × 3 cessation-termini
    = 3 valid Principle-determinations
```

## 9. Interpretive Note

**Translation note.** The Bhāṣya says the three Principles name one unconditioned liberation: abandonment of attachment, abandonment of the other afflictions, and cessation of the basis already free of affliction. Its prose construes `vastunaḥ` as that purified basis; the kārikā’s syntax remains compressed.

This verse is exceptionally important for the project’s controlled Principle semantics. Principle again proves not to possess one isolated dictionary meaning. Its meaning is produced by the system in which it functions:

```text
Chapter 1
    Principle of cognition

existential classification
    Principle of possible existence

VAK 6.78
    Principle-determination of unconditioned liberation
```

Here the three Principles are three ways the End determines itself according to what has ended. Virāga names freedom relative to attachment; Prahāṇa names it relative to the remaining afflictions; Nirodha names it relative to the purified conditioned basis itself.

The third is especially severe. Liberation is not exhausted by purity of an ongoing conditioned consciousness. Even the affliction-free basis remains conditioned and can itself reach cessation. Thus the Path’s End is not a perfected *bhava* preserved forever.

For the Organon reconstruction, one unconditioned freedom differentiates itself through its determinate termini. That is a relational unity, not a trinity of substances. The source-level claim remains Vasubandhu’s exact threefold naming of the same unconditioned liberation.
