# VAK_6.06

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

```sanskrit
व्यपकर्षद्वयवतो नासन्तुष्टमहेच्छयोः ।
लब्धे भूयःस्पृहातुष्टिरलब्धेच्छा महेच्छता ॥ ६.६ ॥
```

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

```text
vyapakarṣa-dvayavato nāsantuṣṭa-mahecchayoḥ |
labdhe bhūyaḥ-spṛhātuṣṭir alabdhēcchā mahecchatā || 6.6 ||
```

VAK 6.06 states the practical conditions under which the cultivation introduced
in 6.05 can be accomplished. The practitioner requires twofold withdrawal,
which is easy for one of few desires and contentment but not for one governed
by discontent or great desire.

## 3. Padaccheda and Lexical Analysis

```text
vyapakarṣa-dvaya-vataḥ | na | asantuṣṭa-mahecchayoḥ |
labdhe | bhūyaḥ-spṛhā | atuṣṭiḥ | alabdha-icchā | mahecchatā
```

| Form | Morphology | Contextual sense |
|---|---|---|
| `vyapakarṣa-dvaya-vataḥ` | genitive singular masculine possessive compound | of one possessing the two withdrawals |
| `na` | negative particle | not |
| `asantuṣṭa-mahecchayoḥ` | genitive dual masculine compound | of the discontented person and the person of great desire |
| `labdhe` | locative singular neuter past participle of `√labh` | with respect to what has been obtained |
| `bhūyaḥ-spṛhā` | nominative singular feminine compound | longing for more |
| `atuṣṭiḥ` | nominative singular feminine | discontent or dissatisfaction |
| `alabdha-icchā` | nominative singular feminine compound | desire for what has not been obtained |
| `mahecchatā` | nominative singular feminine abstract noun | great desire |

The first half is elliptical. The Bhāṣya supplies the governing question: for
whom is the twofold withdrawal needed for cultivation readily accomplished?
It answers by naming the positive conditions `alpeccha` and `saṃtuṣṭa`, then
excludes their opposites.

The second half states inherited definitions that the Bhāṣya subsequently
subjects to criticism and revises. The kārikā wording must therefore remain
distinct from Vasubandhu's stronger prose determination.

## 4. Grammar

The first half contrasts possessors of practical qualifications:

```text
vyapakarṣa-dvayavataḥ [sukaraṃ / bhāvanā sidhyati]
    for one possessing twofold withdrawal

na asantuṣṭa-mahecchayoḥ
    not for the discontented or greatly desirous
```

The exact supplied predicate comes from the Bhāṣya's framing rather than the
kārikā's expressed syntax. The genitive singular and dual mark the persons in
relation to whom accomplishment is affirmed or denied.

The second half consists of two nominal definitions:

```text
labdhe bhūyaḥ-spṛhā
    atuṣṭiḥ

desire for more with respect to what is obtained
    is discontent
```

```text
alabdhe icchā
    mahecchatā

desire with respect to what is unobtained
    is great desire
```

The Bhāṣya identifies a logical instability: the additional amount desired in
the first definition is itself not yet obtained. Vasubandhu therefore replaces
mere object-status with two distinct intentional relations.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> [Cultivation is accomplished] for one possessing the two withdrawals, not
> for the discontented or greatly desirous. Longing for more with respect to
> what has been obtained is discontent; desire for what has not been obtained
> is great desire.

The supplied predicate in brackets follows the Bhāṣya's explicit question and
answer.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> Cultivation is practicable for one withdrawn in both body and mind—one of
> few desires and contentment—not for one governed by discontent or great
> desire. The inherited formulation calls longing for more of what one already
> has discontent and desire for what one lacks great desire. Vasubandhu refines
> this: discontent is vexation because present possessions seem inferior or
> insufficient, whereas great desire projects itself toward superior and
> abundant goods not yet possessed.

The bodily/mental division, the positive qualifications, and the revised
definitions belong to the Bhāṣya.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> The cultivation of Meaning requires a corresponding organization of life.
> Bodily withdrawal loosens practical entanglement; mental withdrawal loosens
> the unskillful thought that reproduces it. Yet withdrawal remains difficult
> while desire moves in two directions: negatively, as vexation at the
> deficiency of what is present, and positively, as projection toward a
> superior future possession. Contentment and fewness of desires interrupt
> this circuit and establish the practical ground in which cultivation can be
> sustained.

The circuit is:

```text
present possession
    judged deficient
        → vexation
        → projection of superior possession
        → renewed acquisition
        → newly deficient possession
```

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**`Vyapakarṣa-dvaya` — twofold withdrawal**

The Bhāṣya resolves the compound as bodily withdrawal
(`kāya-vyapakarṣa`) and mental withdrawal (`citta-vyapakarṣa`). Together they
make sustained cultivation practicable.

**`Kāya-vyapakarṣa` — bodily withdrawal**

Distancing the practitioner from `saṃsarga`, association or social
entanglement. It reorganizes the external situations that continually solicit
acquisitive and unskillful activity.

**`Citta-vyapakarṣa` — mental withdrawal**

Distancing the practitioner from `akuśala-vitarka`, unskillful thought. It is
not reducible to physical solitude, since the relevant entanglement may
continue as mental activity after the external occasion is removed.

**`Saṃsarga` — association or entanglement**

The external nexus from which bodily withdrawal distances the practitioner.
The Bhāṣya does not condemn every relation indiscriminately; the term functions
here within the practical conditions for cultivation.

**`Akuśala-vitarka` — unskillful thought**

The mental activity from which inward withdrawal distances the practitioner.
This is the internal counterpart to external association.

**`Alpeccha` / `alpecchatā` — of few desires / fewness of desires**

The disposition opposed to great desire. It contracts the projected field of
acquisition and thereby makes twofold withdrawal easier.

**`Saṃtuṣṭa` / `saṃtuṣṭi` — content / contentment**

The disposition opposed to discontent. It establishes a non-vexed relation to
present sufficiency rather than measuring possession against imagined
superiority and abundance.

**`Atuṣṭi` / `asantuṣṭi` — discontent**

The kārikā's inherited account defines it as longing for more of what has been
obtained. Vasubandhu's revised prose definition is more exact: vexation
(`paritāpa`) because what is possessed is not excellent or abundant.

**`Mahecchatā` — great desire**

The desire for excellent and abundant things not possessed. It is not merely
any orientation toward an absent object, but the expansive acquisition-drive
organized around superiority and abundance.

**`Paritāpa` — vexation or distress**

The affective relation that distinguishes discontent in Vasubandhu's revision.
Its object is present possession as judged inferior or insufficient.

**`Praṇīta` and `prabhūta` — excellent and abundant**

The qualitative and quantitative poles organizing the revised definitions.
Discontent judges present possession as lacking them; great desire projects
them into the unpossessed object.

## 8. Logical Determination

The conditions favorable to cultivation are:

```text
Possesses(person, BodilyWithdrawal)
AND Possesses(person, MentalWithdrawal)
AND HasFewDesires(person)
AND Content(person)
    → FavorableConditionFor(person, Cultivation)
```

The two withdrawals have distinct ranges:

```text
BodilyWithdrawal
    counters SocialEntanglement

MentalWithdrawal
    counters UnskillfulThought
```

The inherited definitions are:

```text
DesiresMoreOf(person, ObtainedObject)
    → Discontent(person)

Desires(person, UnobtainedObject)
    → GreatDesire(person)
```

Vasubandhu's objection is:

```text
AdditionalQuantityDesired(x)
    → NotYetObtained(x)

therefore
ObtainedVersusUnobtained
    ↛ SufficientlyDistinguishes(Discontent, GreatDesire)
```

The revised definitions are:

```text
Possesses(person, object)
AND Judges(object, InferiorOrInsufficient)
AND VexedBy(person, object)
    → Discontent(person)

NotPossesses(person, object)
AND Projects(object, ExcellentAndAbundant)
AND Desires(person, object)
    → GreatDesire(person)
```

Thus the distinction is relational:

```text
Discontent
    negative relation to present possession

GreatDesire
    positive projection toward superior future possession
```

## 9. Interpretive Note

**Cultivation has practical conditions.** VAK 6.05 defines cultivational
Prajñā as born from samādhi; 6.06 asks what makes such cultivation sustainable.
The answer reorganizes body, mind, social association, thought, and desire.

**External and internal withdrawal are irreducible.** Physical solitude does
not by itself remove unskillful thought, while an inward claim of detachment
without practical reorganization leaves the external reproduction of that
thought untouched.

**The inherited taxonomy is tested rather than merely repeated.** Vasubandhu
recognizes that desire for “more” concerns an increment not yet obtained. He
therefore replaces a weak extensional division with a stronger relational
one.

**Contentment is not liberating cognition itself.** Fewness of desires,
contentment, and twofold withdrawal are conditions favorable to cultivation.
They must not be identified with the Path of Seeing or its distinctive
abandonment-operation.

**The Path interrupts a practical recursion.** What is present is judged
insufficient; imagined superiority organizes acquisition; the newly acquired
again appears deficient. The two opposing virtues disable both the vexed and
projective moments of this loop.

**Organon interpretation.** VAK 6.06 begins to specify the bhūmi required for
Path Meaning. This is project synthesis, not Vasubandhu's own architectural
terminology:

```text
samādhi-born cultivation
    requires a viable practical ground

practical ground
    bodily withdrawal
    mental withdrawal
    contentment
    fewness of desires

effect
    attention is no longer continually captured
    by deficiency and projected acquisition
```

Meaning cannot become Path merely by achieving a word-independent object in
cognition. Its bearer must also cease reproducing the practical relations that
continually disperse cultivation. The later `saṃyama-bhūmi` synthesis may
integrate this ground, but the Kośa terms here remain `vyapakarṣa`,
`alpecchatā`, and `saṃtuṣṭi`.
