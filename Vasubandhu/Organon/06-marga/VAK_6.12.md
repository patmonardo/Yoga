# VAK_6.12

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

```sanskrit
आनापानस्मृतिः प्रज्ञा पञ्चभूर्वायुर्गोचरा ।
कामाश्रया न बाह्यानां षड्विधा गणनादिभिः ॥ ६.१२ ॥
```

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

```text
ānāpānasmṛtiḥ prajñā pañcabhūr vāyur-gocarā |
kāmāśrayā na bāhyānāṃ ṣaḍvidhā gaṇanādibhiḥ || 6.12 ||
```

VAK 6.12 classifies recollection of inhalation and exhalation and introduces
its sixfold completion. Although the practice is named `smṛti`, its intrinsic
nature is discriminative comprehension. It occurs on five grounds, takes breath as wind for its
cognitive field, depends upon a Desire Realm support, does not belong to
outsiders, and develops through six operations beginning with counting.

The predicates classify a single practice along independent dimensions. The
Bhāṣya distinguishes name from intrinsic nature: `smṛti` names the force by
which the practice functions, while `discriminative comprehension` states what it is by nature.

## 4. Grammar

The opening nominal identity is exact:

```text
ānāpānasmṛtiḥ
    discriminative comprehension [svabhāvataḥ]

recollection of breathing
    is discriminative comprehension [by intrinsic nature]
```

The Bhāṣya supplies `svabhāva` and explains the apparent difference of names:
the practice is designated `smṛti` because it functions through the force or
support of recollection.

The remaining predicates answer distinct classificatory questions:

```text
pañcabhūḥ
    on how many grounds? five

vāyurgocarā
    what is its cognitive field? wind

kāmāśrayā
    what is its basis? the Desire Realm

na bāhyānām
    to whom does it not belong? outsiders

ṣaḍvidhā gaṇanādibhiḥ
    how is it completed? through six operations beginning with counting
```

The standard five-ground count is fixed by the Bhāṣya as the Desire Realm,
three preliminary concentrations, and the intermediate concentration. It also
reports an alternative eight-ground count if equanimity is admitted in the
principal dhyānas.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> Recollection of inhalation and exhalation is discriminative comprehension; it belongs to five
> grounds and has wind as its cognitive field. It has a Desire Realm basis,
> does not belong to outsiders, and is sixfold through counting and the other
> operations.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> Recollection of inhalation and exhalation is discriminative comprehension by
> nature. It occurs on five grounds, has wind as its cognitive field, and
> rests on a Desire Realm basis. It does not belong to outsiders; it is
> sixfold, beginning with counting.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> A minimal embodied process becomes Path not through passive observation but
> through an ordered transformation of cognition. Recollection supplies
> continuity; discriminative comprehension discriminates what that continuity contains. Counting
> establishes reliable sequence, following tracks process, establishing
> stabilizes the cognitive Condition, discerning opens breath into its dharma-complex,
> turning redirects the practice toward the highest mundane Path-conditions,
> and purification enters Seeing and the subsequent Paths. The breath is not
> itself liberating; its disciplined determination becomes continuous with
> liberating cognition.

The movement is:

```text
embodied wind-process
    → recollected sequence
    → stable cognitive Condition
    → discriminated Base-field
    → higher wholesome roots
    → Path of Seeing and beyond
```

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**`Ānāpāna` — inhalation and exhalation**

The Bhāṣya defines these as winds entering and leaving the body. The practice
therefore takes an Elemental process rather than an enduring breather-self as
its immediate cognitive field.

**`Ānāpānasmṛti` — recollection of inhalation and exhalation**

The practice assigned in 6.09 to one dominated by discursive thought. Its name
emphasizes recollective continuity, while its intrinsic nature is discriminative comprehension.

**`Smṛti` — recollection**

The stabilizing force or support through which the practice functions. The
Bhāṣya explains the name through `tad-balādhāna-vṛtti`: the practice proceeds
through reliance upon the power of recollection.

**Discriminative comprehension**

The intrinsic nature of breath-recollection. This prevents the practice from
being reduced to bare attention to sensation. Its development discriminates
Great Elements, derivative Form, consciousness (`citta`) and mental factors (`caitta`), and ultimately the five
Bases.

**`Pañcabhū` — belonging to five grounds**

The standard Bhāṣya enumeration comprises the Desire Realm, three preliminary
concentrations, and the intermediate concentration because the practice is
associated with equanimity. An attributed alternative expands the range to
eight grounds if equanimity is allowed in the principal dhyānas.

**`Vāyur-gocarā` — having wind as cognitive field**

The cognitive field is breath as a moving wind-process. `Gocara` marks what the
cognition ranges over and must remain distinct from `āśraya`, the support upon
which the practice arises.

**`Kāmāśraya` — having a Desire Realm basis**

The embodied basis of the practice belongs to the Desire Realm even when the
cognition itself occurs on a more refined meditative ground. Cognitive field, basis,
and level are independent coordinates.

**`Na bāhyānām` — not belonging to outsiders**

The Bhāṣya gives two reasons: outsiders lack the instruction and cannot awaken
on their own to the subtle dharmas involved. The claim concerns dependence on
a determinate teaching and method, not ownership of breathing as a natural
process.

**`Tattva-manaskāra` — attention according to actuality**

The Bhāṣya classifies breath-recollection under this mode. It thereby rejects
overextended imaginative accounts of where the breath travels when those
accounts conflict with the actual process.

**`Gaṇanā` — counting**

Counting inhalations and exhalations from one to ten through recollection,
without forcing body or consciousness. Distraction requires restarting until samādhi is
obtained.

**The three counting faults**

```text
ūnagaṇanā
    under-counting

adhikagaṇanā
    over-counting

saṃkara
    confusing inhalation and exhalation
```

These faults show that sequence fidelity is itself part of the practice.

**`Anugama` — following**

Following the course of inhalation and exhalation without artificial
construction, discerning how far each proceeds. The Bhāṣya reports expansive
descriptions but rejects their overly literal extension where it violates
`tattva-manaskāra`.

**`Sthāpanā` — establishing**

Stabilizing the breath at a selected bodily point from the nose-tip to the
toes, compared to a thread of jewels. This supplies a governed cognitive
location.

**`Upalakṣaṇā` — discerning**

Analytical expansion of the breath beyond “mere wind”:

```text
four Great Elements
    → derivative material form
    → dependent consciousness (`citta`) and mental factors (`caitta`)
    → five Bases
```

The minimal process becomes an entry into discrimination of the Base
complex.

**`Vivarta` — turning**

Turning away from mere increase of cognition centered on the wind Condition and
connecting the practice with higher wholesome roots through the Highest
Mundane Dharmas. It redirects stabilization into explicit Path-preparation.

**`Pariśuddhi` — purification**

Defined by the Bhāṣya as entry into the Path of Seeing and the subsequent
Paths. This is the culmination of the sixfold sequence, not simply cleaner or
more tranquil breath attention.

## 9. Interpretive Note


**Translation note.** The Bhāṣya distinguishes the practice's name (recollection) from its nature (discriminative comprehension), specifies its grounds and six operations, and explains its exclusion of outsiders without instruction; one grounding statement is textually uneven.

**The name is not the intrinsic nature.** `Smṛti` describes the stabilizing
force; discriminative comprehension defines the practice. A Dataset that classifies only by the
surface name would miss its operative cognitive type.

**Field, basis, and ground remain distinct.** The breath-wind is the cognitive
field; Desire Realm embodiment is the basis; five meditative grounds give the
standard operating range. None should be inferred from either of the others.

**The ground-count has an attributed alternative.** The five-ground account is
the standard explanation here; eight depends upon another position concerning
equanimity in the principal dhyānas. Both provenance and doctrinal condition
must be retained.

**Counting is not mechanical insignificance.** Its faults identify precise
failures of sequence, cardinality, and event-type discrimination. Restarting
after distraction trains faithful continuity rather than forced respiration.

**Discerning changes the scale of the cognitive field.** Breath begins as vāyu but opens
into Great Elements, derived Form, mental dependence, and the five Bases. The
practice does not remain enclosed within one isolated sensation.

**Purification enters Mārga.** The final operations transform a stabilizing
exercise into continuity with the Highest Mundane Dharmas, the Path of Seeing,
and subsequent Paths. This is stronger than a generic claim that breath calms
consciousness.

**Organon interpretation.** VAK 6.12 offers an extraordinary native pipeline of
Practical Meaning. This is project synthesis, while the six operations and
their doctrinal functions are Bhāṣya-grounded:

```text
raw embodied event
    vāyu

ordered observation
    gaṇanā + anugama

stable cognitive field
    sthāpanā

semantic discrimination
    upalakṣaṇā

Path redirection
    vivarta

liberating transition
    pariśuddhi
```

Meaning emerges here neither from the word “breath” nor from sensation in
isolation. It emerges through the ordered relations that connect Elemental
process, recollection, discriminative comprehension, Base analysis, wholesome roots, and Path.
This is a native Kośa model of how an empirical process becomes a vehicle of
Mārga without being mistaken for an independently liberating process.
