# VAK_6.06 Bhāṣya — Two Withdrawals; Discontent and Great Desire

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> vyapakarṣa-dvayavato nāsantuṣṭa-mahecchayoḥ |
>
> labdhe bhūyaḥ-spṛhātuṣṭir alabdhēcchā mahecchatā || 6.6 ||

> Cultivation is readily sustained by one withdrawn in body and consciousness,
> content and of few desires; it is not so for the discontented or greatly
> desirous. Discontent is vexation over what one has as neither excellent nor
> abundant; great desire seeks excellent and abundant things one has not
> obtained.

The Bhāṣya identifies the two withdrawals and the two favorable dispositions.
It also tests the verse's inherited distinction and gives the sharper
definitions used in this anchor.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

For someone thus engaged in cultivation, how is cultivation accomplished?
The verse says: for one possessing the two withdrawals. That means withdrawal
in body and consciousness, by distancing oneself from association and from
unskillful thought. For whom are these two withdrawals easy? For one of few
desires who is content, not for the discontented or greatly desirous. What are
discontent and great desire?

The verse says that discontent is longing for more with respect to what has
been obtained, while great desire is desire for what has not been obtained.
The Abhidharmikas explain this as wanting more of fine robes and similar goods
already possessed, versus wanting such goods when unpossessed. But the
additional amount desired is itself not obtained, even in the first case;
what, then, distinguishes the two? This formulation fits better: discontent
is vexation because what one has is neither excellent nor abundant; great
desire is desire for excellent and abundant things not yet obtained.

## 4. Movement of the Commentary

The commentary first supplies the omitted cultivation predicate, then
specifies bodily and mental withdrawal by the activities from which they
withdraw. It asks which dispositions make that practice easy. After stating
the inherited obtained/unobtained division, it finds that division
insufficient and replaces it with two distinct relations: vexation at what
one possesses and desire projected toward excellent, abundant goods.

## 5. Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Vyapakarṣa-dvaya` is bodily and mental
withdrawal, not a single withdrawal repeated twice. The Bhāṣya specifies
`citta-vyapakarṣa` as distance from unskillful thought; it does not describe
the destruction of consciousness. The positive pair, fewness of desires and
contentment, comes from the prose. The anchor uses the prose's revised
definitions of discontent and great desire while the continuous translation
retains the inherited verse formulation and attributed Abhidharmika account.

**Organon determination.** Cultivation needs a practical ground in which
consciousness can sustain its work. A later MetaPlan can distinguish the
withdrawal's bodily and mental ranges and represent the shift from the
inherited possession test to Vasubandhu's intentional relations. This is a
project formalization, not source terminology.

## 6. The Revised Distinction

| Disposition | Inherited account | Bhāṣya's revised account |
|---|---|---|
| asaṃtuṣṭi | more of what is already got | vexation that what is got is poor or scant |
| mahecchatā | wanting what is not got | wanting the excellent and abundant not got |

The first distinction fails because “more” of what one possesses is itself
unpossessed. The revised distinction uses the practitioner's relation to
present and projected goods rather than possession-status alone.
