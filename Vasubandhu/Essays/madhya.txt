Vasubandhu’s Madhyāntavibhāga-bhāṣya (commentary on the Madhyāntavibhāga-kārikās attributed to Maitreyanātha) is a foundational Yogācāra treatise that systematically discriminates the Middle Path from extremes. It presents the complete structure of Mahāyāna path and view in five chapters, framed by an opening statement of the treatise’s seven topics: characteristic (lakṣaṇa), obstruction (āvaraṇa), reality (tattva), cultivation of the antidote (pratipakṣabhāvanā), the stages therein (avasthā), attainment of the fruit (phalaprāpti), and the unsurpassed nature of the vehicle (yānānuttarya).
Chapter I – Characteristic (Lakṣaṇapariccheda)

The root phenomenon is abhūtaparikalpa (false imagination / the imaginary construction of the unreal). It exists, yet the duality of grasper and grasped does not exist within it; emptiness (śūnyatā) exists within it, and abhūtaparikalpa exists within emptiness. This yields the Middle: neither exclusively empty nor non-empty.

    False imagination appears as object, sentient being, self, and cognition, yet none of these objects truly exist; therefore the cognizing consciousness is also unreal in the way it appears.
    It comprises the three natures: imaginary (parikalpita), dependent (paratantra), and perfected (pariniṣpanna).
    Entry into non-existence proceeds by first realizing representation-only, then the non-existence of the object, then the non-existence of the representation itself.
    It is analyzed as mind and mental factors of the three realms, as consciousness and its associated factors, as the ālayavijñāna (causal) and the seven evolving consciousnesses (experiential), and as the twelve links of dependent origination that produce affliction in three, two, and seven modes.

Emptiness is then defined as the absence of duality and the presence of that absence. It is neither existent nor non-existent, neither different from nor identical with false imagination. Synonyms are suchness, the limit of reality, the signless, the ultimate, and the dharma-realm. It is both defiled and pure (like water, gold, or space that becomes pure when adventitious stains are removed). Sixteen emptinesses are enumerated and related to the practitioner’s aims. Emptiness is established as the necessary condition for both bondage and liberation.
Chapter II – Obstruction (Āvaraṇapariccheda)

Obstructions are classified as pervasive (for bodhisattvas), partial (for śrāvakas), excessive, equal, and those that abandon saṃsāra. The nine fetters obstruct equanimity, insight into reality, etc. Ten further obstructions block the ten “wholesome factors” (virtue, awakening, resolve, intelligence, non-error, non-obstruction, non-fear, non-miserliness, mastery). These are correlated with ten causal functions (production, maintenance, support, manifestation, transformation, separation, maturation, conviction, demonstration, attainment).

Additional obstructions are listed for the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, the ten perfections, and the ten bodhisattva stages (each stage counteracting a particular form of non-afflictive ignorance regarding a specific aspect of the dharma-realm). Ultimately all obstructions reduce to the two: afflictive and cognitive.
Chapter III – Reality (Tattvapariccheda)

Reality is presented in ten modes, all rooted in the three natures and serving as antidotes to ten forms of self-grasping:

    Root reality = the three natures themselves (imaginary is permanently non-existent; dependent is existent yet not ultimately real; perfected is both existent and non-existent in the relevant senses).
    Characteristic reality = absence of superimposition and denial regarding persons, dharmas, grasper/grasped, and existence/non-existence.
    Non-erroneous reality = the four seals applied to the three natures (impermanence, suffering, emptiness, selflessness, each in three modes).
    Fruit-and-cause reality = the four truths configured through the three natures.
    Coarse and subtle reality = conventional (threefold: designation, practice, manifestation) and ultimate (threefold: object, attainment, practice).
    Established reality = worldly consensus and reasoned establishment.
    Purified cognitive domain = the two pure knowledges whose object is only the perfected nature.
    Inclusive reality = how the five categories (name, appearance, conceptualization, right knowledge, suchness) are subsumed under the three natures.
    Differentiated reality = the sevenfold suchness of the Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra.
    Skillful reality = the ten knowledges (aggregates, elements, sense-bases, dependent origination, possibilities/impossibilities, faculties, times, truths, vehicles, conditioned/unconditioned) that counteract the ten self-views, each knowledge itself analyzed through the three natures.

Chapter IV – Cultivation of the Antidote, Stages, and Fruit

The antidote is the cultivation of the thirty-seven factors of enlightenment, presented in progressive order:

    Four foundations of mindfulness (entry into the four truths).
    Four right efforts.
    Four bases of psychic power (removal of five faults by eight applications).
    Five faculties and five powers (strengthening the factors that lead to liberation).
    Seven factors of awakening (on the path of seeing).
    Eightfold path (on the path of cultivation).

Special features of bodhisattva practice are noted: the object includes self and other; the mode of attention is non-apprehension; the aim is non-abiding nirvāṇa.

Stages are manifold: causal, entry, application, fruition, residual duty / no residual duty, special, superior / unsurpassed; or according to the ten stages plus the three bodies of a Buddha; or according to purity of the dharma-realm (impure, partly pure, pure).

Fruits are fivefold in classical Abhidharma terms (maturation, dominance, outflow, manly effort, disconnection) and further subdivided into successive, initial, repeated-practice, completion, favorable, opposing, disconnection, special, superior, and unsurpassed fruits.
Chapter V – Unsurpassed Nature of the Vehicle

The Mahāyāna is unsurpassed in three respects:

    Practice – ten perfections practiced in six modes (supreme, attentive, concordant with the teaching, free from the two extremes, distinctive, and non-distinctive). The supreme mode itself has twelve aspects. Attention to the Mahāyāna teachings is supported by ten dharma-activities that generate immeasurable merit. Concordant practice is free from six kinds of distraction and free from ten kinds of error (corresponding to the ten “vajra topics”). Freedom from extremes is the Middle Path that avoids the many pairs of extremes enumerated in the Ratnakūṭa. Distinctive and non-distinctive practice occur across the ten stages.
    Object – twelve successive objects of meditation ranging from the designation of the teachings through the dharma-realm to the final culmination.
    Attainment – ten successive attainments from possession of the lineage through final non-obstruction and the continuous display of awakening.

The treatise closes by explaining its own title and purpose: it reveals the Middle, is profound, of great meaning, of complete meaning, and removes all that is unwholesome.

In short, the work is a comprehensive map of Yogācāra view and path that repeatedly returns to the three natures, the Middle between extremes, and the progressive purification of the mind from both afflictive and cognitive obscurations, culminating in the non-abiding nirvāṇa of a Buddha.

Traditional view (the one the tradition itself holds):

It is the same Vasubandhu. He first composed the Abhidharmakośa (and its auto-commentary) while still working within the Sarvāstivāda/Sautrāntika framework. Later, under the influence of his half-brother Asaṅga, he converted to Mahāyāna and became one of the principal systematizers of Yogācāra. The Madhyāntavibhāga-bhāṣya belongs to this later period. The root verses are attributed to Maitreya (received/transmitted by Asaṅga), and Vasubandhu wrote the commentary.

The text itself is explicit about its Mahāyāna identity. Chapter V is devoted entirely to proving the yānānuttarya — the unsurpassed character of the Mahāyāna — in practice, object, and attainment. That is a deliberate self-positioning against the śrāvaka systems that the Kośa had so carefully mapped.

Modern scholarship:

There was a mid-20th-century theory (especially associated with Erich Frauwallner) that there were two different Vasubandhus — an earlier Sautrāntika author of the Kośa and a later Yogācāra author of the Mahāyāna works. That “two Vasubandhus” hypothesis has largely fallen out of favor. Most contemporary scholars now accept a single author whose thought developed, seeing clear continuity of vocabulary, style of argument, and philosophical progression from the Kośa through the transitional works into the mature Yogācāra texts (including this one, the Viṃśatikā, Triṃśikā, etc.).

So when we eventually move from the Kośa to this text, we will be reading the same extraordinary mind after it has made the decisive turn into the Mahāyāna. That is exactly why the relationship between the two works is so interesting: you can watch the same analytical precision and structural clarity that we admire in the Kośa now being put in service of a completely different soteriological and ontological vision.

Yes.

There is a reason those two alone receive that kind of attention from us.

Vasubandhu never leaves the ground of rigorous analysis. Even at his most radical — when he dissolves the grasper and the grasped, when he installs the three natures, when he maps the entire path of the Mahāyāna — the architecture remains crystalline, almost architectural. He does not leap into the unconditioned; he constructs the conditions under which the unconditioned can be realized without remainder. That is why the Kośa and the Madhyāntavibhāga feel like successive stages of the same mind: the same precision, the same refusal of vagueness, now turned toward a different horizon.

Śaṅkara moves differently. Once the adhyāsa is established, the rest is almost a continuous disclosure of what was already the case. The transcendental is not constructed; it is uncovered by the removal of superimposition. The tone is different — more absolute, more final, less patient with intermediate structures.

Yet both demand the same posture from the reader: complete attention, because neither wastes a single conceptual move. Both are willing to dismantle every comfortable dualism, and both leave the student with nowhere left to stand except the clarity they have forced into being.

That is why they are the only two we study in full prostration. Everything else can be read with interest or respect. These two require the whole of us.

When you are ready, we can begin placing the Madhyāntavibhāga next to the Kośa and watch the same intelligence at work on the other side of the turn.
