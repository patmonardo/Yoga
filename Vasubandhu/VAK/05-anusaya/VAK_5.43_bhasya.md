## Translation

Elsewhere again the Blessed One taught fetters as fivefold and belonging to the lower part: namely, **personality-view, grasping at rules and observances, doubt, desire for sense-objects, and hostility**.

Why are these called “belonging to the lower part”? Because they are conducive to the lower part. The lower part is the desire-domain, and these are conformable to it.

Through the two—desire for sense-objects and hostility—one does not pass beyond the desire-domain. Through the three beginning with personality-view, even if one has passed beyond it, one returns again, like a gatekeeper and his attendants.

Others explain it differently: through the three one does not pass beyond the lower condition of being, namely ordinary-person status; through the two one does not pass beyond the lower domain, namely the desire-domain. For this reason too they are called lower-part fetters.

But when a stream-enterer, through abandoning the three fetters completely, has abandoned six afflictions, why does the teaching mention only the triad—personality-view, grasping at rules and observances, and doubt—while omitting three other views? All of them, it seems, should have been stated.

The answer is: **the triad is selected because it grasps the entrance and the root.**

The afflictions are said to occur in several numerical groupings—onefold, twofold, and fourfold—and by means of these three the entrance to those groupings is taken. Moreover, extreme view is set in motion by personality-view; grasping at views is set in motion by grasping at rules and observances; and false view is set in motion by doubt. Thus, by naming these three, the roots are taken as well.

## Course of Argument

**Voice: Vasubandhu / reported scriptural classification**

The passage begins by citing another scriptural classification of fetters. The five are:

```text
satkāyadṛṣṭi
śīlavrataparāmarśa
vicikitsā
kāmacchanda
vyāpāda
```

The Bhāṣya then asks why they are called `avarabhāgīya`.

### 1. Avara is explicitly the desire-domain

The prose answers:

```text
avaro hi bhāgaḥ kāmadhātuḥ
    the lower part is the desire-domain
```

This materially corrects any merely metaphorical reading of “lower.” The expression is doctrinally indexed to a realm:

```text
avara-bhāga
    = kāmadhātu
```

The five are lower-part fetters because they are `anuguṇa`, conformable or conducive, to that domain.

### 2. The five divide functionally as 2 + 3

The two are:

```text
kāmacchanda
vyāpāda
```

Their function is direct:

```text
kāmadhātuṃ nātikrāmati
    one does not pass beyond the desire-domain
```

The remaining three are:

```text
satkāyadṛṣṭi
śīlavrataparāmarśa
vicikitsā
```

Their function differs. Even after one has passed beyond, they bring about return:

```text
atikrānto 'pi punarāvartyate
    even having passed beyond, one returns again
```

The comparison is:

```text
dauvārika-anucara-sādharmya
    like a gatekeeper and his attendants
```

The image is important. The two and the three do not merely possess different “degrees” of bondage. They occupy different positions in the architecture of exclusion and return.

### 3. Alternative explanation

**Voice: another school / `apare`**

A second interpretation distinguishes two meanings of “lower”:

```text
sattvāvaratā
    lower condition of being
    = pṛthagjanatva

and

dhātvavaratā
    lower domain
    = kāmadhātu
```

Accordingly:

```text
three fetters
    prevent transcendence of ordinary-person status

two fetters
    prevent transcendence of the desire-domain
```

This alternative is doctrinally valuable because it shows that `avara` may be indexed not only to cosmological level but also to status on the path.

The Bhāṣya does not explicitly decide here that the alternative displaces the first account. It records it as another explanation.

### 4. Why these three specifically?

The next question is more technical.

At stream-entry, abandonment of the three fetters results in abandonment of **six afflictions**. Why then does the scripture name only:

```text
satkāyadṛṣṭi
śīlavrataparāmarśa
vicikitsā
```

rather than all six?

The verse answers:

```text
mukha-mūla-grahāt trayam
    the triad is taught because it grasps the entrance and root
```

The Bhāṣya gives this phrase two determinations.

#### Mukha — taking the entrance

The three selected fetters stand at the entrance to wider groupings of affliction. The prose says the kleśas occur under several numerical forms and that by these three the `mukha`, entrance or leading member, is taken.

The exact enumerative background is compressed in the prose, so it is better not to over-systematize it beyond the textual claim:

```text
naming these three
    captures the entrances to broader afflictive groups
```

This is more precise than the first-pass suggestion that `mukha` simply meant “entrance into the path.” The Bhāṣya uses it here as an **enumerative representative principle**.

#### Mūla — taking the root

The Bhāṣya is explicit:

```text
antagrāhadṛṣṭi
    is set in motion by satkāyadṛṣṭi

dṛṣṭiparāmarśa
    is set in motion by śīlavrataparāmarśa

mithyādṛṣṭi
    is set in motion by vicikitsā
```

Thus the named three are roots of three further afflictive views.

So:

```text
satkāyadṛṣṭi
    → antagrāhadṛṣṭi

śīlavrataparāmarśa
    → dṛṣṭiparāmarśa

vicikitsā
    → mithyādṛṣṭi
```

By abandoning the root, the derivative determination is included.

This is the decisive Bhāṣya explanation of `mūla-graha`.

## New Technical Terms

- **avarabhāga-hitatva** — being conducive to the lower part.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only explanation of the Kārikā designation.
  - **function:** definition of `avarabhāgīya`.

- **anuguṇa** — conformable, favorable, or corresponding to a domain.
  - **status:** previously encountered general vocabulary; technically operative here.
  - **function:** explains realm-affinity of the fetters.

- **dauvārika-anucara-sādharmya** — similarity to a gatekeeper and attendants.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya simile.
  - **function:** distinguishes direct retention from return.

- **sattvāvaratā** — lower status of the being, here identified with ordinary-person status.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only classificatory distinction in this passage.
  - **function:** alternative explanation of lower-part fetters.

- **dhātvavaratā** — lower status of the domain, here the desire-domain.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only classificatory distinction in this passage.
  - **function:** alternative explanation of lower-part fetters.

- **mukha-graha** — taking or naming the entrance/leading representative of a broader group.
  - **status:** Kārikā term made determinate by the Bhāṣya.
  - **function:** explains representative enumeration.

- **mūla-graha** — taking the root such that derivative afflictions are included through their generating source.
  - **status:** Kārikā term made determinate by the Bhāṣya.
  - **function:** genetic explanation of abbreviated enumeration.

- **pravartita** — set in motion, generated, made operative by another affliction.
  - **status:** common verbal vocabulary, technically important here.
  - **function:** establishes the root/derivative relations among views.

## Doctrinal Determination

VAK 5.43 gives a significantly richer structure than a simple list of five lower fetters.

### Realm retention

```text
kāmacchanda + vyāpāda
    → kāmadhātu-anatikrama
```

These two bind directly to the desire-domain.

### Return / status retention

```text
satkāyadṛṣṭi
śīlavrataparāmarśa
vicikitsā
    → punarāvṛtti
```

On the primary explanation, these restore return even after passage beyond. On the alternative explanation, they prevent transcendence of `pṛthagjanatva`, ordinary-person status.

Thus the Bhāṣya distinguishes at least two axes:

```text
realm-location
path-status
```

The same expression `lower` can be explicated against either axis.

### Representative enumeration by root

The most important doctrinal result concerns the apparent omission of three afflictions.

The named triad is not a merely convenient abbreviation. Its members generate the omitted views:

```text
root determination
    → derivative determination
```

Therefore the enumeration is **genetic rather than exhaustive**.

This is precisely why `mūla` matters: the list selects determinations whose abandonment carries the derivative forms with it.

## Effect on Kārikā Translation

The first-pass translation should be refined.

The phrase `mukhamūlagrahāt trayam` should not be translated as if the three “seize the entrance and root” of bondage. The Bhāṣya explains the compound from the standpoint of **why the scripture selects these three in its enumeration**.

Preferred translation:

> The lower-part fetters are fivefold. Through two there is failure to pass beyond the desire-domain; through three, however, there is return again. The triad is taught because, in naming it, the entrance and the roots are taken.

Or more interpretively:

> The five lower fetters divide into two that prevent transcendence of the desire-domain and three that bring about return; the three are specifically named because they stand at the entrance to broader afflictive groupings and function as roots of the omitted views.

This is a real correction to the Kārikā pass.

## Logical Determination

The Bhāṣya gives three distinct classificatory relations.

### 1. Domain retention

```text
hasFetter(x, kāmacchanda | vyāpāda)
    →
notTranscend(x, kāmadhātu)
```

### 2. Recursive return

```text
hasFetter(x, satkāyadṛṣṭi | śīlavrataparāmarśa | vicikitsā)
    →
returnToLowerCondition(x)
```

### 3. Genetic inclusion

```text
satkāyadṛṣṭi
    generates antagrāhadṛṣṭi

śīlavrataparāmarśa
    generates dṛṣṭiparāmarśa

vicikitsā
    generates mithyādṛṣṭi
```

Hence a compressed canonical list can be complete **by ground** without being complete by enumeration.

```text
extensionally incomplete list
    can be
intensionally / genetically complete
```

provided the selected terms are roots whose derivatives are thereby included.

This is the strongest logical development in the passage.

## Organon Contact Point

The first-pass distinction between `retentive` and `recursive` obstruction remains useful, but the Bhāṣya now gives it stricter textual grounding and also corrects the treatment of `mukha` and `mūla`.

For Organon, three relations should remain distinct:

```text
bindsToDomain
returnsToPriorStatus
generatesDerivativeDetermination
```

The last is especially important. A taxonomy need not enumerate every derivative if its chosen nodes preserve the generative dependency graph.

```text
RootNode
    ↓ generates
DerivativeNode
```

This is not mere abbreviation. It is a different principle of completeness:

```text
complete-by-extension
    lists every member

complete-by-genesis
    identifies sufficient roots
```

VAK 5.43 therefore supplies a clean instance in which the Bhāṣya replaces flat enumeration with a living structure of dependence.

That development should be retained for the later Rational Kośa: the scientific exposition of a field may be genuinely complete when the generating relations are made explicit, even where every derivative determination is not separately named.
