Existence in all times is maintained because it has been stated, because of the twofold condition, because cognition has an existent object, and because of fruition. Those who affirm that all exists are called Sarvāstivādins; four forms are admitted.

## Literal Prose Translation

What, then, is this past and future that is spoken of: does it exist or not?

If it exists, then because there is existence in all times, permanence of conditioned formations would follow. If it does not exist, how could one be connected with it or disconnected from it?

The Vaibhāṣikas do not maintain the permanence of conditioned formations, because they possess the marks of the conditioned. But they distinctly maintain existence in all times.

Why?

Because it has been stated. The Blessed One said, in effect: if past form did not exist, the learned noble disciple could not become indifferent toward past form; because past form exists, he becomes indifferent toward it. Likewise, if future form did not exist, he could not refrain from delighting in future form; because future form exists, he does so.

Because of the two. It has been said that vijñāna arises dependent upon two. What two? Eye and forms, and so on through mind and dharmas. If past and future did not exist, vijñāna having them as its object-support could not arise dependent upon two.

Thus past and future existence is established first from scripture; it is also established by reasoning.

Because cognition has an existent object. Vijñāna operates when there is an object, not when there is none. If past and future did not exist, vijñāna would have a nonexistent object-support; consequently vijñāna itself would not occur because of the absence of an object-support.

Because of fruition. If the past did not exist, how could wholesome and unwholesome karma produce a future fruit? At the time when the fruit arises, the maturation-cause is not then present.

Therefore, say the Vaibhāṣikas, past and future certainly exist.

Those who say that all exists—past, future, and present—are admitted as Sarvāstivādins. Those who distinguish, saying that the present exists and that past karma which has not yet given its fruit exists, while past karma which has already given its fruit and the future do not exist, are Vibhajyavādins. The Sarvāstivādins themselves are fourfold.

## Philosophical Translation

The Bhāṣya reaches the ontological question from the practical machinery of bondage. Past and future afflictions have just been said to connect and disconnect a continuum from objects. This immediately raises a dilemma: if past and future are simply nonexistent, those relations lose their terms; but if all three times exist in exactly the same manner, conditioned dharmas seem to become eternal.

The Vaibhāṣika position attempts to hold both determinations together. Conditioned dharmas remain conditioned and therefore marked by origination, duration, decay, and impermanence; nevertheless, past and future are not sheer nonbeing. Their sufficient existence is required by scripture, cognition, object-reference, and karmic fruition.

The doctrine `sarvam asti` is therefore not introduced as a thesis of timeless substance. It is an attempt to preserve determinate temporal efficacy without collapsing past and future into nothing.

## Course of Argument

### 1. The dilemma generated by 5.23–5.24

The Bhāṣya asks explicitly:

```text
atīta-anāgata
    asti?
    na asti?
```

Both simple answers create difficulty:

```text
if asti
    → śāśvatatva seems to follow

if na asti
    → saṃyoga / visaṃyoga becomes unintelligible
```

The Vaibhāṣika therefore rejects the inference:

```text
exists across the three times
    ≠
is permanent
```

because conditioned dharmas still possess `saṃskṛta-lakṣaṇa`.

### 2. First ground: `uktatvāt` — because it has been stated

The first proof is scriptural.

The cited teaching argues from practical relation to temporal existence:

```text
indifference toward past form
    presupposes past form

non-delight in future form
    presupposes future form
```

The point is not merely grammatical reference to past and future. The noble disciple adopts a determinate practical stance toward them.

### 3. Second ground: `dvayāt` — because vijñāna arises from two

The Bhāṣya removes the ambiguity left in the Kārikā pass. `dvaya` is explicitly:

```text
faculty + object

cakṣus + rūpa
...
manas + dharma
```

Vijñāna arises dependent upon this pair. Past and future dharmas can serve as the object-side of mental vijñāna. If they were absolutely nonexistent, the required pair would collapse.

Thus `dvayāt` is not a second mysterious ontological principle. It is the canonical two-condition structure of cognition.

### 4. Third ground: `sadviṣayāt` — because vijñāna has an existent object

The Bhāṣya then makes the epistemological thesis explicit:

```text
sati viṣaye vijñānaṃ pravartate
nāsati
```

Vijñāna operates when there is an object; not when there is none.

Hence cognition of past and future cannot be cognition whose object is sheer nonbeing. Otherwise the very notion of object-supported vijñāna would be destroyed.

This argument is stronger than merely saying that we can think *about* the past and future. It says that the Kośa's theory of vijñāna requires a real `ālambana`.

### 5. Fourth ground: `phalāt` — because of fruit

The causal argument concerns karma.

```text
past karma
    → future vipāka
```

When the fruit comes into existence, the karmic maturation-cause is no longer present. If the past were sheer nonbeing, the Bhāṣya asks how a future fruit could arise from the earlier wholesome or unwholesome karma.

Thus the theory needs a mode in which past karma remains sufficiently real for causal efficacy without remaining presently active.

### 6. Sarvāstivādin versus Vibhajyavādin

Vasubandhu now gives an operational doctrinal criterion.

```text
Sarvāstivādin
    past exists
    present exists
    future exists

Vibhajyavādin
    present exists
    past karma not yet fruited exists
    past karma already fruited does not
    future does not
```

This is important: `Sarvāstivāda` is defined here by a temporal existence claim, not by every doctrine historically associated with the school.

The verse then announces four distinct Sarvāstivāda explanations, developed in 5.26.

## Voice Identification

**Voice: Vasubandhu** — frames the dilemma and organizes the proofs and school distinctions.

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika position** — past and future exist, but conditioned formations are not eternal because conditioned marks remain applicable.

**Voice: scriptural authority** — cited as the first ground for past/future existence.

**Voice: Vibhajyavādin position** — reported as a competing temporal ontology: present plus unfruited past karma exist; future and exhausted past karma do not.

## New Technical Terms

- **sarvakālāstitā** — existence in all times; **status:** central Bhāṣya doctrinal expression; **function:** common thesis whose internal interpretations are about to be distinguished.
- **saṃskṛtalakṣaṇa** — marks of the conditioned; **status:** previously encountered doctrine; **function:** blocks the inference from three-time existence to permanence.
- **dvaya** — the pair of faculty and object; **status:** Bhāṣya clarification of compressed Kārikā term; **function:** canonical condition for the arising of vijñāna.
- **sadviṣaya** — having an existent object; **status:** central epistemological determination; **function:** argues that cognition cannot operate upon sheer nonbeing.
- **vipākahetu** — maturation-cause; **status:** previously encountered karmic term; **function:** grounds the causal argument from past karma to future fruit.
- **sarvāstivādin** — one who asserts that past, future, and present all exist; **status:** school designation; **function:** defines the doctrinal class under investigation.
- **vibhajyavādin** — one who makes a discriminating temporal assertion rather than `sarvam asti`; **status:** school designation introduced here; **function:** contrast class for the Sarvāstivāda thesis.

## Doctrinal Determination

The Bhāṣya's central distinction is:

```text
three-time existence
    ≠
unchanging persistence
```

The Vaibhāṣika must therefore defend both:

```text
past and future are not sheer nonbeing
and
conditioned dharmas remain impermanent
```

This makes temporal mode itself the philosophical problem. The question is no longer merely whether dharmas exist, but:

```text
what changes when a dharma is
future → present → past
if its dravya is not reduced to nothing?
```

That question produces the four theories announced at the end of the verse.

The four proofs also fall into two explicit classes in the prose:

```text
āgama
    uktatvāt
    dvayāt

yukti
    sadviṣayāt
    phalāt
```

So Vasubandhu presents the Vaibhāṣika position as supported first by scriptural testimony and then by reasoning.

## Effect on Kārikā Translation

**Clarification required.**

The first-pass translation left `dvayāt` deliberately compressed. The Bhāṣya resolves it decisively:

```text
dvaya
    = faculty and object
    = the two conditions upon which vijñāna arises
```

A better translation is therefore:

> Existence in all times is maintained because it has been stated, because cognition arises dependent upon the two [faculty and object], because cognition has an existent object, and because of fruition. From asserting that all exists, one is called a Sarvāstivādin; four forms are admitted.

No other canonical vocabulary change is required.

## Logical Determination

5.25 is a reflection upon the presuppositions of the preceding relational theory.

```text
5.23–5.24
    past/future anuśaya can bind

reflection:
    what must be true for that relation to be possible?

5.25
    past/future must possess sufficient being
```

But this immediately produces contradiction:

```text
existence across time
    appears to imply permanence

nonexistence outside the present
    destroys relation, cognition, and fruition
```

The Vaibhāṣika thesis occupies the middle determination:

```text
real enough for relation
real enough for cognition
real enough for fruition
but still conditioned and temporally differentiated
```

The problem is therefore not solved by the word `asti`. It is displaced into the determination of temporal difference. Verse 5.26 supplies four competing principles for that difference.

## Light Organon Contact Point

The strongest transferable distinction is between **existence**, **present activation**, and **causal efficacy**.

A determination need not be presently active in order to remain:

```text
referable
causally relevant
normatively binding
```

But treating that persistence as simple immutable identity would be equally mistaken.

For an agent architecture this suggests separate predicates such as:

```text
existsAsDetermination
isPresentlyActive
remainsCausallyRelevant
hasProducedResult
```

The Kośa's problem is precisely how these can vary without turning one temporal determination either into eternal substance or into sheer deletion.

## Bhāṣya Pass Result

VAK 5.25 is the explicit ontological foundation of the temporal binding analysis of 5.23–5.24. Vasubandhu presents the Vaibhāṣika thesis through a dilemma and four arguments:

```text
1. uktatvāt
   scriptural declaration

2. dvayāt
   vijñāna depends upon faculty + object

3. sadviṣayāt
   vijñāna requires an existent object

4. phalāt
   past karma must support future fruition
```

The critical qualification is equally explicit: the Vaibhāṣikas do **not** infer permanence from three-time existence, because dharmas retain the marks of the conditioned. Thus the real question becomes how temporal difference is constituted within a doctrine of universal existence.

That is the problem taken up immediately by the four Sarvāstivāda theories of VAK 5.26.
