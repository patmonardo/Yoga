# VAK_5.27 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

**IAST**

> kiṃ vighnaṃ tat kathaṃ nānyad adhvāyogas tathā sataḥ |
> ajātanaṣṭatā kena gambhīrā khalu dharmatā || 5.27 ||

**Best current translation**

What obstructs it? How is that not another? How is temporal assignment possible for what exists in that way? By what is it unborn and destroyed? Indeed, the nature of dharmas is profound.

## Literal Prose Translation

If past and future dharmas exist substantially, why are they called past and future? The reply had been that the temporal courses are determined through operative activity. But then what is the operative activity of a presently functioning eye of the same kind? Giving and receiving a result. Yet past homogeneous causes and the like also give results; therefore operative activity would belong to the past as well, or there would be a half-operation, producing a confusion of temporal characteristics.

Further: if a dharma exists in the very same nature, what prevents it from exercising its activity always? If the answer is that the conditions are incomplete, this does not solve the problem if those conditions too are admitted to exist eternally.

Is operative activity something different from the dharma? If so, what activity makes that activity past, future, or present? If it is none of these and yet exists, it would be unconditioned and eternal. If activity is not different from the dharma, however, the same dharma existing in the same nature cannot be assigned now to one temporal course and now to another merely by appealing to activity.

The Vaibhāṣika replies: a dharma not yet arisen is future, one arisen but not destroyed is present, and one destroyed is past. But this is precisely what requires explanation. If past and future exist substantially just as the present does, what makes the future dharma "not yet arisen" and the past dharma "destroyed"? What was previously absent, such that it may be called unborn? What is later absent, such that it may be called destroyed? Without accepting that what was not becomes, and what has become again ceases to be, the three times cannot be established.

Vasubandhu therefore says that merely asserting conditioned characteristics while denying real arising and destruction is verbal only. To say that the dharma is eternally existent but not eternal is an unprecedented manner of speaking.

He then states his alternative interpretation of scriptural claims that the past and future "exist." We too say that the past and future exist: past means what was formerly; future means what will occur when its cause is present. They are said to exist in this sense, not substantially (`na tu punar dravyataḥ`). Scripture states their existence in order to establish the former reality of the cause and the futurity of the fruit and thereby reject nihilistic denial of cause and result.

Likewise, past and future can function as objects of cognition without possessing present substantial existence. When one remembers a past form or feeling, one does not apprehend "it exists," but "it was." Future cognition takes something as what will be. If such an object existed in exactly the same way as a present object, it would be present. Therefore cognition can have both being and non-being as object.

Nor do the Sautrāntikas explain a result as arising directly from a substantially existent past karma. The result arises from a `saṃtāna-viśeṣa`, a special modification or determination of the continuum produced by that prior karma.

Accordingly, Vasubandhu rejects as unacceptable within the teaching the thesis that past and future exist substantially. A defensible `sarvam asti` is the one stated in scripture, notably with reference to the twelve spheres, not an eternal inventory of dharmas across three times.

## Philosophical Translation

The Sarvāstivāda solution fails because it tries to derive genuine temporal difference from an invariant substantial dharma without giving a genuinely genetic middle term. If `kāritra` differs from the dharma, the temporal status of `kāritra` itself must be explained; if it does not differ, it cannot explain how one and the same intrinsically existent dharma becomes future, present, and past. The doctrine therefore either multiplies unexplained temporal entities or merely redescribes the original problem.

Vasubandhu's alternative is not the crude assertion that past and future are nothing. Rather, temporal predicates are grounded in genesis and cessation: the past is what has occurred; the future is what will occur when its cause is present. Their "existence" is therefore not `dravyataḥ`, as an independently subsisting thing analogous to the present. Their reality is preserved through causal continuity, recollection, anticipation, and the transformation of the continuum.

The decisive replacement is thus:

```text
substantial persistence of the same past dharma
    ↓ rejected

causally determined transformation of the continuum
    ↓
saṃtāna-viśeṣa
```

Temporal efficacy does not require a past entity to remain numerically present. It requires the past occurrence to have transformed the continuum in a way from which a later fruit can arise.

## Course of Argument

### 1. The preferred Vaibhāṣika theory is tested from its own criterion

**Voice: Vasubandhu / Objector against Vaibhāṣika**

The third theory of 5.26 made `kāritra` the criterion of temporal state. Vasubandhu asks what actually prevents an eternally existent dharma from exercising `kāritra` eternally.

```text
same svabhāva always exists
    +
kāritra belongs to it
    →
why only occasional operation?
```

The appeal to incomplete conditions does not help if those conditions are themselves eternally existent.

### 2. The distinction between dharma and kāritra collapses

**Voice: Vasubandhu / Objector**

If activity is different from the dharma, activity requires its own activity to explain its temporal status. This launches a regress or makes activity itself timeless.

If activity is not different from the dharma, it cannot perform the mediating work required of it:

```text
Dharma = Kāritra
    →
no explanatory difference between
existent dharma and temporal operation
```

This is the central dialectical failure.

### 3. `ajāta` and `naṣṭa` force genuine arising and cessation

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

The Vaibhāṣika uses the ordinary temporal definitions:

```text
ajāta
    future

jāta, avinaṣṭa
    present

vinaṣṭa
    past
```

But if all three exist in exactly the same substantial manner, those predicates lose their ground. Something must genuinely be absent before arising and absent after cessation if `ajāta` and `naṣṭa` are to mean anything.

Hence:

```text
abhūtvā bhavati
    what was not becomes

bhūtvā punar na bhavati
    what has become again ceases to be
```

must be admitted in some proper sense.

### 4. Vasubandhu reinterprets `asti` rather than simply denying past and future

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

This is the constructive center of the passage:

```text
atīta
    yad bhūtapūrvam
    what formerly was

anāgata
    yat sati hetau bhaviṣyati
    what will occur when its cause is present
```

They are said to exist in this way:

```text
na tu punar dravyataḥ
    but not substantially
```

This preserves causal truth without converting temporal relations into substances.

### 5. Cognition does not prove substantial existence of every object

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

Past recollection cognizes something as **having been**; anticipation cognizes something as **to be**. Their intentional object need not therefore exist in the same way as a present thing.

The argument culminates in:

```text
ubhayaṃ vijñānasyālambanam
    bhāvaś ca abhāvaś ca

both are objects of knowledge:
being and non-being
```

Thus `sadviṣayatva` cannot establish Sarvāstivāda substantialism.

### 6. Causal continuity replaces a persistent past karma

**Voice: Sautrāntika position, adopted here by Vasubandhu**

The Sautrāntika does not say:

```text
past karma still substantially exists
    → later fruit
```

but:

```text
past karma
    → transformation of continuum
    → saṃtāna-viśeṣa
    → later fruit
```

This is the genuinely positive alternative supplied by the Bhāṣya.

### 7. Restricted rehabilitation of `sarvam asti`

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

The unacceptable thesis is:

```text
past and future exist dravyataḥ
```

The acceptable statement is scriptural and contextual:

```text
sarvam asti
    insofar as the teaching itself determines the domain,
    e.g. the twelve spheres
```

Thus Vasubandhu does not simply invert Sarvāstivāda into "nothing exists except the present." He reconstructs the senses in which past and future discourse is legitimate.

### 8. Vaibhāṣika final reply

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika position**

The Vaibhāṣikas persist: past and future do indeed exist.

Where the reasoning cannot carry one further, they invoke the profundity of the dharma-nature:

```text
gambhīrā khalu dharmatā
```

Vasubandhu immediately resists treating profundity as equivalent to being beyond rational establishment.

## Voice Identification

- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — sustained critique of substantial three-time existence; reinterpretation of `asti`; defense of causal continuity and object-reference without dravya-persistence.
- **Voice: Vaibhāṣika position** — temporal distinction through `kāritra`; insistence that past and future really exist.
- **Voice: Sautrāntika position** — result arises from a `saṃtāna-viśeṣa` produced by prior karma rather than from a presently existing past karma.
- **Voice: Objector** — the passage repeatedly uses objection-and-reply form; in the central sequence Vasubandhu occupies the objecting position against Vaibhāṣika doctrine.

## New Technical Terms

- **dravyataḥ** — substantially, as a real entity in its own right.
  - **status:** crucial Bhāṣya determination in this debate.
  - **function:** marks precisely what Vasubandhu denies of past and future.

- **bhūtapūrva** — having formerly been.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya clarification.
  - **function:** positive definition of the past.

- **sati hetau bhaviṣyati** — will occur when the cause is present.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya formulation.
  - **function:** positive causal definition of the future.

- **saṃtāna-viśeṣa** — a special determination/transformation of the continuum.
  - **status:** major Bhāṣya/Sautrāntika term.
  - **function:** explains delayed causal fruition without substantial persistence of the past cause.

- **vartamānīkaraṇa** — making present / presentification.
  - **status:** introduced in the dialectic.
  - **function:** proposed Vaibhāṣika explanation of causal efficacy, then criticized.

- **abhūtvā bhavati** — having not been, it becomes.
  - **status:** doctrinally decisive phrase.
  - **function:** genuine arising.

- **bhūtvā punar na bhavati** — having been, it again is not.
  - **status:** doctrinally decisive phrase.
  - **function:** genuine cessation.

## Doctrinal Determination

VAK 5.27 should not be assimilated as merely "Vasubandhu prefers presentism." The Bhāṣya gives a more exact structure.

He rejects:

```text
Past = presently subsisting past substance
Future = presently subsisting future substance
```

But preserves:

```text
Past
    real as having occurred
    causally formative
    recollectable

Future
    real as causally determinable
    anticipatable
    able to arise when its causes obtain
```

Thus:

```text
reality
    ≠
substantial present existence
```

The most important positive doctrine is `saṃtāna-viśeṣa`. A vanished cause can remain efficacious because its occurrence has transformed the continuum. What persists is not the numerically identical past dharma but a genetically altered causal series.

This is the conceptual point that must govern later assimilation.

## Effect on Kārikā Translation

**Clarification only, with major interpretive expansion.**

The first-pass translation correctly captured the four interrogatives. The Bhāṣya shows that the final `gambhīrā khalu dharmatā` occurs after a very long critique and should not be read straightforwardly as Vasubandhu confessing that the Vaibhāṣika theory is simply too profound to explain.

The surrounding prose makes the tone dialectical. The Vaibhāṣika invokes profundity; Vasubandhu's immediate response is effectively that profundity does not entail impossibility of rational establishment.

## Logical Determination

The governing logical issue is **genetic adequacy of the middle term**.

Vasumitra's proposed mediation was:

```text
Dharma
    ↓
Kāritra
    ↓
Temporal State
```

Vasubandhu tests whether `kāritra` genuinely mediates.

If:

```text
Kāritra ≠ Dharma
```

then kāritra requires a further account of its own temporality.

If:

```text
Kāritra = Dharma
```

then it merely repeats the original term and cannot generate temporal difference.

Therefore the mediation fails.

Vasubandhu's own replacement is genetic rather than substratal:

```text
Prior Event
    → transforms continuum
    → determinate causal disposition
    → later arising
```

This is a much stronger structure because the later state is explained as generated from the earlier event through a transformed continuum.

## Light Organon Contact Point

This passage deserves special retention for later assimilation.

The cleanest contact point is not "Buddhism = becoming" or "Sarvāstivāda = substance." It is the methodological standard Vasubandhu applies:

```text
A proposed mediator must generate the distinction it explains.
```

And the positive replacement:

```text
persistence of causal determination
    need not mean
persistence of the numerically identical cause
```

For an agent architecture this distinguishes:

```text
historical event
    from
persistent transformed state
```

A past event can disappear as event while leaving the system genetically altered. That altered continuum is the bearer of later efficacy.

This is far more useful than modeling the past as an eternally retained object with a changed temporal label.

## Bhāṣya Pass Result

VAK 5.27 is a major doctrinal turning point.

The Bhāṣya:

1. dismantles `kāritra` as a sufficient explanation of Sarvāstivāda temporal distinction;
2. rejects substantial (`dravyataḥ`) existence of past and future;
3. affirms genuine arising and cessation;
4. reinterprets scriptural `asti` contextually rather than substantially;
5. allows cognition to take non-being as object;
6. replaces persistent past karma with causal transformation of the continuum (`saṃtāna-viśeṣa`);
7. preserves the causal and epistemic intelligibility of past and future without converting them into present-like substances.

For later philosophical assimilation, the central formula is:

```text
past efficacy
    ≠ persistent past entity

past efficacy
    = transformation already effected in the continuum
```

That is the strongest positive result of the passage.
