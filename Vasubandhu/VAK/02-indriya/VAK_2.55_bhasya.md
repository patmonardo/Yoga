# VAK 2.55 Bhāṣya — The Unconditioned and the Limits of Cause–Result

## Kārikā

```text
sarvatragaḥ sabhāgaś ca dvayadhvagau tryadhvagās trayaḥ /
saṃskṛtaṃ savisaṃyogaṃ phalaṃ nāsaṃskṛtasya te // VAK 2.55 //
```

## Continuous Translation

The pervasive cause and the homogeneous cause belong only to two times, the past and the present; they do not exist in the future. Three causes—the co-arisen, associated, and maturation causes—belong to all three times. The conditioning cause is not confined by time and must be understood both as extending through all times and as dissociated from temporal limitation.

The fruits of these causes are conditioned dharmas together with disconnection. They are not the fruits of the unconditioned.

Why is that? Because none of the six causes can apply to the unconditioned, and none of the five fruits can belong to it.

Why is the Path not accepted as the conditioning cause of disconnection? Because the conditioning cause was defined through non-obstruction with respect to arising, whereas the unconditioned does not arise. How, then, is disconnection called a fruit of the Path? Because it is obtained through the power of the Path.

Would it not follow that only the acquisition of disconnection is the fruit of the Path? No. The Path has one efficacy with respect to acquisition and another with respect to disconnection. It produces the acquisition; it reveals or brings about the disconnection. Thus the Path is not a cause of disconnection in the same sense in which conditioned causes produce conditioned dharmas, yet disconnection is still called its fruit.

If the unconditioned has no dominant fruit, how can it be a conditioning cause? It can be called a conditioning cause merely through not obstructing arising. But it has no fruit of its own, since what is outside the temporal series is incapable of receiving or giving a fruit.

The Sautrāntikas reply that the Blessed One never directly called the unconditioned a cause. It is called a cause only in a secondary sense. Scripture says that whatever causes and conditions produce form are themselves impermanent; since form arises in dependence upon impermanent causes and conditions, how could form be permanent? The same reasoning applies through consciousness.

An objection follows: would the unconditioned then be excluded even as an object-condition of consciousness? No, because the scriptural statement is restricted to causes and conditions productive of arising. An unconditioned object may still serve as an object-condition without itself producing the cognition in the manner of a generative cause.

What, then, is disconnection? To define it merely as analytical cessation, and analytical cessation merely as disconnection, is circular and fails to illuminate its nature.

The Vaibhāṣika says that its nature is known directly only by noble persons. At most it can be said that there exists another real, eternal, wholesome entity called both disconnection and analytical cessation.

The Sautrāntikas reply that every unconditioned dharma is without independent substance. Space is merely the absence of a tangible obstruction. Analytical cessation is the non-arising of further affliction through the power of discriminative knowledge. Non-analytical cessation is the non-arising of a dharma through the incompleteness of its conditions, without such discrimination.

Some other teachers distinguish the two by saying that wisdom has efficacy with respect to preventing the arising of the latent afflictions, whereas the non-arising of suffering follows because the causal afflictions are absent. Others call the later absence of an arisen dharma, through its own cessation, non-analytical cessation. But this last view threatens to make non-analytical cessation impermanent.

Nor should analytical cessation be thought to arise after discrimination. The non-arising of those future dharmas is not produced as a new entity. Rather, without discrimination those dharmas would arise; when discrimination is present, they no longer arise. The efficacy of discrimination consists in establishing a barrier to the arising of what otherwise lacked such a barrier.

How, then, can the faculties be said to lead to the abandonment of past, future, and present suffering? The abandonment of past and present suffering is stated because the craving attached to those aggregates is abandoned. The past affliction deposited a seed in the continuum for the arising of future affliction. When that seed is destroyed, the past affliction too is said to be abandoned, just as karma is said to be exhausted when its maturation is exhausted. Future suffering is abandoned absolutely because, with no seed remaining, it will never arise.

The Sautrāntika does not say that the unconditioned is simply nonexistent. Rather, it exists in the manner in which one speaks of the prior nonexistence or subsequent nonexistence of a sound. Absence does not thereby become a positive substance. Nevertheless, the complete absence of every affliction may rightly be praised as supreme, in order to incline beings toward it.

Nor does this prevent cessation from being an noble truth. Noble persons directly see both that suffering is suffering and that the absence of suffering is absence. Its truth does not require that absence be converted into a positive substance.

The Vaibhāṣika insists that if the unconditioned were merely absence, cognition taking space or nirvāṇa as its object would have a nonexistent object. The Sautrāntika answers that this problem belongs to the broader question of how cognition can take past and future dharmas as objects.

If the unconditioned is posited as an independent substance, the Vaibhāṣika position is preserved—but only by imagining what cannot be apprehended either through an intrinsic nature like form and feeling or through a function like the faculties. The relation expressed by saying “the cessation of this affliction” is more naturally understood as the simple negation of that affliction than as a separate entity possessing it.

How, then, is nirvāṇa acquired? Through obtaining a support absolutely opposed to the renewed arising of affliction and rebirth by obtaining the counteragent. Nirvāṇa is said to be acquired because the continuum has become incapable of producing those dharmas again.

Scripture itself describes nirvāṇa through abandonment, release, exhaustion, dispassion, cessation, calming, disappearance, non-relinking, non-arising, and non-manifestation. The extinction of a lamp is an absence; likewise the liberation of the mind is spoken of as extinction.

The Vaibhāṣika replies that in Abhidharma the unconditioned is called without causal basis, not without reality. “Basis” has several senses: intrinsic nature, object, conjunction, cause, and possession. Here it means causal basis. Thus the unconditioned may still be substantially real while lacking cause and fruit.

Having completed this dispute, the Bhāṣya begins assigning fruits to causes. The maturation fruit belongs to the maturation cause. The dominant fruit belongs to the conditioning cause. Even a merely non-obstructive cause has dominance in that sense, while some conditioning causes also exercise positive efficacy—for example the sense-fields in relation to the five consciousnesses, karma in relation to the receptacle world, and hearing in relation to the later desire to see.

## Argument Structure

The passage distinguishes three questions that must not be collapsed:

1. Can the unconditioned be called a cause in any sense?
2. Can the unconditioned be produced as a fruit?
3. Can disconnection be called a fruit of the Path without being caused as a conditioned event?

The Bhāṣya answers them differently:

```text
unconditioned as non-obstructive condition:
    possible only in a weak or secondary sense

unconditioned as produced fruit:
    impossible

disconnection as fruit of the Path:
    possible through acquisition and disclosure,
    not through ordinary production
```

This is an exact restriction on the scope of the cause–result relation.

## Voices

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika position**

- The unconditioned is a real, eternal, wholesome entity.
- Disconnection and analytical cessation name that real entity.
- Its nature is directly known by nobles.
- It lacks cause and fruit but remains substantially real.

**Voice: Sautrāntika position**

- The unconditioned is not an independent substance.
- Space is absence of obstruction.
- Analytical cessation is non-arising through discriminative knowledge.
- Non-analytical cessation is non-arising through lack of conditions.
- Nirvāṇa is the absolute absence of renewed affliction and suffering.

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

Vasubandhu clearly presses the Sautrāntika critique. His irony appears when he says that the gods may preserve the Vaibhāṣika thesis if it is worth preserving, but that it remains an imagined entity whose intrinsic nature and function cannot be apprehended.

## New Technical Terms

- **savisaṃyoga-phala** — conditioned fruits together with the special fruit called disconnection
  - **status:** developed here
  - **function:** distinguishes an unproduced liberative result from ordinary conditioned effects

- **prapaṇa** — bringing forth, disclosing, or realizing
  - **status:** rare in the Kārikā material
  - **function:** explains how the Path relates to disconnection without producing it as a conditioned entity

- **pratyātmavedya** — individually or directly known in oneself
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only in this local sequence
  - **function:** Vaibhāṣika appeal to noble realization

- **utpatti-pratibandha** — obstruction or barrier to arising
  - **status:** developed here
  - **function:** Sautrāntika explanation of discriminative cessation

- **avastuka** — without causal basis / without a substantial basis, depending interpretation
  - **status:** doctrinally contested
  - **function:** supports the dispute over whether the unconditioned is unreal or merely causeless

## Translation Consequences

The term **phala** cannot be translated uniformly as a produced “effect.” In this passage it includes at least two structures:

```text
productive result:
    a conditioned event generated by a cause

liberative fruit:
    a state obtained through the Path
    without being produced as a conditioned event
```

Likewise, **asaṃskṛta** must not be rendered as a hidden eternal substance by default. The Bhāṣya deliberately stages a dispute over whether it is:

- a real eternal entity, or
- an absence/non-arising known and attained through transformation of the continuum.

## Doctrinal Determination

This is one of the clearest points at which the Kośa separates **genesis** from **truth-status**.

A conditioned dharma is understood through arising from causes and conditions. The unconditioned, precisely as unconditioned, cannot be another member of that genetic chain. Yet liberation must still be intelligibly related to the Path.

The solution is to distinguish:

```text
Path:
    conditions a transformation of the continuum

acquisition:
    arises as a conditioned event

disconnection:
    is realized as the impossibility of renewed affliction
```

Thus the Path does not manufacture nirvāṇa. It makes the continuum incapable of producing bondage again.

## Limited Organon Interpretation

This passage is decisive for the Rational–Empirical distinction.

The empirical continuum is transformed through conditioned causes, temporal sequence, cultivation, and acquisition. But the truth disclosed by that transformation—absolute non-arising of bondage—is not itself one more empirical event.

```text
Empirical / conditioned:
    Path
    counteragent
    acquisition
    transformed support

Rational / liberative determination:
    the impossibility of renewed arising
    known as disconnection or nirvāṇa
```

This is not a second world behind appearance. It is the **prakāśa** of the conditioned continuum in its completed truth.

The genetic judgment therefore reaches a limit:

> Cause explains how the support is transformed; it does not produce the truth that the transformed support reveals.

That gives us a disciplined formula for the later Organon pass:

```text
Relative necessity:
    conditioned causes transform the continuum

Freedom:
    the transformed continuum no longer contains
    the possibility of renewed bondage

Absolute determination:
    non-arising is known as the truth of liberation,
    not as another conditioned product
```

## Relation to VAK 2.56

VAK 2.55 begins the mapping from causes to fruits:

- maturation cause → maturation fruit
- conditioning cause → dominant fruit

VAK 2.56 continues this assignment with homogeneous, pervasive, co-arisen, and associated causes.
