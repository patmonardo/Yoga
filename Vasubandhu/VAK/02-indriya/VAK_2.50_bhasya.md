# VAK 2.50 Bhāṣya — Co-Arisen Cause

## Kārikā

```text
bhūtavac cittacittānuvartilakṣaṇalakṣyavat // VAkK_2.50 //
```

Like the great elements; like citta and the dharmas that follow citta; like the conditioned marks and what they mark.

## Continuous Translation

The conditioning cause has been explained.

Those dharmas that are mutually one another's results are, reciprocally, co-arisen causes. How so?

The four great elements are mutually co-arisen causes. Citta is a co-arisen cause of the dharmas that follow citta, and those dharmas are likewise causes of citta. The conditioned marks are causes of the conditioned dharma that they mark, and that marked dharma is likewise a cause of them. In this way, every conditioned dharma is, as appropriate, a co-arisen cause.

A qualification must nevertheless be added. A dharma is a co-arisen cause of its secondary marks even without mutual resulthood, but those secondary marks are not causes of that dharma.

What, then, are the dharmas that follow citta?

They are all the mental factors associated with citta, the two restraints, and the conditioned marks belonging to those factors and to citta. The two restraints are the restraint belonging to absorption and the uncontaminated restraint.

Why are these called followers of citta? In brief, because they correspond with it in time, result, maturation, homogeneous outflow, and ethical quality. They arise, remain, and cease together with citta and fall within the same temporal period. They have the same result, maturation, and homogeneous outflow. With a wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate citta, they are respectively wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate.

The Vaibhāṣika analysis therefore treats citta as co-arisen cause of a large complex of accompanying dharmas, and these accompanying dharmas as causes of citta. Yet not every simultaneous relation counts as co-arisen causality. Some co-present formations are excluded because they do not have one result, one maturation, and one homogeneous outflow, or because their relation extends before and after the present arising.

An objection is then raised. In familiar causal examples, such as seed and sprout, cause and result are not simultaneous. How can dharmas produced at the same moment stand to one another as cause and result?

The Vaibhāṣika offers the examples of lamp and light, and sprout and shadow. But it must first be considered whether the lamp is really the cause of the light, or whether an earlier causal complex produces a lamp together with its light; likewise, whether an earlier complex produces a sprout together with its shadow.

The defenders of co-arisen causality appeal to regular concomitance: when one member is present, all the others are present; when one is absent, all the others are absent. Since this is taken as the mark of cause and what possesses a cause, simultaneous dharmas may stand in a causal relation.

The difficulty, however, remains sharper when mutual causality is asserted. If inseparable co-presence is enough, then derived matter inseparable from the great elements, secondary marks inseparable from citta, and similar complexes should also count as mutually causal.

The Vaibhāṣika therefore gives the analogy of three staffs standing by one another's strength. Co-arisen dharmas are said to support one another in this way.

But this too requires examination. Do the staffs remain standing through a force belonging to their simultaneous relation, or do they stand as they do because of a prior causal arrangement? Moreover, in the actual example there may also be a cord, a peg, or the earth serving as support.

The Bhāṣya thus records the Vaibhāṣika doctrine of co-arisen cause while preserving serious doubt about whether simultaneity and inseparability are sufficient to establish genuine causal efficacy.

## Argument Reconstruction

The Vaibhāṣika doctrine begins from a strong thesis:

```text
mutually dependent co-presence
    can constitute a causal relation
```

Its principal examples are:

```text
the four great elements
citta and its associated followers
conditioned dharma and its marks
```

The doctrine is not merely that these items happen together. It claims that they are mutually productive or mutually sustaining.

The Bhāṣya then applies a genetic test:

```text
Does the simultaneous item truly produce or sustain its partner?
Or does an earlier causal complex produce both together?
```

If the latter is true, co-presence is evidence of common genesis, not necessarily mutual causation.

This is the central problem of the passage.

## Voices

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika position**  
Dharmas that are mutually one another's results are co-arisen causes. The great elements, citta and its followers, and conditioned dharmas with their marks are central cases.

**Voice: Objector**  
Ordinary causes such as seed and sprout operate successively. How can simultaneous dharmas be cause and result?

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika position**  
Lamp and light, sprout and shadow, and three mutually supporting staffs show that simultaneous causal relations are possible.

**Voice: Vasubandhu**  
These examples do not settle the issue. An earlier complex may produce both members together, and the staff analogy may conceal additional supporting conditions.

## New Technical Terms

- **sahabhūhetu** — co-arisen cause; a cause constituted by simultaneous relation or mutual co-functioning.
  - **status:** introduced as one of the six causes in VAK 2.49 and analyzed here.
  - **function:** explains causal dependence among dharmas present in one event-complex.

- **mithaḥphala** — mutually one another's results.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya specification of co-arisen causality.
  - **function:** defines the strongest form of reciprocal causal relation.

- **cittānuvartin** — follower of citta.
  - **status:** technically developed in this passage.
  - **function:** identifies dharmas that track citta in time, result, maturation, outflow, and ethical status.

- **anulakṣaṇa** — secondary mark or mark-of-the-mark.
  - **status:** previously encountered in the conditioned-mark debate.
  - **function:** tests the limits of reciprocal causality, since the marked dharma may cause its secondary marks without reciprocity.

- **bhāvābhāvaniyama** — regularity of presence and absence.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya causal criterion.
  - **function:** offered as a mark of cause and caused, though not accepted without qualification.

## Translation Consequences

**Sahabhū** should not be reduced to “simultaneous.” The doctrinal claim is causal and relational: the members of one complex are said to support or condition one another in their very co-arising.

**Cittānuvartin** is better translated as “follower of citta” than merely “mental concomitant,” because the passage defines following across several indexed dimensions: time, result, maturation, homogeneous outflow, and ethical quality.

The staff analogy should not be treated as Vasubandhu's conclusion. He reports it and immediately subjects it to criticism.

## Doctrinal Determination

This passage distinguishes three possible readings of simultaneity:

```text
mere coexistence
common production by prior conditions
mutual causal support
```

The Vaibhāṣika affirms the third for selected complexes. Vasubandhu presses the second as a more economical explanation.

The discussion therefore refines the sixfold causal system at its most difficult point: causal relation need not always be temporally successive, but the mere absence of temporal succession does not by itself prove reciprocity.

## Limited Organon Interpretation

This verse is highly relevant to the Rational–Empirical distinction.

Empirically, citta and its factors appear as one simultaneous event-complex. Rationally, the question is what relation grounds that unity.

Two explanatory models are available:

```text
Model A — reciprocal immanence
    the members sustain one another

Model B — common genesis
    an antecedent ground produces the members together
```

The Bhāṣya does not permit us to infer Model A merely from empirical inseparability. Rational illumination—**prakāśa**—must determine whether the unity is genuinely reciprocal or only jointly conditioned.

This yields an important Organon rule:

> Co-presence is empirical evidence; causal form requires genetic determination.

That is exactly the point at which Rational Nature functions within relative reality in a dual way. It does not replace the empirical complex. It illuminates whether the relation appearing within that complex is coexistence, common derivation, or true reciprocity.

## Provisional Summary

VAK 2.50 defines co-arisen cause through mutual resulthood and applies it to the great elements, citta and its followers, and conditioned dharmas with their marks. Yet the Bhāṣya refuses to let simultaneity serve as an automatic proof of causality. Lamp and light, sprout and shadow, and mutually supporting staffs may all be explained by prior conditions that generate the complex together.

The passage therefore preserves the Vaibhāṣika architecture while exposing its central unresolved problem:

```text
simultaneous dependence
    is not yet demonstrably
mutual causal production
```
