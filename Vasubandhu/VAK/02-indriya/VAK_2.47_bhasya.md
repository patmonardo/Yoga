# VAK 2.47 Bhāṣya — Names, Sentences, Syllables, and the Reality of Linguistic Form

## Kārikā

```text
nāmakāyādayaḥ saṃjñāvākyākṣarasamuktayaḥ /
kāmarūpāptasattvākhyā niḥṣyandāvyākṛtās tathā // VAK 2.47 //
```

## Continuous Translation

What are the name-groups and the rest?

“Name-groups and the rest are collections of designations, sentences, and syllables.”

By the expression “and the rest,” sentence-groups and syllable-groups are included.

A name is the making of a designation, as in “form,” “sound,” and so forth.

A sentence is a statement extending as far as the completion of a meaning, as in “Conditioned things are indeed impermanent.” Through it, specific relations of action, quality, time, and the like are understood.

A syllable, or phonetic element, is such as *a*, *ā*, and so forth.

Objection: Are not letters merely names for the parts of written script?

Reply: No. Written signs were not devised in order to communicate parts of writing; rather, the parts of writing were devised in order to communicate syllables. Otherwise, how could sounds that are not heard be understood through writing? Therefore syllables are not names for written signs.

The collections of these designations and the rest are the name-groups and the other groups. The verbal root *uc* is taught in the sense of coming together; its nominal form is *samukti*, collection. What is meant by “aggregation” is meant here by “collection.”

A name-group is, for example, “form, sound, smell, taste, and tangible.” A sentence-group is, for example, “All conditioned things are impermanent; all dharmas are without self; nirvāṇa is peaceful.” A syllable-group is, for example, “ka, kha, ga, gha, ṅa,” and so forth.

Objection: Since these are by nature speech and therefore consist of sound, they must belong to material form. Why then are they called formations dissociated from mind?

Reply: They are not themselves speech. Speech is audible sound, but meanings are not understood from sound alone.

What, then, is speech?

Speech operates with respect to a name, and a name reveals a meaning. Not every mere sound is speech. The sound through which a meaning is understood is speech.

By what sort of sound is a meaning understood?

By one whose application to meanings has been fixed by speakers. Thus the sound “go” is conventionally fixed in several different senses.

But even one who holds that a name reveals a meaning must admit that the linguistic sound has an established application to a particular meaning. Yet this is already accomplished by the meaningful sound itself. Why posit a separate entity called a name?

Nor is it intelligible how speech operates upon a name. Does it produce the name, or does it manifest it?

If speech produces the name, then because speech has the nature of sound, every mere sound should produce a name. Or, if only a specific sound is said to produce the name, that same specific sound can itself reveal the meaning, making the separate name unnecessary.

If speech manifests the name, the same consequence follows. Since speech is sound, every sound should manifest the name; or the specific sound said to manifest the name can itself reveal the meaning.

Moreover, sounds do not possess a simultaneous totality in which all their parts meet for a single moment. Nor can one entity reasonably be produced part by part. How, then, could speech produce a name?

It might be answered that the final moment of verbal expression, depending upon the preceding moments, produces the name, just as the final moment of manifest action produces an unmanifest action.

But then, since the name would be produced only at the final moment, someone who heard merely that final sound should also understand the meaning.

Or it might be imagined that speech produces a syllable and that the syllable produces the name. Yet the same difficulty remains, because the syllables lack simultaneous aggregation.

The same objection applies if speech is held to manifest the name.

Even persons with refined linguistic intelligence and concentrated attention cannot isolate the syllables of speech by their defining marks with complete precision. Therefore speech cannot reasonably be said either to produce or to manifest syllables as separate entities.

Or suppose that a name arises simultaneously with its meaning, like birth and the other conditioned marks. Then a present name could not apply to a past or future object. Parents also assign names to children as they please; which of these names would be the one co-arising with the child? And with what would the names of unconditioned dharmas arise simultaneously? This theory is therefore untenable.

The Blessed One also said: “A verse rests upon a name, and the poet is the support of verses.” Here “name” means a sound whose range has been fixed with respect to meanings; a verse is a special arrangement formed from such names. Yet a special arrangement does not thereby become a separate substance, just as a row or the ordered succession of thoughts is not a distinct entity apart from its members.

Even if one were to imagine that the phonetic element itself is a distinct substance, name-groups and the other groups would simply be collections of those elements. Positing yet another substance corresponding to the collection would be pointless.

The Vaibhāṣikas reply: name-groups do nevertheless exist substantially as formations dissociated from mind. Not all dharmas are accessible to reasoning.

One must still state their classification. To which realms do name-groups and the rest belong? Are they associated with beings or not associated with beings? Are they maturation-results, products of development, homogeneous outflows? Are they wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate?

The answer is:

“They belong to the desire and form realms, are associated with beings, are homogeneous outflows, and are indeterminate.”

They belong to the desire realm and the form realm. Some maintain that they also exist in the formless realm, but there they are inexpressible.

They are associated with beings. The one who signifies through them possesses them; the one signified does not.

They are homogeneous outflows and unobscured-indeterminate.

## Argument Structure

The passage first defines three levels of linguistic articulation:

```text
name
    designation of an item

sentence
    completion of a meaning through relational articulation

syllable
    minimal phonetic element
```

It then asks whether these require three additional real substances beyond meaningful sound and conventional arrangement.

Vasubandhu's test is genetic and functional:

```text
What produces or manifests the alleged entity?
What explanatory work does it perform?
Can that work already be performed by meaningful sound?
Can its parts coexist in the manner required?
Can it account for past, future, freely assigned, and unconditioned referents?
```

The separate substantial name fails each test.

## Voices

**Voice: Vasubandhu** — defines the linguistic units, presses the production/manifestation dilemma, and argues that meaningful sound plus conventional fixation already explains reference.

**Voice: Objector / Vaibhāṣika** — maintains that name-groups, sentence-groups, and syllable-groups are real formations dissociated from mind.

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika position** — concludes that these entities are real even though their reality cannot be fully established by reasoning: “Not all dharmas are accessible to reasoning.”

**Voice: uncertain** — the view that corresponding entities also exist in the formless realm but are inexpressible is attributed only to “others.”

## Doctrinal Determination

This is not a denial of language, meaning, or convention. It is a denial that every stable linguistic function requires a separate substantial dharma.

The Bhāṣya distinguishes:

```text
sound
    audible event

speech
    sound conventionally fixed so that a meaning is understood

name
    designation-function of meaningful speech

sentence
    articulated completion of meaning

linguistic group
    ordered collection of such elements
```

The crucial point is that a functional and conventional structure need not become a separately existing substance.

Meaning is neither reducible to undifferentiated noise nor explained by inserting an invisible name-entity between sound and referent. It belongs to the determinate use of sound within a conventional and cognitive order.

## Translation Consequences

- **nāma** is best translated here as “name” or “designation,” but its function is semantic delimitation, not merely a lexical label.
- **pada** is rendered “sentence” or “statement” in this context because Vasubandhu defines it by the completion of a meaning and by its ability to express relations of action, quality, and time.
- **vyañjana / akṣara** refers to a phonetic element or syllable, not primarily to a written letter.
- **kāya** means an aggregate or group, not a living body.
- **samukti** is a collection or coming-together, not an independently existing whole over and above its members.
- **niḥṣyanda** is a homogeneous outflow or result resembling its cause; it should not be flattened into generic causation.

## New Technical Terms

- **saṃjñākaraṇa** — the making of a designation; **status:** introduced in the Bhāṣya; **function:** defines *nāma*.
- **arthaparisamāpti** — completion of a meaning; **status:** Bhāṣya-specific formulation; **function:** defines the extent of a sentence.
- **vyañjana** — phonetic element or syllable; **status:** newly developed here; **function:** minimal linguistic articulation.
- **kṛtāvadhi** — having a conventionally fixed range or application; **status:** Bhāṣya-only formulation; **function:** explains how sound signifies a determinate meaning.
- **dyotaka** — revealer or indicator; **status:** new in this discussion; **function:** expresses semantic disclosure.
- **samukti** — collection or aggregation; **status:** rare/new; **function:** explains the sense of linguistic “group.”
- **pratīpadārthaka** — having an understood or conventionally established meaning; **status:** Bhāṣya-specific; **function:** connects sound and intelligible reference.
- **anabhilāpya** — inexpressible; **status:** introduced in a disputed classification; **function:** characterizes the alleged formless-realm linguistic entities.

## Logical Determination

The Bhāṣya establishes a general anti-reification rule:

```text
semantic function
    does not entail
separate semantic substance
```

The sequence is:

```text
sound
    becomes meaningful through conventional determination

meaningful sound
    performs designation

ordered meaningful sounds
    complete a statement

collections of these
    are conceptually designated as linguistic groups
```

No additional entity is required at any stage unless an independent causal or explanatory role can be shown.

The temporal argument is especially strong. A word unfolds successively; its phonetic parts do not coexist as one simultaneous substance. Meaning therefore cannot be explained through a static linguistic object produced by the completed sound-series. It depends upon an ordered continuum retained and synthesized by cognition.

## Limited Organon Interpretation

This passage belongs naturally to the Organon's treatment of the Rational–Empirical relation.

The empirical side is the audible sequence:

```text
successive sounds
written marks
speaker and hearer
```

The rational side is the determinate semantic order:

```text
fixed application
completed meaning
relation of action, quality, and time
```

But the rational form is not an occult substance hidden behind speech. It is the intelligible articulation immanent in the conventional use and cognitive synthesis of the sequence.

Thus the Kośa gives us a disciplined intermediate position:

```text
not mere noise
not a hypostatized Name-substance
but meaningful sound organized by convention and cognition
```

For the later Organon pass, the important principle is:

> Language is an empirical temporal stream whose rational form is the determinate articulation of meaning; the form is real as intelligible organization without therefore being a separate substance.

This is exactly the kind of “Light of Substance” we have been identifying: reason illuminates the genesis and structure of meaning without consuming the determinate linguistic event in an all-encompassing substance.
