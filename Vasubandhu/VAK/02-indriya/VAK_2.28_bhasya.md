# VAK_2.28 Bhāṣya — Wholesome Desire-Realm Citta and Remorse

## Kārikā

```text
savitarkavicāratvāt kuśale kāmacetasi /
dvāviṃśatiś caitasikāḥ kaukṛtyam adhikaṃ kvacit // VAkK_2.28 //
```

## Continuous Translation of the Bhāṣya

Desire-realm citta is necessarily accompanied by initial thought and sustained thought. Therefore, in this case:

> Because a wholesome desire-realm citta is accompanied by initial thought and sustained thought, there are twenty-two mental factors.

These necessarily occur: the ten great-ground factors, the ten wholesome great-ground factors, initial thought, and sustained thought.

> In some cases remorse is additional.

Remorse is not present in every wholesome citta. But where it is present, by counting it as the additional factor there are twenty-three mental factors.

What is this factor called *kaukṛtya*?

Literally, *kaukṛtya* is the state of something badly done. Here, however, the dharma that takes such bad action as its object is itself called *kaukṛtya*: it is remorse in the mind.

This is like calling the gateway of liberation that takes emptiness as its object “emptiness,” or calling non-greed that takes the unattractive as its object “the unattractive.” In ordinary speech too, the name of a place is transferred to those who occupy it, as when one says, “the whole village has arrived” or “the whole country has arrived.” The badly done deed serves as the basis or locus of remorse.

Alternatively, this is a figurative designation of the cause by the name of its result. It is like the statement: “These six bases of contact should be understood as former karma.”

But if remorse can take as its object something that was not done, how can it be called *kaukṛtya*?

Even what was not done can be spoken of as though it were something done: “It was not well done by me that I did not do it.”

Which remorse is wholesome?

The remorse felt because one failed to do what was wholesome, or because one did what was unwholesome, is wholesome. Remorse arising from the opposite conditions is unwholesome.

Both kinds can nevertheless be based upon either kind of action.

## Voice Map

- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — establishes the twenty-two-factor base count and the conditional twenty-third.
- **Voice: Objector** — asks what *kaukṛtya* is and how it can concern something not done.
- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — explains the transferred designation and distinguishes wholesome from unwholesome remorse.

## Analytical Reconstruction

### 1. Base configuration

A wholesome desire-realm citta necessarily includes:

```text
10 universal great-ground factors
10 wholesome great-ground factors
vitarka
vicāra
= 22
```

The count is necessary because desire-realm citta is always accompanied by both *vitarka* and *vicāra*.

### 2. Conditional addition

```text
wholesome desire-realm citta without remorse
    = 22 factors

wholesome desire-realm citta with remorse
    = 23 factors
```

The word *kvacit* is decisive. Remorse is not a universal wholesome factor.

### 3. Object and factor are distinguished

The word *kaukṛtya* literally names the badly performed action, but technically it names the mental factor that takes such an action as its object.

```text
badly done deed
    = object or basis

mental remorse concerning it
    = caitta called kaukṛtya
```

The designation is transferred from object, locus, or cause to the mental factor directed toward it.

### 4. Omission can become an object of remorse

The factor is not restricted to completed deeds. Failure to act can be represented retrospectively as a defective action:

```text
“I did badly in not doing it.”
```

Thus omission enters the same evaluative structure as commission.

### 5. Ethical status depends on evaluative direction

Wholesome remorse:

```text
regret over doing the unwholesome
regret over failing to do the wholesome
```

Unwholesome remorse:

```text
regret over doing the wholesome
regret over failing to do the unwholesome
```

Remorse is therefore not wholesome merely because it is painful or self-critical. Its ethical quality depends upon the value of the deed and the direction of the retrospective judgment.

The Bhāṣya’s final statement—both kinds can have either kind of basis—means that the objective act alone does not mechanically determine the ethical status of the remorse. The agent’s evaluative orientation is decisive.

## New Technical Terms

- **kaukṛtya** — mental remorse or regret directed toward an action or omission judged badly done.
  - **status:** introduced here as a caitta outside the fixed great-ground classes.
  - **function:** conditional factor; retrospectively evaluates conduct.

- **vipratisāra** — remorse, regret, or troubled reconsideration of the mind.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only explanatory term in this passage.
  - **function:** direct definition of the mental factor technically called *kaukṛtya*.

- **sthāna / sthānin** — locus and occupant.
  - **status:** explanatory analogy.
  - **function:** shows how the name of the object or basis is transferred to the mental state associated with it.

- **hetūpacāra** — figurative designation of a cause by the name of its result, or related causal transfer of designation.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only semantic explanation.
  - **function:** explains the technical naming of remorse.

- **ubhayādhiṣṭhāna** — capable of being based on either kind.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only qualification.
  - **function:** prevents a simplistic one-to-one identification between the ethical status of an act and that of the remorse concerning it.

## Translation Consequences

- *Kaukṛtya* is best translated here as **remorse** or **regret**, not merely “bad action.”
- *Vitarka* and *vicāra* should remain semi-technical because the passage uses them as fixed components of desire-realm citta rather than merely ordinary thinking and reflection.
- The verse’s “sometimes” marks a genuine optional association, unlike the necessary great-ground factors.

## Compact Determination

```text
wholesome desire-realm citta
    necessarily has 22 factors

if remorse is present
    it has 23

remorse is ethically qualified
    by what the mind regrets,
    not by painful affect alone
```
