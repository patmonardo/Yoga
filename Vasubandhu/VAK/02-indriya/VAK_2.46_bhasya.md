# VAK 2.46 — Birth, Causes and Conditions, and the Status of the Marks

## Kārikā

```text
jātijātyādayas teṣāṃ te 'ṣṭadharmaikavṛttayaḥ /
janyasya janikā jātir na hetupratyayair vinā // VAK 2.46 //
```

> The birth of birth and the remaining secondary marks belong to these; each primary mark operates upon eight dharmas. Birth generates what is to be generated, but not without causes and conditions.

## Bhāṣya — Continuous Translation

If future birth produces what is to be born, why does everything future not arise all at once?

Because:

> Birth generates what is to be generated, but not without causes and conditions.

Birth does not generate apart from the complete conjunction of causes and conditions.

**Objection:** In that case, it is precisely the efficacy of the causes and conditions that we observe. When their conjunction exists, the effect exists; when it does not exist, the effect does not exist. This does not depend upon birth. Therefore it is the causes and conditions themselves that generate.

**Vaibhāṣika:** But, venerable sir, is everything that exists necessarily perceived? Even subtle natures of dharmas exist.

Without birth, moreover, there would be no cognition expressed by “it has arisen.” Nor could one use the genitive expression “the arising of form,” just as one says “the form of form.” The same reasoning should be applied appropriately to duration, aging, and impermanence.

**Response:** Then non-self too would have to be accepted as a separate entity in order to account for the cognition “non-self.” And the number, magnitude, separateness, conjunction, disjunction, priority, posteriority, existence, and other categories imagined by the non-Buddhist teachers would also have to be accepted as separate realities, in order to account for cognitions such as “one,” “two,” “large,” “minute,” “separate,” “conjoined,” “disjoined,” “prior,” “posterior,” and “existent.”

They would also have to be accepted merely in order to justify genitive constructions such as “the conjunction of form.”

How, then, is this genitive construction to be understood—for example, “the own-nature of form”?

Therefore, the expression “it has arisen” is merely a conceptual designation intended to indicate that something, having previously not existed, now exists.

This arising, characterized by having previously not existed and then existing, admits many differentiations. In order to specify which arising is intended, one says “the arising of form,” so that the arising called “form” is understood rather than some other arising. It is like saying “the fragrance of sandalwood” or “the body of the stone child.”

Duration and the remaining marks should be understood in the same way, as appropriate.

**Objection:** If a thing arises without a real entity called birth, why do unconditioned dharmas such as space not also arise?

**Response:** “It arises” means: having previously not existed, it comes to exist. But an unconditioned dharma exists permanently; therefore it does not arise.

Just as, by the nature of things, not everything is accepted as possessing birth, so too one must accept that not everything arises.

Even if everything conditioned equally possessed birth, the other conditions capable of producing one thing would not therefore be capable of producing another. In precisely the same way, all conditions are incapable of producing an unconditioned dharma.

**Vaibhāṣika conclusion:** Nevertheless, birth and the other marks are established as real substances. Authoritative teachings are not to be discarded merely because critics exist. One does not fail to sow barley because deer exist; one does not refuse to eat sweet cakes because flies may land upon them. Therefore objections must be answered and the established doctrine must be followed.

Thus the conditioned marks have been explained.

## Argument Structure

The passage closes the long debate begun in VAK 2.45.

```text
Vaibhāṣika thesis:
    birth, duration, aging, and impermanence
    are real conditioned substances

Vasubandhu's challenge:
    their apparent functions are fully explained
    by the causal stream itself

Vaibhāṣika reply:
    birth operates only when causes and conditions are complete

Vasubandhu's counter:
    then the causes and conditions do the explanatory work
    and birth contributes nothing discernible
```

The question is therefore not whether conditioned dharmas arise. Both parties agree that they do. The question is whether arising requires an additional real dharma called *jāti*.

## Voices

**Voice: Objector / Vasubandhu’s critical argument**  
If arising occurs only when causes and conditions are complete, the observed efficacy belongs to the causes and conditions rather than to a separate entity called birth.

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika position**  
Subtle dharmas need not be directly perceived. The cognition and expression “it has arisen” and grammatical forms such as “the arising of form” point to a real birth-dharma.

**Voice: Vasubandhu**  
Cognition and grammar do not prove a corresponding separate substance. Otherwise every conceptual and grammatical distinction would have to be reified.

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika conclusion**  
Despite the objections, the reality of the four marks remains established by authoritative doctrine.

## New Technical Terms

- **hetu-pratyaya-sāmagrī** — the complete conjunction of causes and conditions
  - **status:** central in this passage; anticipates the later causal taxonomy
  - **function:** explains why a future dharma arises at one moment rather than all future dharmas arising simultaneously

- **abhūtvā-bhāva** — coming to exist after previously not existing
  - **status:** Bhāṣya explanatory formula
  - **function:** defines “arising” without positing a separate birth-substance

- **prajñapti-mātra** — mere conceptual designation
  - **status:** recurring critical term in the Bhāṣya
  - **function:** identifies birth, duration, aging, and impermanence as ways of describing the stream rather than separate substances

- **dharma-prakṛti** — nature or constitution of a dharma
  - **status:** used by the Vaibhāṣika reply
  - **function:** supports the claim that subtle realities may exist without direct perception

- **ṣaṣṭhī-vibhakti / genitive construction** — expressions such as “the arising of form”
  - **status:** grammatical argument
  - **function:** used by the Vaibhāṣika as evidence for distinctness, then rejected by Vasubandhu as ontologically inconclusive

## Translation Consequences

### 1. Birth is not an independent producer

The Kārikā appears to grant birth a generating function, but the Bhāṣya immediately restricts it:

```text
birth generates
    only together with
causes and conditions
```

The opponent then removes the redundant middle term:

```text
complete causes and conditions
    → arising

therefore:
    no additional birth-substance is required
```

### 2. Grammar does not determine ontology

The expression “the arising of form” does not prove that arising and form are two independently real entities.

```text
grammatical distinction
    ≠ substantial distinction
```

Vasubandhu’s reductio is powerful: if every distinct cognition or genitive expression required a distinct substance, one would have to accept the entire Vaiśeṣika-style inventory of number, magnitude, conjunction, disjunction, priority, posteriority, and existence.

### 3. Arising is a determination of the stream

The preferred account is:

```text
not previously present
    → presently present
```

“Birth” names this transition. “The birth of form” specifies which stream-transition is under discussion.

### 4. The unconditioned does not arise

The objection concerning space clarifies the definition:

```text
conditioned:
    previously absent, then present

unconditioned:
    not generated through such a transition
```

The absence of a substantial birth-dharma does not collapse the distinction between conditioned and unconditioned.

## Doctrinal Determination

VAK 2.46 completes a sustained Sautrāntika-style critique of the Vaibhāṣika tendency to reify structural and relational determinations.

The same critical method has now been applied to:

```text
prāpti
aprāpti
sabhāgatā
samāpatti
jīvita
birth, duration, aging, impermanence
```

The recurring question is:

> Does a real and intelligible structural distinction require a further real substance corresponding to it?

Vasubandhu repeatedly answers: not necessarily. A distinction may be fully grounded in the transformation, capacity, sequence, or designation of the continuum itself.

## Restrained Organon Reading

This passage strongly supports the interpretation of Vasubandhu as a thinker of **immanent determination** rather than an all-consuming substance.

The Vaibhāṣika model externalizes the moments of becoming:

```text
thing
    + birth acting upon it
    + duration acting upon it
    + aging acting upon it
    + impermanence acting upon it
```

Vasubandhu relocates those moments within the intelligible movement of the thing’s causal stream:

```text
causal conjunction
    → arising
    → continuation
    → transformation
    → interruption
```

This is a “Light of Substance” insofar as the conditioned thing is understood through its genesis and relations. Yet the light does not consume the determinate dharma into one undifferentiated Absolute. The dharma remains precisely this conditioned determination within this causal series.

The higher Organon issue may later be stated as:

```text
External reflection:
    becoming requires added reflective entities

Immanent reflection:
    becoming is the dharma's own
    cause-conditioned determinacy
```

For the present Bhāṣya pass, the essential conclusion is simpler:

> Causes and conditions explain arising; “birth” names the intelligible transition but need not exist as an additional substance.

## Relation to the Next Passage

The Bhāṣya closes with:

> Thus the conditioned marks have been explained.

It then turns to the remaining *citta-viprayukta-saṃskāras*: the collections of names, sentences, and syllables. VAK 2.47 therefore moves from the ontology of becoming to the structure of linguistic designation.
