# VAK 2.56 Bhāṣya — The Mapping of Causes to Fruits

## Kārikā

### Sanskrit

```text
vipākaphalam antyasya pūrvasyādhipataṃ phalam /
sabhāgasarvatragayor niṣyandaḥ pauruṣaṃ dvayoḥ // VAK 2.56 //
```

### Working Translation

```text
The last cause has maturation as its fruit; the first has predominance as its fruit.
The homogeneous and pervasive causes have homogeneous outflow as their fruit;
the two remaining causes have the fruit of efficacious activity.
```

## Continuous Bhāṣya Translation

Which fruit belongs to which cause?

The last cause has maturation as its fruit.

The maturation cause is called “the last” because it was stated last in the list of causes. Its fruit is the maturation-fruit.

The first cause has predominance as its fruit.

The conditioning cause is called “the first” because it was stated first. Its fruit is the fruit of predominance.

**Objection:** If the conditioning cause is established merely through non-obstruction, what predominance does it exercise?

**Response:** This very non-obstruction is its predominance. Moreover, a conditioning cause can also exercise positive assistance. Thus, for example, the ten sense-bases exercise predominance with respect to the five groups of consciousness, and karmic action exercises predominance with respect to the receptacle-world. The ear and the other faculties can also exercise indirect predominance in the arising of visual consciousness; for example, after hearing something, the desire to see it may arise. Other cases should be understood in the same way.

The homogeneous and pervasive causes have homogeneous outflow as their fruit.

Because their fruits are similar to their causes, the fruit of these two causes is called a homogeneous outflow.

The two have the fruit of efficacious activity.

The co-arisen cause and the associated cause have the fruit of efficacious activity.

The term *puruṣakāra* is used because the activity is, as it were, the activity of a person. Thus expressions such as “crow’s-leg” for a medicinal plant, “mad elephant” as a designation, and “man” are used figuratively. The fruit belonging to such activity is called the fruit of efficacious activity.

What, then, is this so-called efficacious activity? It is the function or causal activity performed by a dharma.

**Question:** Do the other causes also have a fruit of efficacious activity, or only these two?

**Response:** The other causes also have such a fruit, with the exception of the maturation cause. This is because a fruit of efficacious activity arises either simultaneously with its cause or immediately after it, whereas maturation does not arise in either of these ways.

Some, however, say that even the maturation cause has a temporally distant fruit of efficacious activity, just as crops are the fruit of the farmer’s activity.

What, then, is called a maturation-fruit, and what is called a fruit of predominance?

A maturation-fruit is an indeterminate dharma.

More precisely, a maturation is an unobscured-indeterminate dharma. But something not designated as belonging to a sentient being might also be unobscured-indeterminate. Therefore the text specifies:

It is designated as belonging to a sentient being.

But something accumulated through present nourishment or produced as a homogeneous outflow might also be designated as belonging to a sentient being. Therefore the text further specifies:

It arises from what is morally determinate.

Wholesome and unwholesome dharmas are called morally determinate because they are defined with respect to maturation. Hence a dharma that arises later—not simultaneously and not immediately afterward—from such a morally determinate cause is called a maturation-fruit. This is the defining characteristic of maturation.

Why is something produced by karma but not designated as belonging to a sentient being not called maturation?

Because it is common. Another person can also make use of it in the same way.

Maturation, however, is non-common. One person does not experience the maturation of karma performed by another.

**Question:** Why, then, can another person experience the fruit of predominance produced by someone else’s karma?

**Response:** Because it arises from common karma.

A homogeneous outflow is similar to its cause.

A dharma similar to its cause is the homogeneous-outflow fruit, as in the case of the homogeneous cause and the pervasive cause.

**Question:** If the pervasive cause also has a similar fruit, why is it not simply admitted to be a homogeneous cause?

**Response:** Its similarity consists in belonging to the same level and in being afflicted, but not necessarily in belonging to the same specific type. When it is also similar in specific type, it is indeed admitted to be a homogeneous cause.

Therefore a fourfold analysis is made concerning whether a cause is homogeneous, pervasive, both, or neither.

## Argument Reconstruction

VAK 2.56 completes the first explicit cause–fruit table:

| Cause | Principal Fruit |
|---|---|
| maturation cause (*vipāka-hetu*) | maturation-fruit (*vipāka-phala*) |
| conditioning cause (*kāraṇa-hetu*) | fruit of predominance (*adhipati-phala*) |
| homogeneous cause (*sabhāga-hetu*) | homogeneous-outflow fruit (*niṣyanda-phala*) |
| pervasive cause (*sarvatraga-hetu*) | homogeneous-outflow fruit (*niṣyanda-phala*) |
| co-arisen cause (*sahabhū-hetu*) | fruit of efficacious activity (*puruṣakāra-phala*) |
| associated cause (*saṃprayuktaka-hetu*) | fruit of efficacious activity (*puruṣakāra-phala*) |

The mapping does not imply that each cause has only one fruit in every respect. The verse assigns the principal fruit by which each causal type is doctrinally distinguished. The Bhāṣya then immediately qualifies this by allowing the fruit of efficacious activity to several causes, because “efficacious activity” names the operative function of a dharma rather than an exclusive causal class.

### 1. Predominance Is Broader Than Production

The conditioning cause was defined broadly through non-obstruction. The objection is therefore natural: how can mere non-interference count as predominance?

The response has two levels:

```text
minimal predominance:
    the cause does not obstruct arising

positive predominance:
    the cause assists arising directly or indirectly
```

Thus *adhipati* is not identical with efficient production. It names the broader field of enabling, supporting, directing, and permitting conditions.

### 2. Maturation Requires Temporal Distance

Maturation is excluded from simultaneous and immediately subsequent production:

```text
maturation cause
    → transformation of the continuum
    → later non-common result
```

This distinguishes maturation from direct activity. The result must arise later from a morally determinate cause and must belong specifically to the continuum of the sentient being whose karma matured.

### 3. Common and Non-Common Fruits

The Bhāṣya distinguishes:

```text
common karmic result:
    receptacle-world and shared environment
    available to multiple beings

non-common maturation:
    body, faculty, and experience
    belonging to one continuum
```

This is why a karmically produced external environment may be a fruit of predominance without being a maturation-fruit in the strict sense.

### 4. Similarity Has Degrees

The homogeneous-outflow fruit need not duplicate the cause in every respect. The pervasive cause may be similar to its fruit in level and afflicted status while differing in specific afflictive type.

Therefore similarity must be indexed:

```text
same level
same contaminated status
same specific type
```

Homogeneous causality requires the stronger similarity; pervasive causality can operate through the broader afflicted field.

## Voices

- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — introduces the cause–fruit mappings and clarifies the definitions.
- **Voice: Objector** — asks how non-obstruction can amount to predominance and whether other causes possess the fruit of activity.
- **Voice: Vaibhāṣika position** — supplies the formal taxonomy of causes and fruits, the definition of maturation, and the fourfold analysis of homogeneous and pervasive causes.
- **Voice: uncertain / alternate teachers** — proposes that even the maturation cause has a temporally distant fruit of activity, compared with a farmer’s crops.

## New Technical Terms

- **adhipati-phala** — fruit of predominance; the result of enabling, supporting, or non-obstructive causal conditions.
  - **status:** new as an explicit fruit-category.
  - **function:** distinguishes broad conditioning efficacy from homogeneous production and karmic maturation.

- **puruṣakāra-phala / pauruṣa-phala** — fruit of efficacious activity; the result of a dharma’s operative function.
  - **status:** new in this passage.
  - **function:** identifies causality through present or immediately subsequent activity.

- **vipāka-phala** — maturation-fruit; an unobscured-indeterminate, sentient-being-associated result arising later from wholesome or unwholesome karma.
  - **status:** previously encountered in germ, now formally defined.
  - **function:** distinguishes karmic maturation from immediate causal production.

- **niṣyanda-phala** — homogeneous-outflow fruit; a result similar to its cause.
  - **status:** previously encountered, now formally mapped to causes.
  - **function:** explains continuity and propagation within a causal series.

- **asādhāraṇa** — non-common or individually appropriated.
  - **status:** newly decisive here.
  - **function:** marks the result that belongs specifically to one sentient continuum.

- **sādhāraṇa-karma** — common karma.
  - **status:** newly explicit.
  - **function:** explains shared environmental results without treating them as one individual’s maturation.

## Translation Consequences

1. **Adhipati** should not be reduced to “dominant cause” in the sense of forceful production. Its scope includes non-obstruction and indirect assistance.
2. **Puruṣakāra** is better rendered “efficacious activity” than “human action.” The term is analogical and refers to the operative function of a dharma.
3. **Vipāka** must retain the sense of maturation rather than generic result. Its defining features are moral determination of the cause, temporal delay, individual appropriation, and unobscured-indeterminate status of the result.
4. **Niṣyanda** should be translated consistently as “homogeneous outflow” or “homogeneous continuation,” because mere “result” loses the similarity relation.

## Doctrinal and Logical Determination

The verse establishes that causal knowledge is not complete until the mode of manifestation of the result is known.

```text
cause known only as prior condition
    = incomplete judgment

cause known through its proper fruit-type
    = genetically determined judgment
```

The four fruit-types express four different relations of ground to manifestation:

```text
vipāka:
    ground transformed into a temporally distant, appropriated result

adhipati:
    ground enabling or directing the arising of another

niṣyanda:
    ground continuing through similarity

puruṣakāra:
    ground manifesting through operative efficacy
```

The same empirical event can therefore stand under more than one rational determination. A crafted object, for example, may be the fruit of the artisan’s activity and also a fruit of predominance for other enabling conditions. The causal relation is not exhausted by one abstract predicate.

## Limited Organon Interpretation

This is the clearest formulation yet of the Rational–Empirical dual nature of the Kośa’s causal science.

The empirical side presents the result as Being-there:

```text
body
faculty
cognition
environment
action-produced event
```

The rational side determines **how the ground is present in that result**:

```text
as maturation
as predominance
as homogeneous continuation
as efficacious activity
```

Thus *hetu–phala* is not merely an external succession of two things. It is the genetic judgment that identifies the form by which the prior ground is illuminated in the present result.

The important restraint is that the Kośa does not collapse these forms into one Absolute Cause. Its causal light is differentiated. *Prakāśa* means that each fruit becomes intelligible through the precise mode of its derivation.

## Relation to VAK 2.55

VAK 2.55 established the limit of causality:

```text
conditioned cause
    does not produce the unconditioned
```

VAK 2.56 now determines the positive field of conditioned fruits:

```text
Path and conditioned causes
    transform, enable, continue, and act

liberative truth
    is not another product of these operations
```

This preserves the distinction we needed between the Path and its Fruit. The Path has conditioned causal efficacy; disconnection is realized through that efficacy without becoming merely another conditioned maturation or outflow.
