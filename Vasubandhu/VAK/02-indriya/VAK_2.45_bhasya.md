# VAK 2.45 Bhāṣya — The Four Marks of the Conditioned

## Kārikā

```text
lakṣaṇāni punar jātir jarā sthitir anityatā // VAkK_2.45 //
```

**Translation**

```text
The marks are birth, aging, duration, and impermanence.
```

## Bhāṣya Translation

The four marks of a conditioned dharma are birth, duration, aging, and impermanence. A dharma in which these occur is thereby marked as conditioned; from the contrary, the unconditioned is distinguished.

Birth causes the dharma to arise. Duration maintains it. Aging makes it age. Impermanence destroys it.

An objection is raised: the sūtra speaks of three marks of the conditioned. Why, then, is a fourth stated here?

The omitted one is duration. When the sūtra says “change during duration,” this is another designation for aging, just as “arising” is another designation for birth and “disappearance” another designation for impermanence.

The sūtra mentions those dharmas that move formations through the temporal series, and it does so in order to produce disillusionment. Birth moves formations from the future time into the present. Aging and impermanence weaken them and move them from the present into the past. It is like a man who has entered a dense forest and is attacked by three enemies: one drags him out of the thicket, while the other two deprive him of life. Duration, by contrast, embraces the formations and remains with them, as though unwilling to be separated from them. For that reason it was not established in the sūtra as one of the marks of the conditioned. Moreover, even the unconditioned remains in its own intrinsic nature.

Others explain that the sūtra combines duration and aging into the single expression “change during duration.” Why do so? Duration is a possible basis of attachment. Therefore the Buddha displays it together with the misfortune of aging, as one might display beauty accompanied by calamity, so that beings will not become attached to it.

Thus there are four marks of the conditioned.

But these four—birth and the others—are themselves conditioned. They must therefore possess further birth, duration, aging, and impermanence. The Vaibhāṣikas accept this. There is a birth of birth, a duration of duration, an aging of aging, and an impermanence of impermanence. These are secondary marks.

Does this not produce four marks for each mark, followed by an infinite regress?

No, they reply, because the primary and secondary marks operate with respect to eight dharmas. “Operation” means activity or causal efficacy. A single conditioned dharma arises as a ninth together with eight marks and secondary marks. The primary birth, excluding itself, produces the other eight dharmas. The birth of birth produces only that primary birth. It is like one hen that produces many offspring and another that produces only a few. In the same way, primary duration maintains the other eight while duration-of-duration maintains only primary duration. Aging and impermanence are to be understood correspondingly. Thus no regress occurs.

The Sautrāntikas reply: this is splitting empty space. Birth and the other marks do not exist as separate substances in the manner in which they are described. There is no proof of their substantial existence—neither perception, inference, nor authoritative scripture—such as there is for form and the other dharmas.

What, then, of the sūtra statement: “The arising of the conditioned is discerned; its passing away is discerned; its change during duration is discerned”?

One who knows only the wording but not the meaning is dear to the gods, for the Blessed One taught that one should rely upon meaning.

What is the meaning?

Ordinary persons, blinded by ignorance, become attached to the stream of formations as Self and as belonging to Self. To turn them away from this false conviction, the Blessed One wished to reveal that the stream of formations is conditioned and dependently arisen. Therefore he taught the three marks of the conditioned. He was speaking of the stream, not of an isolated instant. The arising and other marks of a single instant are not discerned; and what cannot be discerned is not fit to serve as a mark.

The expression “of the conditioned” is repeated in the sūtra so that the listener will understand that these are marks of conditionedness. Otherwise one might suppose that the existence of a conditioned thing is marked by something separate from it, as a water bird marks the presence of water, or that its moral quality is marked as one recognizes the qualities of a maiden.

For a stream, its beginning is arising; its cessation is passing away. The stream’s continuation is duration. The difference between its earlier and later phases is change during duration.

Thus it is said:

```text
Birth is the beginning of a stream;
passing away is its interruption;
duration is the stream itself;
and change during duration is the difference
between its earlier and later phases.
```

And again:

```text
Birth is a previously nonexistent occurrence;
duration is continuity;
passing away is the cutting off of that continuity;
and change during duration is the difference
between the earlier and later phases of the continuity.
```

A momentary dharma would pass away without requiring a distinct substantial duration. Once it has passed away, such a duration would be useless. Therefore duration is the stream itself.

This also makes sense of the Abhidharma definition: “What is duration? The non-destruction of arisen formations.” An isolated arisen instant does not remain undestroyed.

Likewise, when the Jñānaprasthāna asks of a single citta, “What is its arising? Birth. What is its passing away? Death. What is its change during duration? Aging,” the reference can properly be understood as the citta-continuum belonging to one class of existence.

Even at each moment the marks of the conditioned can be explained without positing separate substances. At each moment, arising is the coming-to-be of what previously was not; passing away is the nonexistence of what had come to be; duration is the connection of each earlier moment with the following moment; and change during duration is the dissimilarity of that sequence.

But when similar moments arise, are they not without difference?

No. Their difference is inferred from differences of causal force. A diamond or other object thrown strongly, weakly, or not at all falls after different periods. This establishes differences in the transformation of the great elements. Conditioned dharmas whose differences are not very great are still perceived as similar despite their transformation.

An objection follows: in the final moment of a sound or flame, and at the time of final nirvāṇa when the six sense bases have no succeeding moment, there is no duration or change during duration. Would the marks therefore fail to apply universally?

No. The sūtra does not say that duration alone is the mark. It says “change during duration.” Wherever there is duration, there is necessarily change; therefore the account of the marks is not disrupted.

In summary, the Blessed One indicates that the conditioned is that which, having not existed, comes to exist; having existed, does not continue to exist; and whose continuity called duration becomes otherwise and otherwise.

Why posit birth and the other marks as substances separate from this?

A mark need not be a separate substance from what it marks. The marks of a great person are not separate from the great person; the dewlap, tail, hump, hoof, and horns that mark a cow are not separate from the cow; hardness and the other characteristics of the elements are not separate from those elements. Smoke is recognized at a distance through its upward motion, yet that motion is not another substance apart from the smoke. The same reasoning applies here.

The conditionedness of form and the other dharmas is not grasped simply by grasping their intrinsic nature. One must also know their prior nonexistence, their subsequent nonexistence, and the differences within their continuum. Thus they are not marked merely by themselves; but neither are birth and the other marks distinct substances.

Suppose nevertheless that birth and the others were separate substances. Then, since they coexist, one dharma would at the same time be born, remain, age, and perish.

The Vaibhāṣika replies that their activities occur at different times. Future birth performs its function; once the dharma has arisen, duration, aging, and impermanence perform theirs in the present. Thus the dharma does not remain, age, and perish at the moment it is born.

But this first requires proving whether a future dharma exists substantially. Even if it does, how can birth be future while performing its activity? A mark of futurity must be supplied. Once birth has completed its activity, how does it remain present? A mark of presentness must also be supplied.

Moreover, if duration, aging, and impermanence all perform their activities simultaneously in the present, then the dharma would be maintained, aged, and destroyed in the very same instant. Which is it to do—remain, age, or perish?

If one says that their activities occur successively, momentariness is contradicted. If one says that a single moment is long enough for the whole sequence to be completed, then one must still explain why duration maintains the dharma while aging does not age it and impermanence does not destroy it at that same time.

The answer given is that duration is stronger.

What establishes its greater strength?

It maintains the dharma until impermanence destroys both it and the dharma.

But once impermanence has performed that task, it cannot perform it again, just as birth cannot produce again what it has already produced.

This may be plausible for birth, since what has already been brought into the present cannot be brought into the present again. But duration could in principle maintain its object indefinitely. Why should it become incapable?

What blocks it? Aging and impermanence.

If they are stronger, they should have been stronger from the beginning. And once duration has ceased operating, neither aging nor impermanence themselves remain, nor does the dharma. Upon what, then, could they operate, and what work would remain for them to perform?

The dharma did not perish immediately because duration held it. Once duration abandons it, it necessarily fails to remain. That very failure to remain is its destruction. There is no need for a separate destructive substance.

One might define duration as the non-destruction of an arisen dharma and impermanence as its destruction. Aging, however, cannot be explained in the same way. If aging leaves the dharma exactly as it was, aging has not occurred. If the dharma becomes otherwise, then it is already another dharma. Therefore aging cannot belong to one and the same substantially identical entity.

As the verse says:

```text
If it remains exactly the same, aging is impossible;
if it becomes otherwise, it is another thing.
Therefore aging cannot properly belong
to one and the same entity.
```

Another school says that impermanence destroys only after encountering a cause of destruction. But then it would be like saying that, after encountering harītakī, a goddess causes purgation. Why invent the goddess? Let destruction arise from the destructive cause itself.

Further, citta and caittas are admitted to be momentary and to perish without depending upon an external cause of destruction. Duration and impermanence would therefore have to perform their activities at the same time, resulting once again in the absurdity that one and the same dharma is both maintained and destroyed in one instant.

Therefore the marks of the conditioned are taught with reference to the stream. Read in this way, the sūtra is properly understood.

## Voice Map

- **Vaibhāṣika position:** birth, duration, aging, impermanence, and their secondary marks are real conditioned substances with distinct causal activities.
- **Objector:** raises the problems of the sūtra’s three marks, infinite regress, simultaneity, and incompatible activities.
- **Sautrāntika position:** the four marks are conceptual descriptions of the conditioned stream, not separate substances.
- **Vasubandhu:** strongly supports the Sautrāntika critique and repeatedly presses the contradictions in the substantialist account.

## New Technical Terms

- **saṃskṛta-lakṣaṇa** — mark of the conditioned.
  - **Status:** central to this passage.
  - **Function:** distinguishes conditioned from unconditioned dharmas.
- **anulakṣaṇa** — secondary mark, such as birth-of-birth.
  - **Status:** introduced in the Bhāṣya’s Vaibhāṣika defense.
  - **Function:** prevents the primary marks from lacking the marks required of conditioned entities.
- **kāritra / puruṣakāra** — activity, operation, causal efficacy.
  - **Status:** important in the temporal debate.
  - **Function:** explains how a mark acts upon its marked dharma.
- **pravāha** — stream or continuum.
  - **Status:** decisive Sautrāntika term.
  - **Function:** true referent of the scriptural marks.
- **prabandha** — continuity, connected succession.
  - **Status:** used in defining duration.
  - **Function:** replaces duration as a separate substance.
- **sthity-anyathātva** — change during duration.
  - **Status:** scriptural expression.
  - **Function:** difference between earlier and later phases of a continuum.

## Translation Consequences

1. **Jāti** here is best translated as “birth” or “arising,” but the Bhāṣya distinguishes a substantial Vaibhāṣika birth from a merely descriptive beginning of a stream.
2. **Sthiti** should not automatically be read as enduring substance. In the preferred account it is continuity itself.
3. **Jarā** is not merely biological old age. It is alteration within the continuing series.
4. **Anityatā** is not a destructive agent in the preferred account. It is the dharma’s failure to continue.
5. **Lakṣaṇa** does not require an ontologically separate mark. A thing may be marked by a feature inseparable from its own mode of existence.

## Doctrinal Determination

VAK 2.45 extends the critique already applied to prāpti, aprāpti, samāpatti, and jīvita. Whenever the Vaibhāṣika converts a structural relation into an additional real entity, Vasubandhu asks whether that entity possesses an observable own-nature, a distinct causal function, or a valid proof.

The preferred analysis is genetic and serial:

```text
arising
    = beginning of the stream

duration
    = continuity of the stream

aging
    = transformation between earlier and later phases

impermanence
    = interruption or failure of continuation
```

The conditioned is therefore not a stable entity upon which four external forces operate. It is the very process of arising, continuing through difference, and ceasing.

## Limited Organon Interpretation

This passage gives the later Organon pass a major problem of Essence:

```text
substantial mark
    versus
immanent determination
```

The Vaibhāṣika makes the marks into externalized reflective determinations. The Sautrāntika restores them to the movement of the continuum itself.

A Hegel-clean reconstruction may eventually say:

```text
Birth, duration, aging, and impermanence
are not things beside the conditioned.
They are the conditioned dharma's own moments
of becoming explicit.
```

For the present pass, however, the central Kośa result is simpler and decisive: conditionedness belongs to the genesis and transformation of the stream, not to four additional substances imposed upon it.
