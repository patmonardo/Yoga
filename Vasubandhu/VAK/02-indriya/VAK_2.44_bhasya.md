# VAK 2.44 Bhāṣya — The Two Cessation-Attainments, the Return of Citta, and the Life Faculty

## Kārikā

```text
bodhilabhyā muneḥ na prāk catustriṃśatkṣaṇāptitaḥ /
kāmarūpāśraye bhūte nirodhākhyādito nṛṣu // VAK 2.44 //
```

## Working Translation of the Kārikā

For the Sage, the attainment is obtained with awakening, not before, because awakening is acquired in thirty-four moments. Both attainments can arise with a support belonging to the desire or form realm; the attainment called cessation is first produced among human beings.

---

## Continuous Translation of the Bhāṣya

Although these two attainments differ in many respects, both the attainment of non-ideation and the attainment of cessation can be produced in the desire realm and in the form realm.

Those who do not accept that the attainment of non-ideation can occur in the form realm contradict the following canonical passage:

> There can be a form-realm existence that is not an existence consisting of the five destinies. This is the existence of form-realm beings—ideational gods whose citta is temporarily different, those who have entered the attainment of non-ideation or the attainment of cessation, and the non-ideational gods who have obtained the non-ideational result.

Therefore both attainments may occur with a support belonging to the desire or form realm.

There is nevertheless this difference:

> The attainment called cessation is first produced among human beings.

The attainment of cessation is first generated among human beings. Later it may occur in the form realm in those who had previously acquired it and subsequently lost it.

**Question:** Can one fall away even from this attainment?

**Answer:** Yes. Otherwise the sūtra addressed to Udāyin would be contradicted. The Blessed One says there that a monk endowed with morality, concentration, and wisdom repeatedly enters and emerges from the cessation of ideation and feeling. If he does not attain final knowledge in this very life or at the time of death, then after the breakup of the body he is reborn among certain divine beings possessing a mind-made body, beyond the gods who consume morsel-food. Reborn there, he again repeatedly enters and emerges from the cessation of ideation and feeling.

In this passage the divine mind-made body is a form-realm body. Yet the attainment of cessation belongs to the summit of existence. How could one who had not fallen away from that attainment be reborn in the form realm?

Members of another school maintain that there is also an attainment of cessation belonging to the level of the fourth dhyāna. On their view the passage can be explained without accepting a fall from the attainment. But their thesis that the attainment of cessation can belong to the fourth dhyāna is itself untenable, because the sūtra speaks of the nine successive attainments.

**Objection:** If their succession is fixed, how can practitioners enter the attainments in a transposed order?

**Reply:** The rule applies to one who is producing them for the first time. Those who have obtained mastery can later pass over intermediate attainments and enter them in a different order.

Thus the two attainments differ with respect to level: the attainment of non-ideation belongs to the fourth dhyāna, whereas the attainment of cessation belongs to the summit of existence.

They also differ with respect to preparation. The former is preceded by an attention that conceives non-ideation as escape; the latter by an attention directed toward peaceful abiding.

They differ with respect to continuum. The former belongs to the continuum of an ordinary person; the latter to the continuum of a noble person.

They differ with respect to result. The former produces existence among the non-ideational beings; the latter produces a result connected with the summit of existence.

They differ with respect to the time at which their result is experienced. The result of the former is fixed and experienced at the next rebirth. The latter may be experienced in one of two later lives, or its maturation may remain unfixed.

They differ with respect to first production. The attainment of non-ideation may first be produced in two realms, whereas the attainment of cessation is first produced only among human beings.

**Question:** Since both attainments have the cessation of citta and caittas as their nature, why are they called respectively the attainment of non-ideation and the attainment of the cessation of ideation and feeling?

**Answer:** Because the preparatory practice for each is directed against the factors named. The expression functions as when knowledge of another’s citta is called “knowledge of another’s citta” even though it also knows the mental factors associated with that citta.

### How does citta arise again?

**Question:** How can citta arise again after citta has remained ceased for a long time?

The Vaibhāṣikas answer that past dharmas exist. Therefore a past citta can serve as the immediately preceding condition for the later citta.

Others answer differently:

How does material form arise again among beings reborn in the formless realm after their material form has long ceased? It does not arise from earlier material form; it arises from citta. In the same way, after cessation, citta arises from the body endowed with faculties and not from an immediately preceding citta.

The former teachers say that these two—citta and the body endowed with faculties—contain one another’s seeds.

The venerable Vasumitra says in the *Paripṛcchā*:

> This difficulty applies only to one who maintains that the attainment of cessation is entirely without citta. On my view, the attainment contains citta.

The venerable Ghoṣaka rejects this. The Blessed One has said that when consciousness is present, the conjunction of the three is contact, and conditioned by contact there arise feeling, ideation, and volition. If consciousness were present in the attainment, ideation and feeling would not cease.

An opponent replies: The sūtra also says that craving arises conditioned by feeling, yet an arhat may have feeling without craving arising. Likewise, perhaps contact may be present without feeling and the other factors arising.

This analogy fails. The arising of craving is specified as depending upon feeling born from contact joined with ignorance. But in the statement concerning the arising of feeling from contact, contact is not qualified in this way. Therefore the cases are not parallel.

Accordingly, the Vaibhāṣikas maintain that the attainment of cessation is without citta.

**Question:** How can something without citta be called an attainment (*samāpatti*)?

**Answer:** Because it brings the great elements into a condition of equilibrium. It is also called an attainment because one enters it by means of the citta immediately preceding the attainment: through that citta one “comes into” this state.

### Substance or designation?

**Question:** Are these two attainments real substances, or are they conceptual designations?

The Vaibhāṣika says that they are real substances because they obstruct the arising of citta.

Vasubandhu rejects this. The citta that enters the attainment is itself sufficient to obstruct the arising of a different citta. That citta arises in opposition to subsequent mental activity and transforms the support so that citta does not operate for a period of time.

What is designated as “the attainment” is simply this non-operation of citta. Before entry it did not exist, and after emergence it no longer exists. It is called conditioned because it is established through a conditioned transformation. Alternatively, the term “attainment” designates the support’s being brought into precisely this condition.

The non-ideational result should be understood in the same way. A citta arises that is opposed to the subsequent operation of citta; the mere non-operation of citta is then designated as the non-ideational state.

The Vaibhāṣikas do not accept this interpretation.

Thus the two attainments have been explained.

## Transition: Jīvita, the Life Faculty

**Question:** What is life?

> Life is the lifespan.

The Abhidharma says: “What is the life faculty? The lifespan belonging to the three realms.” But this statement does not yet explain what kind of dharma lifespan is.

The kārikā therefore adds:

> It is that which supports warmth and consciousness.

The Blessed One says:

> When lifespan, warmth, and consciousness abandon the body, it lies cast aside like an insentient piece of wood.

The dharma that supports warmth and consciousness and causes them to continue is called lifespan.

**Objection:** What then supports lifespan itself? If lifespan, warmth, and consciousness support one another, which one ceases first and causes the other two to cease? Their mutual dependence would entail that none could ever cease.

A reply proposes that karma supports lifespan, because lifespan continues for as long as karma has projected it.

But why not say directly that karma supports warmth and consciousness as well?

The Vaibhāṣika resists this because it would follow that all consciousness up to death is maturation-result.

Perhaps karma supports warmth, and warmth supports consciousness. But then in the formless realm consciousness would lack a support, since there is no warmth there. One might reply that there karma directly supports consciousness. Yet one cannot arbitrarily make warmth the support in one realm and karma the support in another.

The conclusion is that lifespan must be accepted as supporting warmth and consciousness.

Vasubandhu answers: We do not deny lifespan, but we deny that it is a separate substance. It is the temporal projection of the persistence of a homogeneous continuum produced by karma belonging to the three realms. For as long as karma has projected that a particular class-continuum is to persist, it persists; this projected duration is called lifespan.

It is like the projected time required for crops to ripen, or like the duration of an arrow’s flight after it has been released.

One should not imagine that a separate quality arises in the arrow and independently produces its movement. If there were one unchanging quality, differences in speed and the eventual fall of the arrow would be inexplicable. Nor can resistance by wind alone account for the fall.

The Vaibhāṣikas nevertheless maintain that lifespan is a separate real entity.

### Death and the exhaustion of lifespan

Does death occur only through exhaustion of lifespan, or can it occur otherwise?

The *Prajñapti* gives a fourfold analysis:

1. death through exhaustion of the karma that matures as lifespan, but not through exhaustion of merit;
2. death through exhaustion of the karma that matures as enjoyments, but not through exhaustion of lifespan;
3. death through exhaustion of both;
4. death through failure to avoid harmful or adverse conditions.

Voluntary relinquishing of lifespan need not be added as a fifth case, because death then still occurs through exhaustion of the lifespan that has been relinquished.

The *Jñānaprasthāna* further distinguishes a lifespan bound to a continuum from a lifespan that, once arisen, persists without interruption. Among desire-realm beings who have entered the attainment of non-ideation or cessation, lifespan remains dependent upon the integrity of the bodily support. Among beings absorbed in the form and formless realms, it persists once produced because the support is not exposed to the same injuries.

The Kāśmīra masters therefore distinguish a lifespan subject to interruption from one not subject to interruption and accept untimely death.

The Bhāṣya then reviews the canonical fourfold classification of embodiments according to whether one’s own intention, another’s intention, both, or neither can terminate them. The examples show that the duration of a continuum is governed by a precise conjunction of karmic projection, bodily support, realm, attainment, and path-status—not by a single undifferentiated vital substance.

With this, the life faculty has been explained.

## Argument Structure

### 1. Two attainments, one abstract cessation

Both attainments suspend citta and caittas, but their truth is determined by their genesis:

```text
attainment of non-ideation
    ordinary continuum
    mistaken conception of escape
    fourth dhyāna
    fixed rebirth result

attainment of cessation
    noble continuum
    peaceful abiding
    summit of existence
    non-fixed result profile
```

The abstract absence of ideation therefore does not determine the spiritual value of the state.

### 2. The return of citta

Three explanations appear:

- **Vaibhāṣika:** a past citta exists and serves as immediately preceding condition.
- **Former teachers / Sautrāntika tendency:** citta and the embodied faculty-complex contain one another’s seeds; citta can re-emerge from the transformed support.
- **Vasumitra:** a subtle citta remains in cessation.

Ghoṣaka and the Vaibhāṣikas reject the third view because consciousness would entail contact, feeling, and ideation.

### 3. The ontological test

The same critical method used against *prāpti* returns:

```text
Does the explanatory work require a separate substance?
```

Vasubandhu says no. A preceding citta transforms the support; the later absence of mental operation is designated as an attainment. The structure is causal and genetic, but the “attainment” need not be an additional entity.

### 4. Life as projected duration

The dispute over *jīvita* repeats the same pattern:

```text
Vaibhāṣika:
    lifespan is a distinct real dharma

Vasubandhu:
    lifespan is the karmically projected duration
    of a homogeneous continuum
```

Thus cessation and life are both explained through transformations and temporal determinations of the continuum rather than through added substances.

---

## Voices

- **Vaibhāṣika position:** both cessation-attainments and lifespan are distinct real dharmas; past citta can condition the returning citta.
- **Vasubandhu:** argues that the attainments are conceptual designations based on transformed non-operation; lifespan is karmically projected duration, not a separate substance.
- **Former teachers:** citta and the body endowed with faculties mutually contain one another’s seeds.
- **Venerable Vasumitra:** cessation-attainment contains citta.
- **Venerable Ghoṣaka:** rejects a conscious cessation-attainment.
- **Other school:** admits a fourth-dhyāna cessation-attainment.
- **Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣikas:** defend the summit-of-existence location and accept fall from the attainment and untimely death.

---

## New Technical Terms

- **vyutkrānta-samāpatti** — attainment entered in transposed order after mastery; absent from the kārikā; clarifies that fixed succession governs initial acquisition, not every later entry.
- **prakāma-vaśitva** — complete mastery over the attainments; Bhāṣya-only in this unit; permits omission of intermediate states.
- **samanantara-pratyaya** — immediately preceding condition; previously encountered Abhidharma causal category; central to the return-of-citta problem.
- **anyonyabījaka** — mutually containing one another’s seeds; Bhāṣya-only and doctrinally important; describes the reciprocal causal relation of citta and embodied support.
- **acittika / sacittika samāpatti** — attainment without citta / with citta; Bhāṣya-only controversy over cessation.
- **citta-pravṛtty-apravṛtti** — operation/non-operation of citta; Bhāṣya analytical language; supports the prajñapti interpretation.
- **āśraya-samāpādana** — bringing the support into a determinate condition; Bhāṣya-only functional definition of attainment.
- **sthiti-kālāvedha** — projection or determination of a duration of persistence; Bhāṣya-only; Vasubandhu’s account of lifespan.
- **nikāya-sabhāga** — homogeneous class-continuum; previously encountered in relation to commonality; here the continuum whose duration is karmically projected.
- **akāla-mṛtyu** — untimely death; Bhāṣya-only in this unit; death before the ordinary completion of lifespan through destructive conditions.

---

## Translation Consequences

1. **Samāpatti should remain “attainment,” not merely “trance.”** The Bhāṣya explains it through entering, bringing the support into equilibrium, and transforming the conditions of mental operation.
2. **Nirodha here is determinate suspension.** It is not the unconditioned cessation identical with nirvāṇa, but a conditioned attainment whose value depends on noble genesis and purpose.
3. **Bīja is explicitly causal.** The phrase *anyonyabījaka* makes the body endowed with faculties and citta reciprocal seed-bearers. This is stronger than a metaphor of residue.
4. **Jīvita is best translated “life” or “lifespan,” with its technical meaning stated.** Vasubandhu understands it as projected duration, whereas the Vaibhāṣika treats it as a distinct dharma.
5. **Prajñapti does not mean unreal in the sense of useless.** A designated attainment or lifespan remains causally and practically exact, though it is not a separate substance.

---

## Doctrinal Determination

VAK 2.44 reveals a consistent Vasubandhian strategy:

```text
experienced structural fact
    does not automatically imply
separate ontological substance
```

The cessation of mental operation is real as an event-structure. The persistence of life is real as temporal continuity. But both may be explained through the transformed continuum and its causal projection.

This gives the chapter a unified Dharma theory:

```text
prāpti
    status of capacity in a continuum

samāpatti
    transformation governing operation or non-operation

jīvita
    projected duration of the continuum
```

## Limited Organon Interpretation

The passage sharply distinguishes **abstract state identity** from **genetic truth**. The two attainments share the predicate “cessation of citta and caittas,” but they are not therefore spiritually identical. Their level, preparation, continuum, result, and mode of acquisition determine what the cessation means.

For Organon, this suggests:

```text
A state is not known by its extensional profile alone.
Its Concept includes the genesis by which it was produced.
```

The seed formulation is especially important:

```text
citta and embodied support
    are reciprocal seed-bearers
```

This is not yet an Absolute Knowing. It is a Dharma account of how conditioned cognition can disappear and return without appealing to an enduring self. The conditioned continuum contains the principial capacity for renewed cognition.

The later “Organon Light” pass should therefore preserve three distinctions:

1. cessation as mere non-operation;
2. cessation as noble, knowledge-generated attainment;
3. unconditioned cessation as liberation.

Their surface resemblance must not erase their different genetic laws.
