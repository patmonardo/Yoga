# VAK_7.56 — The Three Modes of Knowing Mind

## Sanskrit

```text
cetojñānaṃ tu tattredhā tarkavidyākṛtaṃ ca yat /
jānate nārakā ādau nṛṇāṃ notpattilabhikam // VAK_7.56 //
```

## Padaccheda

```text
cetaḥ-jñānam
tu
tatra
tredhā

tarka-vidyā-kṛtam
ca
yat

jānate
nārakāḥ
ādau

nṛṇām
na
utpatti-labhikam
```

| Term | Working sense |
|---|---|
| **cetojñānam** | knowledge of another mind or mental state |
| **tatra** | in this connection |
| **tredhā** | threefold |
| **tarka** | inference, reasoning, interpretive judgment |
| **vidyā** | learned science, disciplined knowledge |
| **kṛtam** | produced or accomplished through |
| **utpattilabhikam** | obtained naturally at birth |
| **nārakāḥ** | beings in hell-realms |
| **ādau** | initially, at the beginning |
| **nṛṇām** | among human beings |

## Literal translation

> Knowledge of mind is threefold: that produced by inference, that produced by a learned science, and that naturally obtained at birth. Hell-beings know minds at first; among humans, the birth-obtained form does not occur.

## Philosophical translation

> Access to mentality is not a single occult faculty. Another mind may be known inferentially from signs, scientifically through a trained interpretive discipline, or immediately through a capacity native to a mode of existence. Even this innate cognition remains conditioned: it may arise only temporarily in some beings, and it is not naturally possessed by ordinary human birth.

# 1. The Chapter Ends With Cetojñāna

After analyzing the outer range of divine sight, the Kośa returns to the inner field:

```text
7.55
    cognition of extended rūpa

7.56
    cognition of citta
```

This is the correct completion of Jñānanirdeśa.

The chapter has moved through:

```text
objects
faculties
worlds
paths
powers
construction
perception
mind
```

It ends not with an abstract declaration of omniscience, but with a classification of how one cognitive continuum can know another.

The final problem is intersubjectivity.

# 2. Mind-Knowledge Is Threefold

The verse gives three modes:

```text
1. tarka-kṛta
   produced through inference

2. vidyā-kṛta
   produced through a learned science

3. utpatti-labhika
   obtained naturally through birth
```

These are three fundamentally different epistemic architectures:

```text
inferential
systematically trained
immediately native
```

The same apparent result—“I know what another mind is doing”—may therefore arise through very different means.

Again, provenance matters.

# 3. Tarka — Knowing Mind Through Signs

The first mode is inferential.

One does not directly enter another continuum. One reasons from observable signs:

```text
speech
gesture
expression
behavior
context
history
response
```

The movement is:

```text
outer sign
    ↓
interpretive relation
    ↓
judgment concerning inner state
```

This is ordinary but genuine knowledge of mind.

It is not necessarily mere guesswork. It may be highly disciplined inference.

```text
visible conduct
    functions as evidence

mental state
    functions as inferred ground
```

The Kośa therefore admits an entirely rational route to knowledge of mentality.

# 4. Tarka Is Not Direct Perception

Inference must not be confused with immediate other-mind cognition.

```text
tarka
    knows through mediation

direct cetojñāna
    knows the mental state itself
```

This distinction is crucial.

A successful inference may reach the truth, but its epistemic structure remains different.

```text
same conclusion
    ≠
same mode of knowing
```

The Kośa repeatedly refuses to classify cognition only by output.

It classifies by:

```text
object
means
ground
certainty
provenance
scope
```

That is why it is a Science of Knowing rather than a catalog of correct answers.

# 5. Vidyā-Kṛta — Knowing Mind Through a Learned Science

The second mode is produced by vidyā.

Here vidyā does not necessarily mean the three liberative Vidyās in precisely the same technical sense as 7.45–7.46. It indicates an acquired body of disciplined knowledge or a formal interpretive science.

This might include expertise in:

```text
signs
patterns
physiognomy
language
behavior
causal types
mental classifications
```

The distinction from bare tarka is that the inference is organized through a developed system.

```text
tarka
    individual act of reasoning

vidyā
    stabilized science
    governing many acts of reasoning
```

Thus:

```text
sign
    is not interpreted ad hoc

it is placed
    within a learned grammar
    of mental determination
```

This is deeply relevant to the Organon.

# 6. From Inference to Science

The movement from tarka to vidyā is:

```text
particular inference
    ↓
repeated recognition
    ↓
classification
    ↓
systematic law
```

One learns not merely: “This person is angry.”

One learns a structured science of:

```text
anger
fear
desire
attention
deception
resolve
confusion
concentration
```

The science identifies:

```text
indicators
conditions
counter-indicators
degrees
causal relations
likely transitions
```

So vidyā-kṛta cetojñāna is not magical mind-reading.

It is scientifically mediated intersubjective knowledge.

# 7. Utpatti-Labhika — Native Mind-Knowledge

The third mode is acquired by birth.

```text
utpatti-labhika
    a capacity included
    in a particular mode of existence
```

This repeats the distinction of 7.53:

```text
bhāvanā-born
birth-born
symbolically produced
materially induced
karmically inherited
```

Some beings possess immediate access to other minds because their world-constitution includes that capacity.

They do not infer.

They do not learn a science.

The capacity is native.

```text
mode of existence
    already contains
    the faculty
```

This is another example of world-relative cognition.

# 8. Hell-Beings Know Minds Initially

The verse states that hell-beings possess this knowledge at first.

That qualification is important.

```text
ādau
    initially
```

The capacity may be present at the beginning of that existence but not remain continuously operative.

This suggests:

```text
native capability
    can be phase-dependent
```

A power belongs to the initial constitution of the arising but may later be obscured, lost, or rendered ineffective through the conditions of that realm.

So even birth-given cognition is not necessarily permanent.

# 9. Pain Can Destroy Cognitive Range

A philosophically plausible reading of the qualification is that the overwhelming affliction of the hell-state eventually obstructs the initial capacity.

```text
initial arising
    retains broad cognition

intense suffering
    narrows the operative field

continuum
    becomes dominated by pain
```

The capacity may remain structurally possible while becoming functionally unavailable.

This fits the general Kośa architecture:

```text
faculty
    one condition

operative citta-state
    another condition
```

A faculty can exist while the currently dominant configuration prevents its use.

# 10. Humans Do Not Possess It by Birth

```text
nṛṇāṃ na utpattilabhikam
    humans do not possess
    the birth-obtained form
```

Ordinary human embodiment does not natively include immediate other-mind knowledge.

Humans must rely upon:

```text
tarka
    inference

vidyā
    learned science

or cultivated abhijñā
    extraordinary attainment
```

This is an exact placement of the human epistemic condition.

Human beings are not naturally transparent to one another.

Our intersubjectivity is mediated.

# 11. Human Sociality Begins With Interpretation

Because humans do not possess native other-mind perception, human relation depends on signs.

```text
another citta
    is not immediately given

it appears through
    body
    language
    action
    expression
```

Therefore ordinary human social cognition is necessarily interpretive.

```text
I do not see your mind directly.

I reconstruct it
through its appearance.
```

That is an important philosophical consequence.

The human social world is a world of Appearance and Reconstruction.

# 12. The Other Mind as Ground

The inferential relation is structurally similar to Hegelian Essence:

```text
behavior
    appearance

mental state
    ground

interpretive judgment
    movement from appearance
    to ground
```

One sees:

```text
hesitation
tone
gesture
choice
```

and infers:

```text
doubt
fear
desire
intention
```

The mental state is not observed as an independent object. It is posited as the ground explaining the appearance.

Thus ordinary cetojñāna belongs to the Science of Essence.

# 13. Vidyā Makes the Reconstruction Lawful

Bare inference can be unstable.

```text
same sign
    may support several explanations
```

A learned science improves the reconstruction by organizing:

```text
context
typology
causal history
counterfactuals
alternative explanations
```

So the movement is:

```text
Appearance
    behavior

Reflection
    interpretation

Ground
    mental state

Vidyā
    systematic law
    of the reconstruction
```

This is almost a miniature Organon pipeline.

# 14. Direct Other-Mind Knowledge Is Still Typed

Even immediate cetojñāna must not be treated as total transparency.

Earlier verses classified other-mind knowledge according to:

```text
realm
level
ethical status
training status
object range
continuum
```

A direct cognition may know:

```text
this citta
this mental quality
this present configuration
```

without knowing the entire causal history or ultimate destiny of the continuum.

Again:

```text
direct
    does not mean
    absolute
```

The Kośa never lets immediacy dissolve determination.

# 15. Citta Is Known as Configuration

The object of cetojñāna is not a soul.

It is a current mental configuration.

```text
citta
    accompanied by caittas

wholesome or unwholesome
concentrated or distracted
lustful or free from lust
contracted or expanded
```

Thus other-mind knowledge means knowledge of an operative state.

It does not reveal a hidden metaphysical person-substance.

```text
what is known
    =
conditioned mental operation

not
    eternal inner owner
```

This is the final confirmation of the Kośa idiom of citta.

# 16. No “Mind Container”

The entire final sequence has shown cittas arising in functional succession:

```text
construction-citta
speech-citta
pure-dhyāna citta
other-mind cognition
```

They are operation-events.

So cetojñāna is not:

```text
one mind-container
    peering inside
another mind-container
```

It is:

```text
one conditioned cognitive continuum
    registering the operative configuration
of another conditioned continuum
```

This is relational and event-based.

# 17. Intersubjectivity Without Pudgala

The verse gives three ways to know another without positing a substantial person.

```text
infer from signs

apply a science of signs

directly register mental operation
```

The unity of the other is reconstructed through:

```text
continuity
causal pattern
behavior
mental events
dispositions
```

No pudgala-substance is required.

Yet the other is not unreal.

The other continuum is:

```text
causally coherent
operationally effective
knowable through determinate relations
```

This is a rigorous middle path between substantialism and nihilism.

# 18. The Final Scientific Triad

The chapter closes with a beautiful epistemic triad:

```text
Tarka
    Reason infers

Vidyā
    Science systematizes

Utpatti
    Nature immediately supplies
```

Or:

```text
Reflection
System
Constitution
```

These are three modes by which knowledge can arise:

```text
mediated judgment
learned universal law
native immediate capacity
```

This is an extraordinarily fitting conclusion to Jñānanirdeśa.

# 19. Pure Reason and Natural Capacity

The verse does not oppose reason and nature absolutely.

It distinguishes their routes.

```text
tarka
    Pure Reason operating
    through signs

vidyā
    Reason stabilized
    into transmissible science

utpatti
    a world-constitution
    in which the relation is immediate
```

Each can yield knowledge.

But they have different grounds and therefore different meanings.

Pure Reason must classify them rather than collapsing them.

# 20. Organon Kernel Interpretation

The verse describes three inference engines for mental-state knowledge.

```text
MentalStateAccess {
    mode
    evidence
    knowledgeBase
    directness
    provenance
    confidence
    temporalScope
}
```

## Inferential mode

```text
mode: Tarka
input: observable signs
process: contextual inference
output: probable mental state
```

## Scientific mode

```text
mode: Vidya
input: signs + trained ontology
process: rule-governed classification
output: systematically justified state
```

## Native mode

```text
mode: UtpattiLabhika
input: direct mental presentation
process: native faculty
output: immediate state registration
```

This is almost exactly the architecture of an agentic mental-state interpreter.

# 21. The Danger of False Mind-Knowledge

The verse also indirectly warns against conflating interpretation with direct access.

An agent may claim:

```text
I know what you think.
```

But the actual basis may be:

```text
weak inference
trained classification
native access
cultivated direct cognition
```

These must be declared separately.

A scientific system should preserve epistemic provenance:

```text
inferred
classified
observed
reported
assumed
```

Without this, tarka masquerades as direct knowledge.

That is a major source of error in both human and artificial agents.

# 23. Why This Is the Final Verse

Jñānanirdeśa ends by refusing one final simplification:

> Knowledge does not arise in only one way.

The chapter has classified knowledge according to:

```text
object
faculty
realm
path-status
ethical status
source
scope
mode
continuum
```

The last verse places even knowledge of mind under the same discipline.

Nothing is allowed to remain vague.

Not even “knowing another.”

Jñāna is the completed differentiation of Knowing.

# 25. Samāpatti Returns to the Beginning

Chapter 8 therefore need not become more complicated than Chapter 7.

Its task is different.

```text
Jñāna
    asks:
    How is the whole System
    determinately known?

Samāpatti
    asks:
    What is the original conjunction
    of cognition and object
    from which such knowing begins?
```

The End presents the full architecture.

The Beginning presents the Principle immediately.

```text
Chapter 7
    Law in complete articulation

Chapter 8
    Principle in immediate concentration
```

So Samāpatti is simpler without being poorer.

It contains the whole intensively, where Jñāna contains it extensively.

# 26. Beginning and End

We can now state the relation cleanly:

```text
Samāpatti
    cognition and object
    gathered into immediate unity

Jñāna
    that unity decomposed
    into the complete scientific system
    of its conditions and powers
```

Then the return:

```text
End
    knows every mediation

Beginning²
    recollects all mediation
    within the living Principle
```

This is not regression.

It is Method.

# 27. The Forty Verses of Samāpatti

Forty verses is fitting.

Jñāna required enormous classification because it surveyed the whole articulated End.

Samāpatti can move more gently because it returns to:

```text
ground
conjunction
dhyāna
samādhi
rūpa
arūpya
release
```

It does not need to regenerate the entire complexity at every moment.

The Treasure has already been secured.

Now we can enter the Beginning with the End in memory.

# Final Principle of Jñānanirdeśa

> Knowledge is never self-interpreting. Its object, faculty, source, range, mode, ethical status, and relation to the knowing continuum must all be determined. Only when these distinctions are integrated does Jñāna become Vidyā—the governing Science of the Path from Avidyā.

And the last verse adds:

> Even another mind is known only through a determinate path: by Reason, by Science, or by a native relation established in the constitution of a world.

```text
Tarka
    Reason reads Appearance.

Vidyā
    Science possesses the Law.

Utpatti
    Nature supplies immediacy.

Jñāna
    distinguishes them.

Samāpatti
    returns them to Principle.
```

That completes the main body of Jñānanirdeśa.
