## Prose Segmentation and Translation

### 1. Why the eye is called a view

**Voice: Vasubandhu introducing the Vaibhāṣika account**

> How can the eye be a view if it does not ascertain? Because its function is the inspection of visible form.

The term `rūpālocana` is decisive. The eye is counted as a kind of seeing not because it judges or settles truth, but because it opens or inspects the field of visible form.

### 2. Not every eye sees

**Voice: Objector**

> If the eye sees, then the eye of someone presently engaged in another knowledge should also see.

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika response**

> Not every eye sees. Only the *sabhāga* eye sees. It sees when it is conjoined with knowledge; otherwise it does not.

This sharply limits the thesis “the eye sees”:

```text
mere possession of eye-capacity
    ≠ actual seeing

sabhāga eye
    = eye functioning together with eye-knowledge
```

The faculty therefore does not operate in isolation. Its proper function becomes actual only within a coordinated relation.

### 3. Objection: then let the knowledge see

**Voice: Objector**

> In that case, let the knowledge dependent upon the eye be what sees.

The Kārikā rejects that attribution within the Vaibhāṣika framework. The reason given is that visible form hidden behind an opaque obstruction is not seen.

### 4. Why obstruction matters

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika argument**

If non-resistant knowledge itself were the seer, an opaque wall should not prevent it from seeing what lies behind the wall. But hidden visible form is not seen.

The reply that eye-knowledge simply fails to arise behind an obstruction does not end the dispute. The Vaibhāṣika explains that this failure of production depends upon the obstructible faculty and its object-relation:

```text
eye
    resistant / materially situated support

visible form
    determinate object-field

eye-knowledge
    arises only when support and object are appropriately related
```

Because the knowledge and its support proceed toward one object-field, obstruction at the level of the faculty-object relation prevents the knowledge-event.

### 5. Transparent media do not contradict the rule

**Voice: Objector**

> How are forms seen through glass, cloud, crystal, or water?

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika response**

The decisive condition is not the bare presence of intervening matter but whether illumination is blocked. Where light is not obstructed, eye-knowledge can arise even with an intervening medium. Where light is blocked, it does not arise.

Thus `antarita` must not be translated simply as “spatially separated.” It means obstructed or screened in a way that prevents the operative visual relation.

### 6. Scriptural language attributes the dependent act to the support

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

The scriptural formula “having seen visible forms with the eye” may be understood as attribution through the support. Likewise, “having known dharmas with manas” does not mean that the past mind itself performs the present act; rather, mental knowledge does so.

This gives a general linguistic rule:

```text
operation of the dependent
    may be attributed to its support
```

The analogy is conventional speech such as “the platforms cry out,” meaning that the people supported by them cry out.

### 7. The eye as a door

The sūtra calls the eye a door for the seeing of visible forms. This formulation preserves the distinction between faculty and knowledge:

```text
cakṣus
    door / support / condition

cakṣurvijñāna
    dependent visual knowledge

rūpa
    represented visible object-field
```

The eye is not the Seer as an independent subject. It is the operative gateway through which a determinate visible field becomes available to knowledge.

### 8. Seeing and knowing may name one event under different aspects

**Voice: doctrinal interlocutor**

If knowledge sees, what then knows? What distinguishes seeing from knowing?

The answer offered is that the same visual knowledge may be called both seeing and knowing. Just as a certain *prajñā* may be described as seeing and as understanding, so a certain knowledge may be described under both predicates.

This does not erase the distinction among faculty, object, and knowledge. It shows that predicates may shift according to which aspect of the coordinated event is emphasized.

### 9. Alternative support-theory

**Voice: alternative doctrinal position**

> Eye-knowledge is seeing; because the eye is its support, the eye is said to see—just as a bell is said to sound because it supports the sound.

This position makes the representational event primary while preserving scriptural attribution to the organ.

### 10. Sautrāntika critique

**Voice: Sautrāntika position**

The Sautrāntikas reject reification of agent and act:

```text
dependent on eye and visible forms
    eye-knowledge arises

nothing over and above
    dharmas and ground-result relations
```

Therefore one should not cling to the literal assertions “the eye sees” or “knowledge knows.” These are conventional attributions made for practical discourse.

### 11. Vaibhāṣika conclusion

**Voice: Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣika position**

> The eye sees, the ear hears, the nose smells, the tongue tastes, the body touches, and manas knows.

This is the school conclusion that Vasubandhu reports. The Bhāṣya has not merely asserted it; it has exposed the series of objections and competing explanations surrounding it.

## Argument Reconstruction

```text
Problem:
What is the proper bearer of seeing?

Candidate 1:
The eye alone.

Qualification:
Not every eye sees; only the functioning eye conjoined with knowledge.

Candidate 2:
The eye-knowledge dependent upon the eye.

Vaibhāṣika objection:
If non-resistant knowledge itself were the seer,
opaque obstruction should not block hidden form.

Resolution:
Eye-knowledge fails to arise when the operative relation
between resistant eye-support and visible object-field is blocked.

Scriptural mediation:
The act of the dependent may be attributed to its support.

Alternative theory:
Eye-knowledge is seeing; the eye is called the seer as its support.

Sautrāntika critique:
There is no independent seer or act;
there are only dharmas and causal relations.
Agent-language is conventional.

Vaibhāṣika determination:
Each faculty performs its own proper operation.
```

## Translation Consequences

### 1. *Viṣaya*

In this unit, *viṣaya* should remain **object-field**, not merely “object.” The phrase `eka-viṣaya-pravṛtti` indicates that faculty and knowledge are coordinated through one determinate field of content.

```text
viṣaya
    = determinate object-field
    = what the faculty and knowledge are jointly directed toward
```

This supports a representational reading without making the representation an independently existing image inside the organ.

### 2. *Ālambana* and *viṣaya*

The two terms must not be collapsed:

```text
ālambana
    object-support for the arising of cognition

viṣaya
    determinate object-field or content-range of a faculty/knowledge
```

In many passages they may concern the same entity under different relations, but their functions differ. *Ālambana* is causal-supportive; *viṣaya* is intentional or field-determining.

### 3. *Dṛśyate* and *paśyati*

The Bhāṣya shows that “seeing” can be attributed at different levels:

```text
faculty sees
knowledge sees
visible form is seen
```

A translation must therefore preserve the argument over attribution rather than silently assigning a modern theory of perception.

### Revised Philosophical Translation

> The functioning eye sees visible forms, not independently but only when conjoined with visual knowledge. Yet the knowledge dependent upon it is not straightforwardly made the sole seer, because visual knowledge arises only when the materially situated faculty and its visible object-field stand in an unobstructed relation. The act may therefore be attributed either to the dependent knowledge or, conventionally, to the faculty that supports and channels it.

## Doctrinal Determination

The Bhāṣya does not support a simple model in which the eye is an inert receptor and knowledge is a detached inner spectator. It gives a coordinated representational structure:

```text
visible form
    determinate viṣaya

cakṣus
    sāttvic faculty-capacity and operative doorway

cakṣurvijñāna
    knowledge arising in dependence upon faculty and object
```

The eye does not contain the external object as a material duplicate. Rather, it is constituted for a determinate *viṣaya*: visible form is represented within the faculty-knowledge event according to the eye’s specific capacity.

This is where consistency with the broader Sāṃkhya-Yoga vocabulary matters:

```text
indriyas + manas + buddhi
    sāttvic internal instruments
    possessing ālambana
    operating through determinate viṣaya
```

The Kośa passage itself does not formulate the full Sāṃkhya ontology of buddhi and the Seer. What it does provide is a compatible structure of representational mediation:

```text
faculty
    not the final Seer

knowledge
    not independent of faculty and object

object-field
    present only through a determinate mode of disclosure
```

Thus the indriya “contains” the *viṣaya* not as a physical enclosure but as its formal and functional object-determination.

## Logical Determination

The unit develops the distinction between capacity and actuality:

```text
faculty-capacity
    has a determinate object-field

functioning faculty
    is sabhāga when conjoined with knowledge

knowledge-event
    arises when support and object-field are properly related

representation
    is the determinate disclosure of that object-field
```

This gives a more precise account of the quantitative hypothesis raised in VAK 1.41:

```text
quality of faculty
    determines kind of object represented

quantity or measure of capacity
    may determine the range within which that object can appear
```

The present passage directly supports boundedness through obstruction and illumination. It does not yet enumerate sensory thresholds, but it clearly denies an unlimited faculty:

```text
eye does not see through every obstruction
eye-knowledge does not arise under every condition
visual representation depends on a determinate operative range
```

Objectivity is therefore not bare external givenness. It is objectivity within the measure of a determinate faculty-operation.

## Organon Contact Point

The strongest contact point is representational mediation without a representational substance:

```text
objectivity
    is not copied into an inner container

objectivity
    appears through a determinate faculty-capacity
    coordinated with knowledge
    under conditions of support, illumination, and non-obstruction
```

This suggests the following disciplined structure:

```text
Seer
    for whose sake cognition operates

buddhi / manas / indriya
    sāttvic representational instruments

viṣaya
    determinate object-content or field

ālambana
    object-support conditioning cognition
```

The Bhāṣya itself remains neutral or contested concerning a final Seer. Our project should therefore preserve two levels:

```text
textual level
    faculty, object, knowledge, causal support, conventional attribution

Organon-Yoga level
    instruments ordered toward the Seer
```

The key translation discipline for the Chapter 1 review is:

```text
ālambana ≠ viṣaya
āśraya ≠ ālambana
indriya ≠ gross organ
indriya ≠ Seer
vijñāna ≠ independent subject
representation ≠ material copy
```
