# VAK_1.10 Bhāṣya

## Differentiation of the Five Material Object-Domains

This file records the Bhāṣya-pass study for `VAK_1.10.md`.

The Kārikā file remains unchanged in this commit. Where the Bhāṣya requires a correction or refinement to the first-pass translation, that decision is recorded here for a later Kārikā revision pass.

---

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

### IAST

> rūpaṃ dvidhā viṃśatidhā śabdas tv aṣṭavidhaḥ rasaḥ /
>
> ṣoḍhā caturvidho gandhaḥ spṛśyam ekādaśātmakam // VAkK_1.10 //

### Bhāṣya-informed translation

> Visible form is twofold and, in further enumeration, twentyfold. Sound is eightfold; taste is sixfold; smell is fourfold; and the tangible has eleven kinds as its nature.

The word `rūpa` here does not mean the whole rūpa aggregate. It means the visible object-domain, `rūpāyatana`, introduced among the five material objects in VAK 1.09.

---

## 2. Natural Bhāṣya Unit

The natural unit begins after the five faculties have been defined and the Bhāṣya turns to the five objects:

> *arthāḥ pañca nirdeśyāḥ. tatra tāvat...*

It proceeds through the complete analysis of:

```text
visible form
sound
taste
smell
tangible
```

and closes with the discussion of whether a sensory cognition arises from one material factor or from a collection of factors.

The unit therefore has two levels:

```text
classification of each object-domain
    ↓
explanation of how cognition takes that domain as object
```

This makes VAK 1.10 more than a list. It is an analysis of the internal structure of sensory object-fields and their relation to vijñāna.

---

## 3. Bhāṣya Analysis

### A. Visible form is twofold

> *rūpaṃ dvidhā.*
>
> *varṇaḥ saṃsthānaṃ ca.*

### Translation

> Visible form is twofold: color and configuration.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The first division is:

```text
rūpāyatana
    = varṇa
    + saṃsthāna
```

`Varṇa` is visible color or chromatic appearance. `Saṃsthāna` is configuration, shape, or spatial arrangement.

This distinction is important because the object-domain is not treated as an undifferentiated visible thing. It contains at least two ways in which something is visually determined:

```text
how it appears chromatically
how it is configured spatially
```

---

### B. Color and configuration

> *tatra varṇaś caturvidho nīlādiḥ. tadbhedā anye.*

### Translation

> Color is fourfold, beginning with blue; the other colors are subdivisions of these.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The four basic colors are:

```text
blue
yellow
red
white
```

The remaining color determinations are treated as variations or subdivisions of these primary forms.

> *saṃsthānam aṣṭavidhaṃ dīrghādi visātāntam.*

### Translation

> Configuration is eightfold, beginning with long and ending with irregular.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The eight configurations are:

```text
long
short
round
circular
raised
lowered
even
uneven
```

The distinction between color and configuration gives the visible domain an internal grammar rather than reducing sight to reception of a simple object.

---

### C. The twentyfold visible domain

> *tad eva rūpāyatanaṃ punar ucyate viṃśatidhā.*

### Translation

> That same visible object-domain is further said to be twentyfold.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The twenty are:

```text
blue
yellow
red
white
long
short
round
circular
raised
lowered
even
uneven
cloud
smoke
dust
mist
shadow
sunlight
light
darkness
```

The Bhāṣya then glosses several less obvious members:

```text
sāta
    even configuration

visāta
    uneven configuration

mahikā
    mist or fog

ātapa
    sunlight

āloka
    light from moon, stars, fire, herbs, or jewels

chāyā
    a condition in which forms remain visible

andhakāra
    the opposite condition, in which forms are not visible
```

The visible domain therefore includes not only determinate colors and shapes, but also environmental conditions of visibility.

That is already significant for a Knowledge Domain interpretation. The domain of sight includes both visible determinations and the conditions under which visibility is possible or impossible.

---

### D. A variant enumeration

> *kecin nabhaś caikavarṇam iti ekaviṃśatiṃ saṃpaṭhanti.*

### Translation

> Some also count the sky as a single color and thus recite twenty-one.

**Voice:** uncertain school position

The Bhāṣya records an alternative enumeration without endorsing it as the principal count.

This is a useful reminder that the classificatory system preserves intra-scholastic variants rather than pretending that every list is uncontested.

---

### E. Color without configuration, configuration without color

> *asti rūpāyatanaṃ varṇato vidyate na saṃsthānataḥ...*

### Translation

> There is visible form that is known by color but not by configuration.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

Examples include:

```text
blue
yellow
red
white
shadow
sunlight
light
darkness
```

> *asti saṃsthānato na varṇataḥ.*

### Translation

> There is visible form known by configuration but not by color.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The example is the spatial delimitation involved in bodily expression or bodily manifestation.

> *asty ubhayathā. pariśiṣṭaṃ rūpāyatanam.*

### Translation

> There is also visible form known in both ways; this is the remainder of the visible object-domain.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The point is not that color and configuration are always inseparable. The Bhāṣya distinguishes cases in which cognition determines one, the other, or both.

---

### F. A dispute about whether one entity can be known in both ways

> *kathaṃ punar ekaṃ dravyam ubhayathā vidyate?*

### Translation

> But how can one entity be known in both ways?

**Voice:** Objector

> *asty ubhayasya tatra prajñānāt.*

### Translation

> It is so because both are cognized there.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The objector worries that color and configuration would have to be two separate entities. The reply appeals to distinct cognition within one perceptual presentation.

The decisive clarification follows:

> *jñānārtho hy eṣa vidir na sattārthaḥ.*

### Translation

> For this verb “is known” concerns cognition, not existence.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

This is one of the most important sentences in the unit.

```text
vidyate here
    = is cognized / is discriminated

not

vidyate here
    = exists as a separately established entity
```

The classification is epistemic before it is ontological. It tells us how a field is cognized, not necessarily how many independent substances must be posited.

This gives direct support to reading the object-divisions as Knowledge Domains or modes of determination within cognition.

---

### G. Sound is eightfold

> *śabdas tv aṣṭavidhaḥ.*

### Translation

> Sound, however, is eightfold.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The first division gives four kinds:

```text
caused by appropriated great elements
caused by non-appropriated great elements
called sentient
called non-sentient
```

These become eight through the further distinction:

```text
agreeable
unagreeable
```

Thus:

```text
4 causal or source-types
    ×
2 affective values
    =
8 kinds of sound
```

---

### H. Appropriated and non-appropriated causes of sound

> *tatropāttamahābhūtahetuko yathā hastavākchabdaḥ.*

### Translation

> Sound caused by appropriated great elements is, for example, the sound of the hand or of speech.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

Here `upātta` means materiality appropriated as part of a living continuum.

> *anupāttamahābhūtahetuko yathā vāyuvanaspatinadīśabdaḥ.*

### Translation

> Sound caused by non-appropriated great elements is, for example, the sound of wind, trees, or rivers.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The sound-domain is therefore classified partly by the status of its material cause.

---

### I. Sentient and non-sentient sound

> *sattvākhyo vāgvijñaptiśabdaḥ.*

### Translation

> Sound called “sentient” is the sound that is vocal communication.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

`Vāgvijñapti` marks sound as an expressive act associated with a sentient agent.

> *asattvākhyo 'nyaḥ.*

### Translation

> The other sound is called non-sentient.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The distinction is not simply between sounds emitted by organisms and sounds emitted by non-organisms. The key case of sentient sound is communicative vocal manifestation.

---

### J. A disputed mixed cause of sound

> *upāttānupāttamahābhūtahetuko 'py asti śabda ity apare.*

### Translation

> Others say that there is also sound caused jointly by appropriated and non-appropriated great elements.

**Voice:** alternate school position

The example is:

> *hastamṛdaṅgasaṃyogaja*

> sound produced by the conjunction of hand and drum.

The Bhāṣya rejects treating this as one sound with a double material basis:

> *sa tu yathaikau varṇaparamāṇur na bhūtacatuṣkadvayam upādāyeṣyate tathā naivaiṣṭavyaḥ.*

### Translation

> But just as a single atom of color is not accepted as depending upon two sets of four great elements, this sound too should not be accepted in that way.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The response preserves a rule of material individuation: one determinate material event should not be assigned two independent elemental bases merely because two causal continuities meet in its production.

---

### K. Taste is sixfold

> *rasaḥ ṣoḍhā.*

### Translation

> Taste is sixfold.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The six tastes are:

```text
sweet
sour
salty
pungent
bitter
astringent
```

The Bhāṣya gives no further dispute here. The classification is treated as straightforward.

---

### L. Smell is fourfold

> *caturvidho gandhaḥ.*

### Translation

> Smell is fourfold.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The four arise through two distinctions:

```text
pleasant smell
unpleasant smell
```

and:

```text
even smell
uneven smell
```

The Bhāṣya notes that the śāstra elsewhere gives only three:

```text
pleasant
unpleasant
even
```

**Voice:** textual variant / śāstra citation

The present verse therefore uses a more differentiated fourfold enumeration.

---

### M. The tangible is elevenfold

> *spṛśyam ekādaśātmakam.*

### Translation

> The tangible has eleven kinds as its nature.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The eleven are:

```text
earth
water
fire
wind
smoothness
roughness
heaviness
lightness
cold
hunger
thirst
```

The first four are the great elements. The remaining seven are derivative tangible qualities.

---

### N. Smoothness, roughness, heaviness, and lightness

> *ślakṣṇatvaṃ mṛdutā.*

### Translation

> Smoothness is softness.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

> *karkaśatvaṃ paruṣatā.*

### Translation

> Roughness is harshness.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

> *gurutvaṃ yena bhāvās tulyante.*

### Translation

> Heaviness is that by which things are weighed.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

> *laghutvaṃ viparyayāt.*

### Translation

> Lightness is understood by the opposite.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

These are not abstract physical quantities. They are tangible determinations disclosed in bodily contact.

---

### O. Cold, hunger, and thirst are named through their effects

> *śītam uṣṇābhilāṣakṛt.*

### Translation

> Cold is that which produces desire for warmth.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

> *jighatsā bhojanābhilāṣakṛt.*

### Translation

> Hunger is that which produces desire for food.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

Thirst is understood correspondingly as producing desire for drink.

The Bhāṣya explains the naming rule:

> *kāraṇe kāryopacārāt.*

### Translation

> This is a figurative designation of the effect in the cause.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The terms “hunger” and “thirst” name tangible bodily conditions through the desires they produce.

This is a useful semantic caution. The list does not always name an item by its bare intrinsic structure; sometimes it names a cause through its experienced effect.

---

### P. Tangibles in the form realm

> *tatra rūpadhātau jighatsāpipāse na staḥ. śeṣam asti.*

### Translation

> In the form realm, hunger and thirst are absent; the remaining tangibles are present.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The Bhāṣya adds qualifications concerning heaviness and cold:

```text
individual garments may not be weighable there,
but accumulated garments may be weighed

injurious cold is absent,
but beneficial coolness is said to exist
```

**Voice:** Vasubandhu reporting doctrinal explanation

This preserves the elevenfold tangible domain while allowing realm-specific absence or transformation of particular members.

---

### Q. Does cognition arise from one object or many?

> *yad etad bahuvidhaṃ rūpam uktaṃ tatra kadācid ekena dravyeṇa cakṣurvijñānam utpadyate yadā tatprakāravyavacchedo bhavati.*

### Translation

> Among the many kinds of visible form just described, visual cognition sometimes arises from one material item, when there is discrimination of that particular kind.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

> *kadācid bahubhir yadā na vyavacchedaḥ.*

### Translation

> At other times it arises from many, when there is no such discrimination.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

Examples include seeing from a distance:

```text
a military formation composed of many colors and shapes
a collection of jewels
```

The same principle applies to auditory and the other sensory cognitions.

This is especially important for the Knowledge Domain interpretation:

```text
vijñāna may take
    one distinctly determined item
or
    a structured collection as one field
```

A domain is therefore not reducible to one atomic object. It may be a unified field of many material factors.

---

### R. How many tangibles generate body cognition?

One position states:

> *kāyavijñānaṃ tu paraṃ pañcabhiḥ spraṣṭavyair utpadyata ity eke.*

### Translation

> Some say that body cognition arises from at most five tangibles.

**Voice:** school position

These five are:

```text
the four great elements
plus one of smoothness and the other derivative qualities
```

Another position states:

> *sarvair ekādaśabhir ity apare.*

### Translation

> Others say that it can arise from all eleven.

**Voice:** alternate school position

The Bhāṣya preserves the disagreement rather than settling it immediately.

---

### S. Objection: would the five cognitions then have general objects?

> *nanu caivaṃ samastālambanatvāt sāmānyaviṣayāḥ pañca vijñānakāyāḥ prāpnuvanti na svalakṣaṇaviṣayāḥ.*

### Translation

> But if they take collections as their objects, would not the five groups of cognition have general objects rather than objects characterized by their own particular marks?

**Voice:** Objector

The concern is that cognition of a collection appears to be cognition of a universal or common object.

The reply is:

> *āyatanasvalakṣaṇaṃ praty ete svalakṣaṇaviṣayā iṣyante na dravyasvalakṣaṇam ity adoṣaḥ.*

### Translation

> They are accepted as having particular-character objects with respect to the particular character of the sense-domain, not with respect to the particular character of each individual substance; therefore there is no fault.

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

This is the decisive conclusion of the unit.

```text
svalakṣaṇa of the āyatana
    = the determinate character of the whole sensory domain

not necessarily

svalakṣaṇa of one isolated material substance
```

The sense cognition is particular because it belongs to a determinate domain—visible, audible, tangible, and so forth—even when many material factors compose the presented field.

This strongly supports treating the sensory divisions as Knowledge Domains rather than merely lists of external things.

---

## 4. Argument Summary

```text
The five material object-domains are internally differentiated.
        ↓
Visible form divides into color and configuration,
and is further enumerated as twenty modes.
        ↓
The classification concerns how the domain is cognized,
not necessarily how many independent entities exist.
        ↓
Sound is classified through material cause,
communicative status, and agreeable or disagreeable character.
        ↓
Taste, smell, and tangibility each possess their own modal structures.
        ↓
A sensory cognition may arise from one distinctly discriminated item
or from many factors presented as one field.
        ↓
Its object remains particular because the particularity belongs
to the determinate sense-domain, not necessarily to one isolated substance.
```

The central movement is:

```text
object enumeration
    → mode of cognition
    → domain-specific particularity
```

## 5. New Technical Terms

## **varṇa**

- **Provisional meaning:** color or chromatic appearance
- **Status:** introduced in the Bhāṣya analysis of VAK 1.10
- **Function:** first fundamental division of the visible object-domain

## **saṃsthāna**

- **Provisional meaning:** configuration, shape, spatial arrangement
- **Status:** introduced in the Bhāṣya analysis of VAK 1.10
- **Function:** second fundamental division of the visible object-domain

## **rūpāyatana**

- **Provisional meaning:** visible-form domain
- **Status:** implicit in the Kārikā; explicit and central in the Bhāṣya
- **Function:** names visible form as a determinate sensory domain rather than the entire rūpa aggregate

## **prajñāna**

- **Provisional meaning:** cognition or discriminative apprehension
- **Status:** Bhāṣya-only in this unit
- **Function:** explains how one presentation may be cognized through color, configuration, or both

## **jñānārtha**

- **Provisional meaning:** having cognition as its intended sense
- **Status:** Bhāṣya-only in this unit
- **Function:** clarifies that `vidyate` concerns being known, not asserting separate existence

## **upāttamahābhūtahetuka**

- **Provisional meaning:** caused by appropriated great elements
- **Status:** Bhāṣya-only in this unit
- **Function:** classifies sounds arising from materiality incorporated into a living continuum

## **anupāttamahābhūtahetuka**

- **Provisional meaning:** caused by non-appropriated great elements
- **Status:** Bhāṣya-only in this unit
- **Function:** classifies sounds such as those of wind, trees, and rivers

## **sattvākhya**

- **Provisional meaning:** designated as sentient
- **Status:** Bhāṣya-only in this unit
- **Function:** identifies communicative vocal sound

## **vāgvijñapti**

- **Provisional meaning:** vocal manifestation or communicative speech-expression
- **Status:** previously encountered conceptually; explicit here in sound classification
- **Function:** distinguishes sentient communicative sound from other sound

## **kāryopacāra**

- **Provisional meaning:** figuratively naming a cause by its effect
- **Status:** Bhāṣya-only in this unit
- **Function:** explains why bodily causes are called hunger and thirst through the desires they produce

## **āyatanasvalakṣaṇa**

- **Provisional meaning:** the particular character of a sensory domain
- **Status:** Bhāṣya-only in this unit
- **Function:** preserves the particularity of sense cognition even when its object-field contains many substances

## **dravyasvalakṣaṇa**

- **Provisional meaning:** the particular character of an individual substance
- **Status:** Bhāṣya-only in this unit
- **Function:** distinguished from domain-specific particularity

## 7. Kārikā Translation Decision

The first-pass translation is broadly correct, but several refinements should be preserved for the later revision.

### 1. `rūpa` here must be explicitly restricted

```text
rūpa in VAK 1.10
    = visible-form object-domain

not

rūpa in VAK 1.09
    = the whole rūpa aggregate category
```

### 2. `dvidhā viṃśatidhā` is hierarchical

The phrase should not suggest two unrelated counts. The Bhāṣya gives:

```text
first division
    color / configuration

further enumeration
    twenty modes
```

### 3. `spṛśyam ekādaśātmakam`

This is stronger than merely “the tangible is elevenfold.” It means:

> The tangible has eleven kinds as its constitutive nature.

### 4. The first-pass Organon rendering should be made more precise

The object-domain is not simply “internally articulated.” The Bhāṣya specifically establishes that:

```text
its articulations are modes of cognition
and
its particularity belongs to the domain as a domain
```

## 8. Provisional Organon Contact Point

The Bhāṣya gives substantial support to the project thesis that the dhātus and āyatanas are Knowledge Domains.

The strongest textual basis in this unit is:

> *jñānārtho hy eṣa vidir na sattārthaḥ*

> “This expression concerns cognition, not existence.”

And later:

> *āyatanasvalakṣaṇaṃ praty ete svalakṣaṇaviṣayā iṣyante na dravyasvalakṣaṇam*

> “They have particular objects with respect to the particular character of the domain, not of each individual substance.”

This yields a provisional project determination:

```text
āyatana
    = a domain with its own determinate mode of disclosure

vijñāna
    = knowing articulated according to that domain

svalakṣaṇa
    = the particularity of the domain as known
```

Your stronger claim can now be stated more sharply:

```text
the material dhātus are not merely external things

rather

they are determinate object-domains
whose identity is inseparable from the vijñānas
that disclose them
```

The present Bhāṣya supports this strongly at the level of sensory cognition.

It does not yet by itself prove that all dhātus ontologically “belong” to Vijñānaskandha or that Vijñānaskandha is already paramārthika supersensible Vidyā. That larger thesis remains a project interpretation to test against the formal eighteen-dhātu derivation and the later definition of vijñāna.
