## Prose Segmentation and Translation

### 1. Is seeing performed with one eye or both?

**Voice: Vasubandhu introducing the question**

> If the eye sees, does one see visible forms with one eye or with both?

The first answer is deliberately non-exclusive:

> There is no fixed rule here.

Seeing may occur with one eye or with both. The Kārikā emphasizes the two-eye case because the resulting vision is clearer.

### 2. The Abhidharmika explanation of binocular vision

**Voice: Abhidharmika position**

> One also sees with both eyes, because seeing is clearer.

When both eyes are open, the visual presentation is more purified or distinct. When one eye is closed and the other half-closed, one may apprehend two moons and similar doubled appearances.

The Bhāṣya rejects the idea that this doubling occurs because one of the two eyes has itself become intrinsically altered. The variation belongs to the coordinated conditions of visual presentation.

### 3. Knowledge is not spatially divided like form

**Voice: Vasubandhu or accepted Abhidharma explanation**

The interruption of one physical support does not entail a corresponding spatial division of eye-knowledge, because knowledge is not established in spatial location in the manner of material form.

This preserves three distinct levels:

```text
physical support
    spatially located and divisible

faculty
    operative capacity dependent upon support

knowledge-event
    not itself spatially distributed like material form
```

The Bhāṣya therefore resists both reduction of knowledge to gross anatomy and detachment of knowledge from the faculty-support complex.

### 4. Do the faculties operate only upon contacted objects?

**Voice: Vasubandhu introducing the question**

> If the eye sees, the ear hears, and mind knows, are their object-fields reached or unreached?

The answer divides the six faculties by mode of object-relation.

### 5. Eye, ear, and mind have unreached object-fields

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

> Eye, ear, and mind have objects that are not reached through contact.

The examples are exact:

```text
eye
    sees visible form at a distance
    but does not see collyrium placed directly upon the eye

ear
    hears sound from a distance

mind
    cannot physically contact its object because it is non-material
```

The point is not that these faculties lack an object. It is that their `viṣaya` does not need to be spatially contacted by the faculty.

### 6. Supernormal eye and ear confirm non-contact operation

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

If eye and ear required direct contact with their objects, the supernormal eye and ear produced in meditators could not arise and operate as taught.

The contrast is with smell and the other contact faculties.

### 7. Objection: why does the eye not see every unreached object?

**Voice: Objector**

> If the eye has an unreached object, why does it not see every unreached thing, including what is very distant or screened off?

This objection assumes that non-contact relation should imply unrestricted range.

### 8. Vasubandhu’s answer: a non-contact capacity is still bounded

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

The magnet does not attract every piece of iron merely because attraction occurs without direct contact.

Moreover, the same difficulty would arise under a contact theory. Why does the eye not see everything that directly touches it, such as collyrium or an applicator-stick?

Likewise, smell, taste, and touch have contacted object-fields, but they do not apprehend every object that is in contact with them. A faculty's mode of contact therefore does not determine an unlimited range of operation.

The conclusion is:

> The eye may have an unreached object, but not every unreached object.

This is one of the clearest Bhāṣya statements of **bounded faculty-capacity**.

### 9. Mind is necessarily non-contact

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

> Mind, because it is non-material, is incapable of physically reaching an object at all.

Mind's relation to dharma as `viṣaya` must therefore be representational or cognitive rather than spatially tactile.

### 10. Alternative view concerning hearing

**Voice: Alternative doctrinal position**

> Some hold that the ear has both contacted and non-contacted objects.

The Bhāṣya records the position without resolving it in this brief clause. Voice attribution beyond “some” is uncertain.

## Argument Reconstruction

```text
Question 1:
Does one see with one eye or both?

Answer:
There is no absolute restriction.
Both-eye seeing is emphasized because it yields clearer visual presentation.

Evidence:
Partial closure of the two eyes can generate doubled appearances.

Ontological qualification:
The physical supports are spatially divided,
but knowledge is not spatially established like form.

Question 2:
Do faculties require physical contact with their objects?

Determination:
Eye, ear, and mind have unreached object-fields.
Nose, tongue, and body operate otherwise.

Objection:
If the eye does not contact its object,
why does it not see every distant or screened object?

Resolution:
Non-contact does not imply unrestricted power.
A capacity is selective and conditioned.

Parallel argument:
Contact faculties also fail to apprehend every contacted object.

Final determination:
The mode of object-relation and the range of faculty-operation
are distinct determinations.
```

## Translation Consequences

### 1. *Viṣaya*

In this passage, `viṣaya` must be translated consistently as **object-field** or **determinate object-field**, not merely “object.” The term identifies the class and range of objects to which a faculty is ordered.

```text
aprāptaviṣaya
    = having an object-field not physically reached

prāptaviṣaya
    = having an object-field physically contacted
```

This rendering preserves both representational determination and functional range.

### 2. *Prāpta* and *aprāpta*

These should not be rendered as “present” and “absent.” The issue is physical reaching or contact.

Preferred renderings:

```text
prāpta
    contacted / physically reached

aprāpta
    non-contacted / physically unreached
```

### 3. *Darśana*

`vyaktadarśana` is best rendered “clear seeing” or “distinct visual presentation.” The Bhāṣya is discussing clarity of the manifested visual field, not yet decisive ascertainment in the stronger sense of `dṛṣṭi` from VAK 1.41.

### Revised Philosophical Translation

> One may see with both eyes, since their coordinated operation produces a clearer visual presentation. Eye, ear, and mind are ordered toward object-fields that they do not physically contact, whereas smell, taste, and touch require contact. Yet non-contact does not make a faculty unlimited: each faculty apprehends only the determinate object-field and range appropriate to its capacity.

## Doctrinal Determination

The Bhāṣya should lead the interpretation. Its direct doctrine is:

```text
faculty
    has a determinate viṣaya

mode of relation
    may be contact or non-contact

range of operation
    remains bounded in either case
```

This supports a representational reading without importing an external psychology. The faculty is not an empty receptor awaiting arbitrary content. It is intrinsically ordered toward a determinate object-field.

```text
cakṣus
    not merely a material organ
    not unrestricted seeing
    but a visual capacity ordered toward rūpa

śrotra
    an auditory capacity ordered toward śabda

manas
    a non-material capacity ordered toward dharma
```

The object-field is therefore contained in the faculty **formally and functionally**, not as a literal image stored inside it.

The Bhāṣya also establishes that representational capacity is conditioned:

```text
non-contact
    does not mean unlimited distance

contact
    does not mean apprehension of everything touching the faculty
```

The range belongs to the capacity itself and its enabling conditions.

## Logical Determination

The passage distinguishes four determinations that must not be collapsed:

```text
āśraya
    the support upon which the faculty depends

indriya
    the determinate capacity

viṣaya
    the object-field for which the capacity is constituted

vijñāna
    the knowledge-event arising through the relation
```

The relation can be represented as:

```text
support
    sustains
faculty-capacity
    is ordered toward
object-field
    and conditions
knowledge-event
```

This is representational, but not yet in the sense of a detached internal picture. Representation is the faculty's determinate ordering toward its `viṣaya`.

The binocular discussion adds an important result:

```text
plural material supports
    can coordinate one clearer object-presentation

without requiring
    knowledge itself to be spatially divided
```

Thus material multiplicity and cognitive unity are distinguishable without being externally separated.

---

## Limited Comparative Contact

A restrained comparison with Sāṃkhya may help identify the philosophical issue, but it must not govern the reading.

The useful comparison is only this:

```text
internal instrument
    is constituted for presentation of a determinate object-field
```

The Bhāṣya itself supplies the operative distinctions through `indriya`, `viṣaya`, contact/non-contact, and bounded range. It does not here teach the Sāṃkhya hierarchy of indriya–manas–buddhi or a Puruṣa for whose sake they operate. Those questions must remain open until the Kośa's own analysis clarifies them.

## Organon Contact Point

The strongest Organon result is that **Objectivity is capacity-bounded**.

```text
faculty-capacity
    determines kind of object
    mode of access
    and range of manifestation
```

The eye's objectivity is therefore not the whole of visible being without qualification. It is visible form as manifestable under determinate conditions of distance, obstruction, support, and coordinated operation.

This gives a disciplined form to the quantitative hypothesis:

```text
quality of faculty
    determines what kind of object can appear

quantity of capacity
    determines within what range it can appear
```

The second proposition remains an Organon development rather than an explicit Bhāṣya doctrine. The text directly supports bounded range, but does not yet formulate magnitude, threshold, or psychophysical measure.
