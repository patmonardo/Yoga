# VAK_3.43_bhasya — Lokanirdeśa

## Kārikā Anchor

**VAkK 3.43**


**Working translation:**

These two do not occur in one-pointed concentration or in a mindless state. The arhat becomes extinguished in one of two indeterminate minds. In gradual death, cessation of mind occurs at the feet, navel, or heart, according to the destination.

## Natural Argument

### 1. Death and rebirth do not occur in concentration or mindlessness

The opening `etau` continues the previous verse and refers to `cyuti` and `udbhava` / `upapatti`.

Vasubandhu explains that neither death nor rebirth occurs while the mind is concentrated (`samāhita`, `ekāgra`). He gives three reasons: concentrated mind belongs to a dissimilar level (`visabhāga-bhūmika`), is deliberately constructed or activated (`abhisaṃskārika`), and is supportive (`anugrāhaka`). These properties are incompatible with the death/rebirth transition being discussed.

Nor can death or rebirth occur in a mindless (`acitta`) state. When the bodily support begins to alter at death, a mind bound to that support must first become manifest and only afterward cease. Rebirth likewise cannot occur without mind, because there is no cause there for interruption of mind and because rebirth does not occur without affliction.

### 2. The arhat's final extinction occurs in an indeterminate mind

The Kārikā then concerns the arhat:

```iast
nirvāty avyākṛtadvaye
```

Vasubandhu explains the two possibilities as:

```text
airyāpathika-citta
    → mind connected with bodily deportment / ordinary activity

vipāka-citta
    → maturation-result mind
```

If there is a maturation equanimity in the desire realm, final extinction may occur in that mind; otherwise it occurs in an `airyāpathika` mind.

Why only in an indeterminate (`avyākṛta`) mind? Because such a mind is weak (`durbala`) and therefore favorable to the cutting off of the mental series (`citta-cheda-anukūla`).

### 3. Sudden death and gradual death

Vasubandhu next asks where in the body vijñāna ceases in one who is dying.

In sudden death (`sakṛn-maraṇa`), the bodily faculty together with mind disappears abruptly.

If death proceeds gradually (`krameṇa cyavate`), the bodily locus of the final cessation differs according to destination.

### 4. Locus of vijñāna-cessation in gradual death

The Bhāṣya supplies the continuation needed to understand the compact Kārikā:

```iast
adhonṛsuragājānām
```

These are, respectively:

```text
adhoga
    → one going downward, to an apāya

nṛga
    → one going to human birth

suraga
    → one going to deva birth

aja
    → one who will not be born again, an arhat
```

The corresponding loci are:

```text
adhoga
    → feet

nṛga
    → navel

suraga
    → heart

arhat
    → heart
```

A variant opinion says that for the arhat vijñāna ceases at the crown of the head (`mūrdhan`).

The sequence is therefore not a universal path through feet, navel, and heart. These are distinct loci correlated with different destinations.

### 5. Why these bodily loci?

The explanation is tied to the cessation of the bodily faculty (`kāyendriya`) at those places. Vasubandhu compares the contraction of the dying bodily faculty to water cast on a heated stone: it contracts and disappears progressively from the feet and other regions.

Thus gradual death is represented as a progressive withdrawal of the bodily faculty, with final vijñāna-cessation occurring at the locus appropriate to the destination.

### 6. `Marmaccheda`

Vasubandhu adds that gradual death commonly occurs in beings struck down through:

```text
marmaccheda
    injury to vital points

vedanā
    intense painful feeling
```

`Marman` is defined as a bodily region which, when severely damaged, brings about death.

These vital points are said to be as though cut when one of the water, fire, or wind domains (`ap`, `tejas`, `vāyu`) becomes excessively predominant, producing an effect like the impact of a sharp weapon and causing intense pain.

They are not literally cut like wood. They are called “cut” because, once affected in this way, they no longer function as before.

The earth domain is excluded because the traditional three bodily disorders are wind, bile, and phlegm, associated principally with wind, fire, and water respectively. Another explanation appeals to analogy with the dissolution of the container-world.

Vasubandhu closes this local discussion by stating that devas do not undergo `marmaccheda`.

## Literal Prose Translation

Even in mental knowledge, these two—death and arising—do not occur in one-pointed concentration or in a mindless state. A concentrated person does not die or arise in rebirth while concentrated, because concentration belongs to a dissimilar level, is deliberately produced, and is supportive. Nor does this occur in one who is mindless. When the bodily support begins to change, a mind bound to that support must necessarily first become manifest and afterward cease; it cannot be otherwise. And at rebirth, since there is no cause for interruption of mind and rebirth cannot occur without affliction, mindlessness is impossible.

It has been said that the death-existence is threefold. An arhat, however, becomes extinguished in one of two indeterminate minds: either a mind connected with bodily deportment or a maturation-result mind. If maturation equanimity exists in the desire realm, extinction may occur in that; if not, it occurs only in a deportment mind. Why only in an indeterminate mind? Because, through its weakness, it is favorable to the cutting off of the mental series.

In what bodily region does vijñāna cease in a dying person? In sudden death, the bodily faculty together with mind disappears abruptly. But if one dies gradually, the cessation of mind occurs at the feet, navel, or heart. For those going downward, going to humans, and going to devas, vijñāna ceases respectively at the feet, navel, and heart. Arhats, who are not born again, also have vijñāna cease at the heart; according to another opinion, at the crown of the head.

This is explained through the cessation of the bodily faculty at those loci. In a dying person the bodily faculty contracts and disappears progressively, like water cast upon a heated stone. Thus gradual death occurs.

For living beings, such gradual death commonly occurs through injury to vital points and through painful feeling. Certain bodily regions, when damaged, bring about death and are therefore called vital points. They are said to be “cut” when water, fire, or wind becomes excessively predominant, as though struck by a sharp weapon, producing intense pain. They are not literally cut like wood; they are called cut because they cease to function as before. The earth domain is not included, since the three bodily disorders are wind, bile, and phlegm, predominantly associated with wind, fire, and water. Another explanation appeals to similarity with the dissolution of the container-world. Devas do not undergo injury to vital points.

## Voice Identification

- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — explanatory prose throughout the main argument.
- **Voice: alternative position** — `mūrdhnīty apare`: “others say [for the arhat] at the crown of the head.”
- **Voice: alternative position** — `bhājanalokasaṃvarttanīsādharmyeṇety apare`: another explanation of why the earth domain is excluded from `marmaccheda`.

## New Technical Terms

- **visabhāga-bhūmika** — belonging to a dissimilar level
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-specific here
  - **function:** reason concentration is incompatible with death/rebirth transition

- **abhisaṃskārika** — deliberately constructed / intentionally activated
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-specific here
  - **function:** characterization of concentrated mind

- **anugrāhaka** — supportive / sustaining
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-specific here
  - **function:** characterization of concentrated mind

- **airyāpathika-citta** — mind connected with bodily deportment or ordinary activity
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-specific here
  - **function:** one of two indeterminate minds in which an arhat may become extinguished

- **citta-cheda-anukūla** — favorable to cutting off the mental series
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-specific formulation
  - **function:** explains why final extinction occurs in an indeterminate mind

- **sakṛn-maraṇa** — sudden death / death at once
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-specific distinction
  - **function:** contrast with gradual death

- **krama-cyuti** — gradual falling-away / gradual death
  - **status:** Kārikā term receiving Bhāṣya explanation
  - **function:** ordered cessation of bodily faculty and vijñāna

- **marman** — vital point
  - **status:** Kārikā/Bhāṣya term
  - **function:** bodily region whose severe injury causes death

- **marmaccheda** — injury or “cutting” of vital points
  - **status:** Bhāṣya technical explanation
  - **function:** common condition of gradual death

## Doctrinal Determination

The Bhāṣya makes four distinctions that the compact Kārikā alone does not show clearly.

First:

```text
cyuti / upapatti
    do not occur in samāhita or acitta states
```

Second:

```text
arhat's final nirvāṇa
    occurs in avyākṛta citta
    because that mind is weak and favorable to citta-cheda
```

Third:

```text
sudden death
    → bodily faculty + mind disappear abruptly

gradual death
    → bodily faculty withdraws progressively
```

Fourth, the bodily loci are destination-specific:

```text
apāya destination → feet
human destination → navel
deva destination  → heart
arhat             → heart
                    (crown according to another opinion)
```

The Bhāṣya therefore does not present a single universal upward course of mind through the body at death. It correlates the final locus of vijñāna-cessation with the destination of the dying being.

## Translation Consequences for the Kārikā

The first pāda should be read with the preceding verse:

```text
na ekāgra-acittayoḥ etau
```

“These two [death and arising] do not occur in one-pointed concentration or in a mindless state.”

`nirvāty avyākṛtadvaye` specifically concerns the arhat:

> “The arhat becomes extinguished in one of the two indeterminate minds.”

And the final pāda requires the Bhāṣya's continuation for full sense. `pāda–nābhi–hṛdaya` are not stages through which every dying mind passes. They are alternative loci correlated with the destination of the being.

This completes the Bhāṣya unit for VAkK 3.43 and leads directly into VAkK 3.44, where Vasubandhu classifies beings into the three `rāśis`.
