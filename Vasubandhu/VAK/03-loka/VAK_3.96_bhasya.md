# VAK_3.96 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā Anchor

```iast
ekadvitricaturdvīpāḥ na ca dvau saha buddhavat /
pratyudyānasvayaṃyāna kalahāstrajitaḥ avadhāḥ // VAkK_3.96 //
```

**Working translation:** The four [cakravartins] rule respectively one, two, three, or four continents; two do not arise together, as with Buddhas. Their conquests occur respectively through reception, personal approach, quarrel, and weapons; yet all cakravartins are without killing.

## Natural Argument

The Bhāṣya first maps territorial scope exactly onto the four wheel-types, in reverse order from lowest to highest:

```text
iron wheel   → one continent
copper wheel → two continents
silver wheel → three continents
gold wheel   → four continents
```

Vasubandhu explicitly calls this the `prājñaptika` account. In the sūtra, only the golden wheel is mentioned because the principal or highest case is being taken.

The statement `na ca dvau saha buddhavat` then opens a substantial doctrinal discussion. A cited sūtra denies the simultaneous arising of two Buddhas in one `loka`, and applies the same rule to cakravartins. The question is what `loka` means here: one trisāhasra-mahāsāhasra world-system, or all world-systems.

One position says Buddhas do not arise elsewhere, lest the Buddha's power be restricted. The `Nikāyāntarīyāḥ` hold instead that Buddhas do exist in other world-systems, arguing from the infinitude of world-systems and the absence of any obstacle preventing equally prepared bodhisattvas from attaining Buddhahood elsewhere. The prohibition therefore concerns simultaneous Buddhas within one world-system, not across all worlds.

Why not two Buddhas in the same world-system? The Bhāṣya gives two reasons: there is no purpose (`prayojanābhāva`) and bodhisattva vows themselves aim at appearing as the unique protector of a world lacking a guide. A single Buddha also intensifies reverence and urgency toward the teaching.

The second half then maps the four wheel-types onto four modes of conquest:

```text
gold   → local rulers come forth voluntarily to receive him
silver → he goes to them; they then submit
copper → he goes to them; quarrel occurs; they then submit
iron   → armed confrontation occurs; they then submit
```

But the concluding `avadhāḥ` is decisive. It does **not** mean “boundaries.” Vasubandhu glosses it immediately: even where conquest is by weapons, `vadha`, killing, does not occur. After conquering, cakravartins establish beings in the ten wholesome courses of action, and for that reason they are destined for rebirth among the gods.

The Bhāṣya then cites the seven treasures of a cakravartin: wheel, elephant, horse, jewel, woman, householder, and counselor/leader. Vasubandhu rejects the idea that sentient treasures arise through another person's karma: no one is produced by another's karma. Rather, beings who have accumulated karma leading to connection with that cakravartin arise through their **own** karmas when he appears.

## Voice Identification

- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — territorial mapping, conquest modes, gloss of `avadhāḥ`, and karma analysis.
- **Voice: Vaibhāṣika / one position (`ity eke`)** — Buddhas do not arise in other world-systems.
- **Voice: Nikāyāntarīya position** — Buddhas do arise in other world-systems.
- **Voice: Objector / dialectical question** — whether `loka` means one world-system or all world-systems, and whether the cakravartin's sentient treasures arise from another's karma.

## New Technical Terms

- **prājñaptika nirdeśa** — designation-based or conventional exposition
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-specific clarification in this passage
  - **function:** marks the fourfold wheel/continent scheme as a prajñapti-style account

- **prayojanābhāva** — absence of purpose
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only argumentative term here
  - **function:** explains why two Buddhas do not arise simultaneously in one world-system

- **praṇidhānavaśa** — by the force of a vow
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only explanatory term here
  - **function:** gives a second reason for the singular appearance of a Buddha

- **avadha** — without killing / non-killing
  - **status:** decisive correction to the Kārikā analysis
  - **function:** qualifies all four cakravartins, including the weapon-conquering ruler

- **daśa kuśala karmapathāḥ** — ten wholesome courses of action
  - **status:** doctrinal expansion beyond the Kārikā
  - **function:** moral order established by the cakravartin after conquest

- **sapta ratnāni** — seven treasures
  - **status:** sūtra-based expansion
  - **function:** defines the cakravartin's royal assemblage

## Doctrinal Determination

The Bhāṣya converts the terse verse into a complete graded sovereignty schema:

```text
wheel-material
    → territorial scope
        → mode of conquest
            → non-killing rule
                → establishment in ten wholesome karmapathas
```

The crucial doctrinal point is that cakravartin sovereignty remains non-homicidal even at its lowest, armed form. The weapon-conquering type can involve combat, but the royal function is still constrained by `avadha`.

The Buddha comparison is also sharply delimited. `buddhavat` establishes a rule of non-co-presence in one world-system, but the Bhāṣya records a live dispute over whether Buddhas exist simultaneously in distinct world-systems.

The karma discussion reinforces a core Abhidharma principle: **one being is not produced by another being's karma**. Shared historical conjunction does not collapse karmic ownership.

## Effect on the Kārikā Translation

**Revision required.**

The previous translation treated `avadhāḥ` as “boundaries / limits.” The Bhāṣya decisively rejects that reading through the gloss:

```iast
śastreṇāpi jayatāṃ badho na pravartate
```

“Even for those who conquer by weapons, killing does not occur.”

A better translation is therefore:

> They rule respectively one, two, three, or four continents; two do not arise together, as with Buddhas. They conquer respectively through reception, personal approach, quarrel, and weapons; yet all are without killing.

## Logical Determination

The verse gives a nested determination of political power:

```text
rank
    → range
        → method
            → ethical limit
```

The final ethical limit is not external to the classification. It qualifies the entire series: even where the method descends to armed confrontation, sovereignty remains bounded by non-killing.

The Buddha debate adds another logical determination:

```text
singularity within one world-system
    ≠ uniqueness across all possible world-systems
```

That distinction is explicitly contested in the Bhāṣya rather than silently settled.

## Organon Contact Point

Light contact only:

```text
A determination of power is incomplete until its limit is stated.
```

Here the cakravartin's territorial and military power is internally limited by `avadha`: conquest does not license killing. This remains a determination within produced Loka and its karmic order.
