## Natural Argument

### 1. Why the remaining twelve-member links are not all re-explained here

Vasubandhu first says that the remaining limbs are not again explained in full. Some have already been treated, while others will be treated in later Kośa chapters.

He explicitly recalls:

- `vijñāna` has already been explained.
- `ṣaḍāyatana` has already been explained.
- `saṃskāra` and `bhava` will be explained in the Karma chapter.
- `tṛṣṇā` and `upādāna` will be explained in the Kleśa chapter.

The present discussion therefore returns to the summary already established:

```text
pratītyasamutpāda
    = kleśa + karma + vastu
```

Here the topic is specifically `kleśa`.

### 2. Kleśa as seed (`bīja`)

Vasubandhu asks what similarity affliction has with seed and the other analogies.

A seed gives rise to sprout, stalk, leaves, and so forth. In the same way:

```text
kleśa
    → kleśa
    → karma
    → vastu
```

Affliction is productive: it generates further affliction, action, and the resultant factual series.

### 3. Kleśa as nāga

A lake occupied or presided over by a nāga does not dry up. Likewise:

```text
kleśa-nāga
    sustains
janma-saras
    the lake of birth
```

The analogy emphasizes the continued maintenance of the birth-series while affliction remains operative.

### 4. Kleśa as root (`mūla`)

Trees whose roots have not been removed grow again even after being repeatedly cut down.

Likewise, so long as the root of affliction has not been extracted, the destinies (`gati`) arise again.

The force of the analogy lies in `anapoddhṛta`: what has not been removed or pulled out at the root continues to regenerate.

### 5. Kleśa as tree (`vṛkṣa`)

Trees produce flowers and fruits again and again at their proper times. Likewise afflictions repeatedly become causes of:

```text
kleśa
karma
vastu
```

The point here is recurrent productivity (`asakṛt`), not merely one-time causation.

### 6. Kleśa as husk (`tuṣa`)

Rice grains enclosed in husk retain the capacity to sprout; bare grains do not.

Likewise karma enclosed by the husk of acquired affliction (`kleśa-prāpti`) is capable of sprouting into another birth, whereas karma by itself is not.

This final analogy is especially exact: the Bhāṣya does not say simply that kleśa itself becomes the next birth. Rather, kleśa enables karma to remain capable of producing renewed existence.

## Literal Prose Translation

The remaining limbs are not again explained. Why? Because some have already been explained and others will be explained later. Of these, vijñāna has already been explained, as has the sixfold sphere. Saṃskāras and bhava will be explained in the chapter on karma, while craving and appropriation will be explained in the chapter on the afflictions. It has already been shown that dependent co-arising, in summary, consists of affliction, karma, and vastu. Here, therefore, affliction is intended.

Affliction is to be understood as like a seed, like a nāga, like a root, like a tree, and like a husk. What similarity does it have to seed and the others? Just as sprout, stalk, leaves, and so forth arise from a seed, so from affliction arise affliction, karma, and vastu. Just as a lake occupied by a nāga does not dry up, so the lake of birth occupied by the nāga of affliction does not dry up. Just as trees whose roots have not been extracted grow again even when repeatedly cut down, so destinies arise again when the root of affliction has not been extracted. Just as trees produce flowers and fruits again and again at the proper times, so afflictions repeatedly become causes of affliction, karma, and vastu. And just as rice grains enclosed in husk are capable of sprouting, whereas bare grains are not, so karma enclosed in the husk of the acquisition of affliction is capable of sprouting into another birth, whereas karma alone is not. Thus affliction is to be understood as like seed and the rest.

## Doctrinal Determination

VAK 3.36 does not merely say that kleśa is a cause. The five analogies distinguish several causal functions.

```text
seed
    → origination of a differentiated series

nāga
    → maintenance of the birth-series

root
    → regeneration unless eradicated

tree
    → repeated production of effects

husk
    → enables karma to retain reproductive capacity
```

The final analogy is doctrinally decisive. Karma is not represented as independently and indefinitely reproductive. Its capacity to germinate into another birth depends upon its connection with affliction.

Thus the Bhāṣya articulates the earlier `kleśa → karma → vastu` scheme more precisely:

```text
kleśa generates and sustains the causal process
karma carries productive efficacy
vastu is produced as result
```

But the continued reproductive efficacy of karma is conditioned by kleśa.

## Translation Consequences for the Kārikā

The Kārikā's compact series should be translated as five distinct analogies rather than as ornamental similes:

```text
bījavat     — like a seed
nāgavat     — like a nāga sustaining a lake
mūlavat     — like an unextracted root
vṛkṣavat    — like a repeatedly fruiting tree
tuṣavat     — like the husk that preserves a grain's power to sprout
```

The Bhāṣya particularly sharpens `tuṣavat`: the husk analogy concerns **kleśa in relation to karma**. Karma joined to the acquisition of affliction can germinate into another birth; karma alone cannot.

This completes the Bhāṣya's explanation of the kleśa analogies in VAkK 3.36.
