# VAK 6.10 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

> āsamudrāsthi-vistāra-saṃkṣepād ādikarmikaḥ |
> pādāsthna ā kapālārtha-tyāgāt kṛtajayaḥ smṛtaḥ || 6.10 ||

**Best current translation:**  
The beginner expands the skeletal representation as far as the sea and then contracts it. One who progressively relinquishes the bones beginning with the feet until only half the skull remains is regarded as having gained mastery.

**Kārikā analysis:** [`VAK_6.10.md`](./VAK_6.10.md)

## Bhāṣya Source

Primary passage: Pradhan 338|02–338|15.

The prose first distinguishes three kinds of practitioner engaged in aśubhā-bhāvanā:

```text
ādikārmika
    beginner

kṛtaparijaya
    one who has acquired mastery

atikrāntamanaskāra
    one whose mode of attention has passed beyond the support
```

VAK 6.10 explains the first two; the third is completed at VAK 6.11.

## Prose Segmentation and Translation

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

The practitioner who wishes to cultivate the unattractive first fixes the mind upon some part of his own body—such as the great toe, the forehead, or another place toward which attention readily settles.

He then progressively removes, in imagination, flesh, moisture, and other covering features until the bones are purified in representation and the complete skeletal framework is seen.

Next he forms the same determination with respect to another body, and then progressively extends it through monastery, park, field, and finally as far as the ocean, until the whole earth is apprehended as filled with skeletal frameworks. This is done **for the increase of adhimokṣa**, the capacity of determinate imaginative resolution.

He then contracts the field again until only his own skeleton remains. This is done **for the contraction and gathering of mind** (`citta-saṃkṣepārtham`). At this point, says the Bhāṣya, the aśubhā practice is considered established for the beginner.

The practitioner who seeks a more refined contraction then relinquishes the bones of the feet while keeping the remainder in mind, and continues this progressive subtraction until half of the skull has been relinquished and only half remains. This is the practitioner who has acquired mastery.

## Argument Reconstruction

VAK 6.09 established aśubha as a counter-practice for rāga but also restricted its efficacy: it suppresses rather than definitively abandons kleśa. VAK 6.10 now explains how that counter-practice itself is trained.

The practice is not a static image of a skeleton. It is a sequence of controlled cognitive operations:

```text
fix attention locally
    ↓
remove covering features
    ↓
establish complete skeleton
    ↓
expand the determination universally
    ↓
contract it back to the singular body
    ↓
progressively relinquish even the skeleton
```

The beginner therefore learns **extension and contraction**; mastery adds **controlled subtraction**.

## The Function of Expansion

The Bhāṣya explicitly gives the purpose of expansion as:

```text
adhimokṣābhivardhanārtham
    for the increase of adhimokṣa
```

This is important. The expansion is not cosmological fantasy and not a claim that the earth is literally full of skeletons. It is a deliberate cultivation of the practitioner's capacity to hold one determinate mode of apprehension across an increasingly broad field.

The counter-determination becomes stable enough to dominate the ordinary attractive presentation of bodies.

## The Function of Contraction

The return from the ocean-wide field to one's own skeleton is explicitly:

```text
citta-saṃkṣepārtham
    for gathering / contracting the mind
```

Thus expansion and contraction train different capacities:

```text
vistāra
    extension of the determinate representation

saṃkṣepa
    concentration of the cognitive field
```

The practitioner must be capable of both. A representation that can only expand is dispersion; one that can only contract is rigidity.

## Mastery as Relinquishment

The most important development is that mastery does not mean holding the skeletal image more intensely.

The mastered practitioner begins to **relinquish the counter-object itself**:

```text
complete skeleton
    ↓
feet removed
    ↓
progressive subtraction
    ↓
half skull remains
```

So the aśubha representation is explicitly instrumental. The Path does not replace one permanent picture of the body with another allegedly final picture.

Instead:

```text
afflictive representation
    is countered by
therapeutic representation

therapeutic representation
    is itself brought under control
    and progressively relinquished
```

This sharply confirms that `pratipakṣa` is functional rather than dogmatic.

## Relation to the Śakti Question

VAK 6.01 introduced a supramundane `śakti` unavailable to the mundane path. VAK 6.09 distinguished suppression from definitive abandonment. VAK 6.10 now shows one form of preparatory capacity being deliberately trained.

The capacity here is not yet the supramundane power of Seeing. It is mastery over the **scope, concentration, and relinquishment of a constructed object-support**.

```text
preparatory path-capacity
    control of representation

supramundane path-capacity
    still to be established
    as definitive abandonment
```

This distinction must remain open as the chapter develops.

## Voice and Doctrinal Attribution

**Voice: Vasubandhu** — explanatory presentation of the practice and its stages.

The prose repeatedly uses `kila`, indicating inherited scholastic practice-description rather than an unqualified autobiographical endorsement. The passage should therefore be read as Vasubandhu transmitting and organizing the established Yogācāra/Abhidharma exercise rather than as evidence for an independent metaphysical claim about the body.

## New Technical Terms

- **ādikārmika** — beginner in the actual cultivation exercise
  - **status:** made technically operative in the Bhāṣya
  - **function:** practitioner-stage classification

- **kṛtaparijaya** — one who has acquired mastery
  - **status:** Bhāṣya expansion of the Kārikā's `kṛtajaya`
  - **function:** practitioner-stage classification

- **atikrāntamanaskāra** — practitioner whose attention has passed beyond the support
  - **status:** introduced here, completed at VAK 6.11
  - **function:** third stage in the practice

- **adhimokṣa** — determinate resolution / resolute imaginative apprehension
  - **status:** Bhāṣya technical term
  - **function:** explains why the skeletal field is expanded

- **citta-saṃkṣepa** — contraction or gathering of mind
  - **status:** Bhāṣya technical phrase
  - **function:** explains why the expanded field is contracted

- **kṛtaparijaya** — acquired command over the practice
  - **status:** Bhāṣya technical determination
  - **function:** result of progressive subtraction

## Translation Consequences

The Kārikā analysis is substantially confirmed, but the Bhāṣya adds two important purpose-clauses that should govern interpretation:

```text
expansion
    → increase of adhimokṣa

contraction
    → gathering of mind
```

The philosophical translation should therefore not present the exercise as mere visualization. It is a deliberate training of determinate apprehension and attentional control.

## Determinate Summary

```text
VAK 6.10

Beginner:
    fixes attention on one bodily part
    analytically removes coverings
    establishes the skeleton
    expands skeleton-determination to the ocean
    contracts it back to the singular body

Mastered practitioner:
    progressively relinquishes the skeleton itself
    until only half the skull remains
```

The decisive result is:

> The counter-object becomes effective only when the practitioner can generate it, extend it, contract it, and finally relinquish it.

Aśubha is therefore not simply a doctrine about what the body "really is." It is a deliberately governed counter-representation whose own appearance must eventually become dispensable.
