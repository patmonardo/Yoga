# VAK 6.03 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

> duḥkhās triduḥkhatāyogād yathāyogam aśeṣataḥ |
> manāpā amanāpāś ca tadanye caiva sāsravāḥ ||

**Best translation:**  
Agreeable, disagreeable, and other contaminated dharmas are, without remainder and according to applicability, suffering because of their connection with the three modes of suffering.

## Bhāṣya Translation and Argument

### 1. Why are all contaminated formations called duḥkha?

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

The initial question is direct: only one part of feeling has the intrinsic nature of painful feeling, so how can **all contaminated formations** (`sarve sāsravāḥ saṃskārāḥ`) be called duḥkha?

The answer is the threefold structure of duḥkhatā:

- **duḥkha-duḥkhatā** — suffering as immediately painful;
- **saṃskāra-duḥkhatā** — suffering belonging to conditioned formation as such;
- **vipariṇāma-duḥkhatā** — suffering through transformation.

All contaminated formations are duḥkha without remainder, but **according to applicability** (`yathāyogam`):

```text
agreeable (manāpa)
    → vipariṇāma-duḥkhatā

disagreeable (amanāpa)
    → duḥkha-duḥkhatā

neither
    → saṃskāra-duḥkhatā
```

The prose explicitly correlates these with the three feelings: pleasant, painful, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant, while extending the same designations to formations productive of those feelings.

### 2. The deepest extension: all conditioned formations are structurally duḥkha

The Bhāṣya then gives a more comprehensive determination. The first two duḥkhatās are mentioned in relation to agreeable and disagreeable formations because they are distinctive to those cases. But:

> **sarve tu saṃskārāḥ saṃskāraduḥkhatayā duḥkhāḥ**
>
> all formations are duḥkha through saṃskāra-duḥkhatā.

This is stronger than the simple three-column classification. `saṃskāra-duḥkhatā` is not confined to the neutral remainder; it is the universal depth-condition of conditioned existence. The pleasant and painful have their special modes, while all saṃskāras are structurally duḥkha.

Vasubandhu adds that **the noble ones alone see this** (`tāṃs tv āryā eva paśyanti`). The accompanying analogy compares a tiny hair lying on the palm with the same hair in the eye: what is scarcely noticed by the ordinary person becomes intensely intolerable to the discriminating vision of the noble one.

Thus the distinction is not that the noble person experiences more gross pain. Rather, the noble person's discrimination is more exact:

```text
ordinary cognition
    detects gross duḥkha

ārya cognition
    detects saṃskāra-duḥkhatā even in exalted existence
```

The text sharpens this by saying that ordinary beings do not form a cognition of suffering toward even the aggregates of Avīci in the same way that noble ones do toward the aggregates at the summit of existence.

### 3. Objection: if conditionedness implies duḥkha, must the Path itself be duḥkha?

**Voice: Objector**

If saṃskāra-duḥkhatā belongs to conditioned things, then the Path should also be duḥkha, since the Path is conditioned.

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

No. Vasubandhu now gives a decisive definition:

> **pratikūlaṃ hi duḥkham** — duḥkha is what is adverse / contrary.

The arising of the Path is not adverse to the noble ones because it brings about the exhaustion of all duḥkha (`sarvaduḥkhakṣayāvāhanāt`). Therefore conditionedness alone is not sufficient to classify the Path under duḥkha-satya.

This prevents a crude equation:

```text
conditioned = duḥkha
```

The relevant determination is instead:

```text
contaminated conditioned field
    → adverse to liberation
    → duḥkha-satya

Path
    → conditioned but productive of exhaustion of duḥkha
    → not duḥkha-satya
```

This is an important qualification of the Kārikā's phrase `sāsravāḥ`: the contaminated qualification is essential.

### 4. How can genuinely pleasant feeling be seen as duḥkha?

The discussion now becomes a substantial controversy.

The first resolution is that noble ones see all existence together with its pleasures as duḥkha because it has one pervasive flavor of saṃskāra-duḥkhatā. Hence duḥkha, not sukha, is established as the noble truth.

But this raises a harder question:

> How do noble ones see a feeling whose own nature is pleasant as duḥkha?

Vasubandhu answers:

> through its impermanence, because it is adverse (`anityatayā pratikūlatvāt`).

He then distinguishes the two aspects:

```text
anitya-ākāra
    formation has arising and passing away

duḥkha-ākāra
    formation is adverse / contrary
```

Seeing impermanence draws forth the aspect of duḥkha because what is impermanent becomes adverse.

### 5. Opponent: there is no pleasant feeling at all

**Voice: Objector / another school position**

A long objection argues that there is in truth no pleasant feeling; all feeling is painful. Scriptural passages are cited, and several arguments are offered:

1. supposed causes of pleasure become causes of pain when intensified or mistimed;
2. what people call pleasure is merely relief from another pain;
3. changing posture appears pleasant only because it counters discomfort.

The opponent therefore treats pleasure as misidentified diminution, alternation, or concealment of pain.

### 6. Abhidharmika reply: pleasant feeling really exists

**Voice: Abhidharmika position, presented approvingly by Vasubandhu**

The reply explicitly states:

> **astyevety ābhidhārmikāḥ** — the Abhidharmikas say: it does exist.

The refutation proceeds by forcing the opponent to define pain. If pain is damaging, then its contrary, what benefits, establishes pleasure. If pain is what is unwanted, then its contrary, what is wanted, likewise establishes pleasure.

Most importantly, the text distinguishes **intrinsic determination** from later evaluative apprehension:

> a pleasant feeling is pleasant by its own characteristic (`svalakṣaṇataḥ`).

The noble ones do not deny that characteristic. They regard the same pleasant feeling under another aspect: as a basis of heedlessness, difficult to maintain, subject to transformation, and impermanent. It is therefore unwanted under that further determination, not because its own characteristic has somehow changed from pleasure into pain.

This is a major reflective distinction:

```text
svalakṣaṇa
    pleasant feeling is genuinely pleasant

paryāya / another aspect
    impermanent
    transformable
    liable to attachment
    therefore duḥkha for liberating cognition
```

### 7. Bondage and liberation depend on how pleasure is seen

The Bhāṣya formulates the practical consequence with exceptional clarity:

- pleasant feeling has **pleasantness by nature** because it is agreeable;
- it has **duḥkha by another mode** because it is subject to transformation and impermanence;
- when seen as pleasure and relished, it conduces to **bondage**;
- when seen as duḥkha, it conduces to **liberation**, because that vision produces dispassion.

Thus the Buddhas instruct practitioners to see it in precisely the way that conduces to liberation.

This is not a denial of empirical pleasure. It is a transformation in the **practical determination of the same datum**.

```text
same pleasant feeling
        ↓
seen only as pleasant
        → enjoyment / attachment
        → bondage

seen under impermanence and transformation
        → duḥkha-determination
        → dispassion
        → liberation
```

The final conclusion of the controversy is therefore twofold:

```text
pleasant feeling exists as pleasant feeling

and

all contaminated existence is duḥkha
through connection with the three duḥkhatās
```

The second proposition does not require erasing the first.

- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — initial threefold explanation; universality of saṃskāra-duḥkhatā; reply concerning the Path; distinction of anitya and duḥkha aspects; presentation and defense of the Abhidharmika resolution.
- **Voice: Objector** — objection that the Path would then be duḥkha; later thesis that no pleasant feeling exists.
- **Voice: Abhidharmika position** — explicit affirmation that pleasant feeling exists (`astyevety ābhidhārmikāḥ`).
- **Voice: uncertain** — several short cited verses and inherited doctrinal formulations inside the controversy are not securely attributable from the passage alone.

## New Technical Terms

- **duḥkha-duḥkhatā** — suffering as intrinsically painful experience.
  - **status:** introduced explicitly in the Bhāṣya's analysis of the Kārikā.
  - **function:** doctrinal distinction.

- **saṃskāra-duḥkhatā** — the structural adversity of conditioned formations.
  - **status:** technically operative in the prose beyond the compressed Kārikā expression `triduḥkhatā`.
  - **function:** universalizing ground of duḥkha across contaminated formations.

- **vipariṇāma-duḥkhatā** — duḥkha through transformation.
  - **status:** technically operative in the prose.
  - **function:** explains how genuinely pleasant feeling belongs to duḥkha-satya.

- **pratikūla** — adverse, contrary, opposed to what is sought.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only definition in this unit.
  - **function:** defining mark used to distinguish duḥkha from mere conditionedness.

- **svalakṣaṇataḥ** — according to its own characteristic.
  - **status:** previously encountered project term, newly decisive here.
  - **function:** preserves the intrinsic pleasantness of pleasant feeling while permitting a distinct liberative determination.

- **ābhiprāyika** — stated with an intended purport rather than as an exhaustive literal characterization.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya technical hermeneutic term.
  - **function:** reconciles scriptural claims that all feeling is duḥkha with the threefold classification of feeling.

## Doctrinal Determination

VAK 6.03 does not teach that every moment of contaminated existence is phenomenally painful. It teaches that the whole contaminated field belongs to duḥkha-satya through differentiated modes.

The Bhāṣya makes this scientifically stronger by preserving two levels simultaneously:

```text
local determination
    pleasant is pleasant
    painful is painful
    neutral is neutral

path determination
    all contaminated formation is to be known as duḥkha
```

The second does not falsify the first. It redescribes the first according to the practical truth required for liberation.

This is why `saṃskāra-duḥkhatā` is especially important. It is the universal depth of the contaminated field, while duḥkha-duḥkhatā and vipariṇāma-duḥkhatā specify especially conspicuous modes.

## Effect on the Kārikā Translation

**Clarification only.**

The existing translation correctly captures exhaustive and differentiated inclusion. The Bhāṣya adds two important controls:

1. `saṃskāra-duḥkhatā` applies universally to formations, not only to the neutral remainder in an exclusive partition;
2. `duḥkha` must not be translated so as to deny the intrinsic pleasantness of pleasant feeling.

Thus the verse should be read as a **classification under the truth of suffering**, not as the claim that all experience has the phenomenal quality of pain.

## Logical Determination

The deepest logical structure is not simple identity but **two determinations of one content under different relations**:

```text
pleasant feeling
    qua svalakṣaṇa
        = pleasant

pleasant feeling
    qua impermanent, transformable, contaminated formation
        = duḥkha
```

There is therefore no contradiction in saying:

```text
sukha is sukha
and
sukha belongs to duḥkha-satya
```

because the predicates arise under different determinations.

The Path depends on learning this difference without collapsing it.

## Organon Contact Point

This passage gives an unusually exact example of **reflection preserving immediacy while altering its systematic determination**.

The Path does not manufacture a false negative predicate over pleasure. It comprehends the pleasant determination inside a larger relation:

```text
immediate quality
    pleasant
        ↓ reflection through
impermanence / transformation / contamination
        ↓
systematic determination
    duḥkha
        ↓
practical consequence
    dispassion and liberation
```

This also illuminates the śakti problem opened in VAK 6.01. Liberating knowledge is efficacious because it can apprehend a determination **under the relation that changes its practical consequence**. The same pleasant feeling, apprehended merely as enjoyable, supports bondage; apprehended adequately under transformation and impermanence, it supports liberation.

That is not yet a complete theory of Path-śakti, but VAK 6.03 supplies a concrete instance of knowledge functioning as practical counterforce.
