# VAK 6.45 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

**tatkṣayāptyā kṣayajñānam aśaikṣo 'rhann asau tadā /**  
**lokottareṇa vairāgyaṃ bhavāgrād anyato dvidhā // VAkK_6.45 //**

## Bhāṣya Source

Pradhan 365|10–366|16.

This passage directly continues VAK 6.44. The ninth and final cultivation-abandonable grade at the summit of existence has just been opposed by `vajropama-samādhi`, the final uninterrupted path.

## 1. From Vajropama-Samādhi to Kṣayajñāna

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

Vasubandhu begins from the final bhavāgra grade described in VAK 6.44:

> `yastvasau bhavāgriko navamaḥ prakāra ukto yasya vajropamena prahāṇam`

The ninth grade at the summit of existence is abandoned by the diamond-like path. The kārikā then states:

> `tatkṣayāptyā kṣayajñānam`

Vasubandhu explains:

> `tasya punarnavamasya prakārasya saha kṣayaprāptyā kṣayajñānamutpadyate`

Together with the attainment of the destruction of that ninth grade, **knowledge of exhaustion** (*kṣayajñāna*) arises.

This is a very exact temporal and logical determination. The destruction of the final grade and the arising of the knowledge that knows exhaustion are not collapsed into one undifferentiated event.

The sequence is:

```text
vajropama-samādhi
    = final ānantarya-mārga
    ↓
final bhavāgra grade destroyed
    ↓
kṣaya-prāpti
    attainment of that destruction
    ↓
kṣayajñāna arises
```

Vasubandhu immediately identifies the moment of `kṣayajñāna` as:

> `vajropamasamādheranantaraṃ paścimo vimuktimārgaḥ`

—the **final liberation-path immediately after vajropama-samādhi**.

Thus VAK 6.45 confirms the transition-rule established throughout Chapter 6:

```text
ānantarya-mārga
    destroys the obstruction

vimukti-mārga
    possesses / knows the achieved separation
```

At the final stage this becomes:

```text
vajropama-samādhi
    ↓
kṣayajñāna
```

## 2. Why This First Kṣayajñāna Is Decisive

Vasubandhu gives the reason this knowledge is called `kṣayajñāna` from its first arising:

> `sarvāsravakṣayaprāptisahajatvāt`

because it arises together with the attainment of the destruction of **all āsravas**.

The final grade is locally only one remaining grade, but its destruction completes the entire prior series of abandonments. Consequently the liberation-path that follows it is not merely knowledge of one isolated local removal. It occurs when the practitioner has attained the exhaustion of the whole afflictive influx-field.

```text
many prior abandonments
    +
last bhavāgra grade destroyed
    ↓
complete āsrava-exhaustion attained
    ↓
kṣayajñāna
```

This makes `kṣayajñāna` a knowledge of **completed purification**, not merely a cognition occurring after a final psychological event.

## 3. The Arising of the Aśaikṣa

The kārikā continues:

> `aśaikṣo 'rhann asau tadā`

Vasubandhu explains that when `kṣayajñāna` has arisen, the practitioner who had been an `arhattva-pratipannaka` becomes:

- **aśaikṣa** — one for whom no further training remains,
- **arhat** — one who has attained the fruit of arhatship.

The decisive phrase is:

> `phalāntaraṃ prati punaḥ śikṣitavyābhāvādaśaikṣaḥ`

He is `aśaikṣa` because there is **nothing further to be trained for with respect to another fruit**.

This gives `aśaikṣa` a precise functional sense. It does not mean that knowledge, activity, or meditative functioning simply cease. It means that the progressive path of training toward a still-unattained liberating fruit has reached completion.

```text
śaikṣa
    further fruit remains to be realized
    therefore training continues

kṣayajñāna attained
    ↓
no further fruit remains to be trained for
    ↓
aśaikṣa
```

This is the formal opening of the **Aśaikṣa Path** in Chapter 6.

## 4. Why He Is Called Arhat

Vasubandhu also explains the title `arhat`.

The passage gives two determinations:

1. the work that had to be done has been accomplished;
2. the practitioner is worthy of worship by beings still governed by attachment.

The exact etymological wording of the e-text is somewhat unstable, but the doctrinal point is clear: `arhat` names one who has completed the required liberating work and has thereby become worthy of veneration.

This is important because the Bhāṣya does not define arhatship merely negatively as absence of kleśa. It is a completed **path-status**, established through the final liberation-path and its knowledge.

## 5. What Makes the Remaining Noble Persons Śaikṣa?

Vasubandhu now explicitly contrasts the arhat with the seven noble persons previously described:

> `anye sapta pūrvoktāḥ pudgalāḥ śaikṣāḥ`

The other seven are **śaikṣas**, persons still under training.

Why?

> `āsravakṣayāya nityaṃ śikṣaṇaśīlatvācchikṣātraye`

because they continually train for the exhaustion of the āsravas in the **three trainings**:

- `adhiśīla` — higher discipline,
- `adhicitta` — higher mind,
- `adhiprajñā` — higher principle / discriminative knowing.

Vasubandhu adds that these three have the natures of:

```text
śīla
samādhi
prajñā
```

respectively.

This is one of the strongest practical-scientific determinations in the passage. The distinction between śaikṣa and aśaikṣa is grounded not in an honorific hierarchy but in whether the threefold liberating discipline still has a further fruit to accomplish.

```text
adhiśīla
    ↓
adhicitta
    ↓
adhiprajñā
    ↓
āsrava-kṣaya
```

The Bhāṣya thereby connects the path-person taxonomy directly to a structure of training.

## 6. Why the Ordinary Person Is Not Therefore a Śaikṣa

**Voice:** Objector, then Vasubandhu

An objection naturally arises: an ordinary person (*pṛthagjana*) may also practice discipline, concentration, and principle. Should such a person also be called `śaikṣa`?

Vasubandhu answers:

> `na`

No.

He gives two reasons:

> `yathābhūtaṃ satyāprajñānāt punaścāpaśikṣaṇāt`

The ordinary person does not yet know the truths **as they really are**, and is also capable of training contrary to the liberating training.

The distinction is therefore not simply:

```text
practices discipline / meditation / prajñā
    = śaikṣa
```

Rather, `śaikṣa` is a technical noble-path status. The noble trainee is irreversibly oriented by the truth that has been seen.

Vasubandhu cites the sūtra formula that the śaikṣa is one who **trains in the training**, with repetition functioning as restriction: the relevant person trains and does not train against that training.

This is a crucial path-discrimination principle:

```text
mere exercise
    ≠
śaikṣa status

śaikṣa
    = noble practitioner
      whose training is governed by yathābhūta truth
      and directed toward āsrava-exhaustion
```

## 7. The Noble Person at Rest Still Counts as Training

A further question follows. If a noble person is momentarily in a natural or ordinary state (*prakṛtistha*), how can that person be described as continuously disposed to training?

Vasubandhu answers:

> `āśayataḥ`

—by **intention / underlying orientation**.

He also appeals to the continuing connection through acquisition of the three trainings.

Thus `śaikṣa` is not restricted to moments in which an explicit path-cognition is manifest. It characterizes the noble continuum by its established orientation and acquired relation to the three trainings.

This is another indication that the Kośa's path-person categories are not descriptions of transient mental episodes. They classify a transformed practical continuum.

## 8. Śaikṣa-Dharmas, Aśaikṣa-Dharmas, and Nirvāṇa

Vasubandhu next defines the corresponding dharmas:

```text
śaikṣa-dharmas
    = the anāsrava dharmas of a śaikṣa

aśaikṣa-dharmas
    = the anāsrava dharmas of an aśaikṣa
```

He then asks why nirvāṇa itself is not classified as `śaikṣa` or `aśaikṣa`.

The answer is symmetrical:

- nirvāṇa is not `śaikṣa`, because an aśaikṣa and even a pṛthagjana can also stand in relation to it;
- nirvāṇa is not `aśaikṣa`, because a śaikṣa and a pṛthagjana can also stand in relation to it.

The path-status predicates therefore belong to the relevant **anāsrava dharmas of persons**, not to nirvāṇa simply in itself.

This prevents the practical classification from being mistaken for an ontological division of nirvāṇa.

## 9. Eight Noble Persons by Name, Five by Distinct Continuum-Status

Vasubandhu gathers the preceding path analysis into the classical eight noble persons:

```text
four pratipannakas
    practitioners progressing toward a fruit

four phalasthas
    persons established in a fruit
```

They are, by name, eight:

```text
1. progressing toward stream-entry
2. stream-enterer
3. progressing toward once-returning
4. once-returner
5. progressing toward non-returning
6. non-returner
7. progressing toward arhatship
8. arhat
```

But Vasubandhu adds a subtle Abhidharma distinction:

> `nāmata ete 'ṣṭau bhavanti / dravyatastu pañca`

They are **eight by name, but five in real distinction**.

Why only five? The first candidate toward stream-entry is distinct, together with the four fruit-positions. The other three candidate-designations do not constitute additional distinct persons over and above practitioners already established in earlier fruits while progressing toward later ones.

This is a powerful clarification of the taxonomy:

```text
naming according to path-function
    = 8

distinct underlying person-statuses
    = 5
```

The eightfold system is therefore functional and relational, not a naive list of eight independent substances or soul-types.

Vasubandhu further notes that this enumeration describes **sequential attainment** (*anupūrvādhigama*). A practitioner already substantially dispassionate toward the desire realm may, while on the path of seeing, be progressing toward once-returning or non-returning without first occupying every ordinary sequential designation.

## 10. Two Kinds of Cultivation Path Recalled

The Bhāṣya now reopens a distinction established earlier:

> `dvividho hi bhāvanāmārga ukto laukiko lokottaraśceti`

The cultivation path is twofold:

```text
laukika
    mundane

lokottara
    supramundane
```

The question is then: by which of these can the noble trainee attain dispassion from which level?

The kārikā answers first:

> `lokottareṇa vairāgyaṃ bhavāgrāt`

Dispassion from the **summit of existence** (*bhavāgra*) is attained only by the **supramundane path**, not by the mundane path.

Vasubandhu gives two connected reasons.

First, there is no mundane level above bhavāgra from which a superior mundane counteragent could operate.

Second, a path belonging to the same level cannot serve as the counteragent to that level's own afflictions.

Why not?

> `tatkleśānuśayitatvāt`

because the afflictions of that level can lie latent in that very basis.

Vasubandhu formulates the general rule:

```text
where a kleśa can anuśaya in a vastu,
that same vastu cannot function toward abandoning that kleśa;

where something is genuinely the counteragent,
that kleśa does not lie latent there.
```

This is a highly determinate theory of counteraction. A field cannot liberate itself from an affliction by means of a determination that remains internally available to that same afflictive field.

At bhavāgra the necessity becomes absolute:

```text
no higher mundane basis exists
    ↓
same-level mundane determination cannot counteract its own latent kleśa
    ↓
only lokottara-mārga can produce final vairāgya
```

## 11. From Other Levels, Dispassion Is Twofold

The kārikā concludes:

> `anyato dvidhā`

Vasubandhu explains:

> `bhavāgrādanyataḥ sarvato bhūmerlaukikenāpi vairāgyaṃ lokottareṇāpi`

From every level **other than bhavāgra**, dispassion may be attained in either of two ways:

```text
laukika-mārga
or
lokottara-mārga
```

Thus VAK 6.45 closes with an asymmetry:

```text
bhavāgra
    → lokottara only

all lower levels
    → laukika or lokottara
```

The following verse takes up the kinds of `visaṃyoga-prāpti` that arise when an Aryan gains such dispassion.

## 12. Path-Knowledge Determination

VAK 6.45 is a major hinge in Mārganirdeśa because it does two things at once.

First, it completes the entire trainee-path:

```text
final ānantarya-mārga
    vajropama-samādhi
    ↓
last kleśa-grade destroyed
    ↓
final vimukti-mārga
    ↓
kṣayajñāna
    ↓
āsrava-kṣaya complete
    ↓
aśaikṣa
    ↓
arhat
```

Second, it retrospectively defines what the **śaikṣa path** has been:

```text
training in
    adhiśīla
    adhicitta
    adhiprajñā

for
    āsrava-kṣaya
```

The path has therefore not been merely a succession of altered mental states. It has been a rationally ordered practical discipline whose criterion is the actual exhaustion of the afflictive field.

The title `aśaikṣa` means that this work has reached completion.

## 13. Organon Contact

The strongest Organon contact here should remain restrained and textual.

The Bhāṣya gives a practical structure of completed mediation:

```text
obstruction
    ↓
counteractive path
    ↓
destruction
    ↓
attainment of destruction
    ↓
knowledge of exhaustion
    ↓
completed path-subject
```

The crucial point is that `kṣaya` alone is not yet the complete determination. The accomplished negation becomes a completed path-position only in the immediately following liberation-path as **known and possessed exhaustion**.

This is not static identity. The status `aśaikṣa` is generated through the whole mediated movement of training and becomes intelligible only as the result of that movement.

For the Rational Kośa project this is especially important: liberation is here presented as a practical science in which the path produces determinate transformations, and those transformations culminate in a knowledge adequate to their accomplished result.

No stronger identification with Yoga, Kant, Fichte, or Hegel is required at this pass.

## 14. New Technical Terms

- **kṣayajñāna** — knowledge of exhaustion; the knowledge arising in the final liberation-path together with complete attainment of āsrava-exhaustion.
  - **status:** introduced explicitly at the transition from trainee to arhat.
  - **function:** cognizes / possesses the completed exhaustion established after the final uninterrupted path.

- **kṣaya-prāpti** — attainment of destruction / exhaustion.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya expansion of the kārikā's `tat-kṣayāptyā`.
  - **function:** marks possession of the accomplished abandonment whose co-arising knowledge is `kṣayajñāna`.

- **aśaikṣa** — one beyond further training.
  - **status:** path-person term foregrounded here.
  - **function:** identifies the practitioner for whom no further liberating fruit remains to be trained toward.

- **adhiśīla** — higher discipline.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only expansion in this verse-context.
  - **function:** first of the three trainings of the śaikṣa.

- **adhicitta** — higher mind.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only expansion in this verse-context.
  - **function:** second of the three trainings; samādhi in nature.

- **adhiprajñā** — higher principle / discriminative knowing.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only expansion in this verse-context.
  - **function:** third of the three trainings; prajñā in nature.

- **anupūrvādhigama** — sequential attainment.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only classificatory term.
  - **function:** qualifies the ordinary eight-person enumeration as a sequential path-schema.

- **prakṛtistha ārya** — noble person presently in an ordinary / natural state rather than manifest path-attainment.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only explanatory phrase.
  - **function:** tests how continuous śaikṣa-status can apply beyond explicit path moments.

## 15. Voice / Doctrinal Caution

**Voice:** Vasubandhu for the principal explanations of `kṣayajñāna`, `aśaikṣa`, the three trainings, the eight noble persons, and the mundane/supramundane distinction.

**Voice:** Objector / questioner in the short challenges concerning whether a pṛthagjana should count as śaikṣa, how an Aryan at rest remains disposed to training, and how nirvāṇa should be classified.

The passage is doctrinally dense but unusually coherent. Its center is not the later scholastic enumeration but a clean practical logic:

```text
training
    → abandonment
    → final liberation-path
    → knowledge of exhaustion
    → completion of training
```

## Determinate Summary

VAK 6.45 completes the movement begun by the diamond-like concentration of VAK 6.44. When the ninth and final bhavāgra grade is destroyed, the immediately following and final liberation-path arises as **kṣayajñāna**, knowledge of exhaustion, together with attainment of the exhaustion of all āsravas. At that moment the practitioner who had been progressing toward arhatship becomes an **aśaikṣa** and an **arhat**.

The Bhāṣya defines the contrast sharply. The seven remaining noble path-persons are `śaikṣa` because they continually train in higher discipline, higher mind, and higher principle toward āsrava-exhaustion. The arhat is `aśaikṣa` because no further liberating fruit remains to be attained through training.

Vasubandhu also consolidates the path taxonomy as eight noble persons by functional designation but only five by distinct underlying person-status, and then recalls the twofold cultivation path. Dispassion from **bhavāgra** can be produced only by the supramundane path, because no higher mundane basis exists and a same-level basis in which its own kleśas can lie latent cannot function as their counteragent. From all lower levels, however, dispassion may be attained either mundanely or supramundanely.

The verse therefore opens the Aśaikṣa Path not with a vague declaration of enlightenment but with a precise practical transition:

```text
final obstruction destroyed
    ↓
final liberation-path
    ↓
kṣayajñāna
    ↓
āsrava-exhaustion possessed and known
    ↓
aśaikṣa / arhat
```
