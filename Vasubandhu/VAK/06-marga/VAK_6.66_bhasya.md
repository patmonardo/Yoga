# VAK 6.66 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

> dhyāneṣu mārgaḥ pratipatsukhā duḥkhānyabhūmiṣu /  
> dhanyābhijñā mṛdumateḥ kṣiprābhijñetarasya tu // VAK_6.66 //

**Best current translation:**  
In the dhyānas the Path is an easy course; in the other grounds it is a difficult course. For one of dull faculty, it is a course of slow realization; for the other [one of sharp faculty], it is a course of quick realization.

**Kārikā analysis:** [`VAK_6.66.md`](./VAK_6.66.md)

## Bhāṣya Source

Pradhan 382|07–382|20.

VAK 6.65 ended by reducing the many classifications of Mārga to four operative phases. VAK 6.66 now introduces another classification of the very same Path, this time under the name *pratipad*, the course or mode of progress by which nirvāṇa is reached.

The Bhāṣya develops four courses by crossing two independent distinctions:

```text
pleasant / difficult course
        ×
slow / quick realization
```

The first distinction depends on the meditative ground; the second depends on the strength of the practitioner's faculties.

## 1. Mārga Is Also Called Pratipad

**Voice:** Vasubandhu

The prose opens:

> `mārga eva punaḥ pratipadityukto nirvāṇapratipādanāt`

The Path itself is also called *pratipad* because it conducts one to nirvāṇa.

This is not a new entity beside Mārga. It is the Path considered under another determination: not merely as the road to nirvāṇa, but as the concrete course of progress by which one advances along it.

The Bhāṣya therefore says:

> `catasraḥ pratipadaḥ`

There are four courses of progress.

It immediately lists the four:

```text
duḥkhā dhandhābhijñā
    difficult, slow realization

duḥkhā kṣiprābhijñā
    difficult, quick realization

sukhā dhandhābhijñā
    easy, slow realization

sukhā kṣiprābhijñā
    easy, quick realization
```

The classification is thus explicitly combinatorial rather than serial.

## 2. The Easy Course: the Four Dhyānas

The kārikā states:

> `dhyāneṣu mārgaḥ pratipatsukhā`

In the dhyānas, the Path is an easy course.

The Bhāṣya explains that this means the four dhyānas:

> `caturdhyāneṣu mārgaḥ sukhā pratipat`

Why?

> `aṅgaparigrahāt śamathavipaśyanāsamatābhyām ayatnavāhitvāt`

Because the requisite factors are fully possessed, because calm (*śamatha*) and insight (*vipaśyanā*) are in equilibrium, and because the course proceeds without strenuous effort.

The structure is therefore:

```text
four dhyānas
    requisite factors present
    śamatha = vipaśyanā in balance
    effortless continuity
        ↓
sukhā pratipad
    easy course
```

The term *sukhā* should therefore not be reduced to ordinary pleasant feeling. The Bhāṣya defines the ease structurally: the Path proceeds with the necessary meditative factors and with balanced calm and insight.

## 3. The Difficult Course: the Other Grounds

The kārikā continues:

> `duḥkhā 'nyabhūmiṣu`

In the other grounds, the Path is difficult.

The Bhāṣya specifies the relevant grounds:

```text
anāgamya
    the preparatory concentration

dhyānāntara
    the intermediate concentration

ārūpya-bhūmis
    the immaterial grounds
```

The reason is twofold:

> `pratipadaṅgāparigrahāc chamathavipaśyanānyūnatvācca yatnavāhitvāt`

The factors of the course are not fully possessed, and either calm or insight is deficient; therefore the Path proceeds only through effort.

Vasubandhu then specifies the asymmetry:

> `śamathanyūne hyanāgamyadhyānāntare vipaśyanānyūnā ārupyā iti`

In the preparatory and intermediate concentrations, calm is deficient; in the immaterial attainments, insight is deficient.

Thus:

```text
anāgamya + dhyānāntara
    insufficient śamatha

ārūpya
    insufficient vipaśyanā

both cases
    imbalance
    effortful progress
        ↓
duḥkhā pratipad
```

This is one of the most conceptually useful points in the passage. Difficulty is not explained psychologically as mere suffering or aversion. It is a structural disequilibrium in the conditions of the Path.

## 4. Each of These Is Again Twofold

The prose now states:

> `sā punardvividhā 'pi pratipat`

Each of these two courses—easy and difficult—is itself twofold.

The verse gives the second axis:

> `dhanyābhijñā mṛdumateḥ kṣiprābhijñetarasya tu`

The reading `dhanyābhijñā` in the source represents the expected technical form *dhandhābhijñā*, “slow realization.” The prose repeatedly gives `dhandhābhijñā` and directly explains it.

For one with weak or dull faculties (*mṛdvindriya*), whether the course is easy or difficult, it is a course of slow realization:

> `mṛdvindriyasya sukhā duḥkhā vā pratipaddhandhābhijñā`

For one with sharp faculties (*tīkṣṇendriya*), the course is one of quick realization:

> `tīkṣṇendriyasya kṣiprābhijñā`

So the complete matrix is:

```text
                    dull faculty        sharp faculty

four dhyānas        easy + slow         easy + quick

other grounds       difficult + slow    difficult + quick
```

The two determinations must not be collapsed.

```text
ease / difficulty
    determined by bhūmi

slow / quick realization
    determined by indriya
```

## 5. What Does Dhandhābhijñā Mean?

The prose pauses to explain the compound:

> `dhandhābhijñā asyāṃ pratipadi`

Realization is slow on this course.

Then:

> `seyaṃ dhandhābhijñā`

Therefore it is called a course of slow realization.

The same construction applies to *kṣiprābhijñā*, quick realization.

Vasubandhu then gives an alternative interpretation:

> `dhandhasya vā pudgalasyeyamiti dhandhābhijñā`

Or it is called *dhandhābhijñā* because it belongs to a slow person.

The important point is that the compound can be understood either from the tempo of realization occurring in the course or from the type of person whose course it is. The doctrinal result remains the same: the distinction tracks faculty-strength.

## 6. Two Independent Determinations of Progress

The Bhāṣya has therefore produced a very precise fourfold classification from two independent principles:

```text
GROUND
    dhyānas
        → easy
    other grounds
        → difficult

FACULTY
    dull
        → slow realization
    sharp
        → quick realization
```

This is not a hierarchy in which “easy” necessarily means “quick,” or “difficult” necessarily means “slow.”

A practitioner can therefore have:

```text
difficult + quick
```

or:

```text
easy + slow
```

That independence is exactly why four *pratipads* are required.

## 7. Transition to the Bodhipakṣya Dharmas

The passage ends by opening the next systematic determination of Mārga:

> `punarapyeṣa mārgo bodhipakṣyākhyāṃ labhate`

Again, this same Path receives the designation *bodhipakṣya*.

This transition is significant. Vasubandhu is not introducing a different Path after the four *pratipads*. The same Mārga is successively determined:

```text
Mārga
    four operative path-phases      VAK 6.65
        ↓
    four courses of progress         VAK 6.66
        ↓
    bodhipakṣya dharmas              VAK 6.67ff.
```

The chapter-ending movement is therefore one of increasingly explicit articulation of one Path under different principles of classification.

## Restrained Organon Contact

The strongest formal feature here is the independence of two determinations.

The Bhāṣya does not infer speed from difficulty or difficulty from speed. Instead, one determination comes from the objective condition of the Path—the meditative ground—while the other comes from the capacity of the practitioner—the faculty.

```text
course as conditioned by ground
        ×
course as conditioned by faculty
        ↓
four determinate forms
```

This gives us a genuinely useful later Organon contact: a concrete course is determined reciprocally by the structure through which one proceeds and by the power of the one proceeding. For the present Bhāṣya pass, however, the important point is simply Vasubandhu's own two-axis construction.

## New Technical Terms

- **pratipad** — course, mode of progress  
  **status:** newly foregrounded here as a designation of Mārga  
  **function:** the Path considered as the concrete mode by which nirvāṇa is reached

- **sukhā pratipad** — easy course  
  **status:** developed here  
  **function:** Path in the four dhyānas, where the requisite factors are present and calm and insight are balanced

- **duḥkhā pratipad** — difficult course  
  **status:** developed here  
  **function:** Path in the other grounds, where factors are incomplete and calm or insight is deficient

- **dhandhābhijñā** — slow realization  
  **status:** technical term explicitly explained here  
  **function:** course associated with weak/dull faculties

- **kṣiprābhijñā** — quick realization  
  **status:** technical term explicitly explained here  
  **function:** course associated with sharp faculties

- **mṛdvindriya** — one of weak or dull faculty  
  **status:** recurrent faculty distinction  
  **function:** determines slow realization

- **tīkṣṇendriya** — one of sharp faculty  
  **status:** recurrent faculty distinction  
  **function:** determines quick realization

- **śamatha** — calm  
  **status:** recurrent contemplative term  
  **function:** one side of the equilibrium that makes the dhyāna-course easy

- **vipaśyanā** — insight  
  **status:** recurrent contemplative term  
  **function:** the other side of the equilibrium that makes the dhyāna-course easy

## Voice / Doctrinal Caution

**Voice:** Vasubandhu throughout the preserved passage.

There is no explicit school controversy here. The main task is classificatory and explanatory.

A textual caution is required for the kārikā's `dhanyābhijñā`. The immediately following prose consistently reads and explains `dhandhābhijñā`, which is the technical expression required by the fourfold classification. The present translation therefore follows the Bhāṣya's explicit gloss rather than treating `dhanyābhijñā` literally.

## Determinate Summary

VAK 6.66 classifies the Path as four *pratipads* by crossing two independent determinations. The meditative ground determines whether progress is easy or difficult: in the four dhyānas it is easy because the requisite factors are present, calm and insight are balanced, and progress is effortless; in the preparatory, intermediate, and immaterial grounds it is difficult because the factors are incomplete and either calm or insight is deficient. Faculty-strength independently determines whether realization is slow or quick: dull faculties yield slow realization, sharp faculties quick realization. The four courses are therefore not a linear hierarchy but a two-axis matrix. The Bhāṣya then turns immediately to the next determination of the same Mārga, its thirty-seven bodhipakṣya dharmas.
