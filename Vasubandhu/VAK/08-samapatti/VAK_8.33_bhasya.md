# VAK_8.33_bhasya

## Kārikā

**IAST**

`nirodhas tu samāpattiḥ sūkṣma-sūkṣmād anantaram /`
`sva-śuddhakādharāryeṇa vyutthānaṃ cetasā tataḥ // VAK_8.33 //`

## Best Current Translation

But cessation is an attainment, immediately following the most subtle of the subtle. From it, emergence occurs through a determinate mind: either a pure mind of its own summit level or a contaminated mind of the lower Nothingness sphere, as specified by the Bhāṣya.

## Bhāṣya Translation

“But cessation is an attainment.”

The cessation of perception and feeling is the eighth Deliverance, the attainment of cessation. It has already been explained previously.

It is called a Deliverance because of turning away from perception and feeling, or from all conditioned dharmas. Others say that it is a Deliverance because one is released from the obstruction to attainment.

One enters it immediately after making the most subtle still subtler. At the summit of existence (`bhavāgra`), perception is subtle; making that again still subtler, one enters cessation.

For one who has entered it, emergence from cessation occurs either through a pure mind belonging to the summit of existence or through a contaminated mind belonging to the sphere of Nothingness (`ākiñcanyāyatana`). Thus the mind associated with entry into this attainment is contaminated; the emergence-mind stands in the specified immediate relation to contaminated cognition.

The final sentence is syntactically compressed. Its doctrinal force is preserved here without over-normalizing the exact temporal formula beyond what the Sanskrit securely supports.

## Natural Argument

Vasubandhu identifies the eighth `vimokṣa` with `saṃjñāveditanirodha`, the cessation of perception and feeling, and therefore with `nirodhasamāpatti`.

The word `vimokṣa` is then justified functionally. On Vasubandhu's primary explanation, cessation is release or turning-away (`vaimukhya`) from perception and feeling, or more comprehensively from all conditioned formations. An alternative explanation defines the release as freedom from an obstruction that prevents attainment.

The transition into cessation is not represented as an abrupt leap. The practitioner begins at `bhavāgra`, where perception has already become extremely subtle, and makes that subtle perception still subtler. Cessation is entered immediately after this final refinement.

Emergence is likewise rule-governed rather than arbitrary. The Bhāṣya specifies two possible emergence minds: a pure mind of `bhavāgra`, or a contaminated mind of the lower `ākiñcanyāyatana` level.

## Argument Reconstruction

1. The eighth Deliverance is the cessation of perception and feeling.
2. This cessation is itself a `samāpatti`.
3. It qualifies as `vimokṣa` because it realizes release from the cognitive-conditioned field, though an alternative school explanation locates the release in removal of an attainment-obstruction.
4. Entry requires a genetic refinement of the already subtle perception at `bhavāgra`.
5. Cessation follows that refinement immediately.
6. Emergence is possible only through determinate classes of mind specified by level and contamination-status.
7. Therefore cessation is not an indeterminate blank but a technically structured attainment with determinate entry and exit conditions.

## New Technical Terms

- **saṃjñāveditanirodha** — cessation of perception and feeling; **status:** previously encountered; **function:** identifies the eighth Deliverance precisely.
- **samāpattyāvaraṇa** — obstruction to attainment; **status:** rare in the Kārikās / Bhāṣya-specific in this local argument; **function:** alternative explanation of why cessation is called a Deliverance.
- **bhavāgra** — summit of existence, the highest formless level; **status:** previously encountered; **function:** immediate basis from which cessation is entered.
- **sūkṣmatara** — still subtler; **status:** ordinary comparative used technically here; **function:** describes the genetic refinement preceding cessation.
- **vyutthāna** — emergence from an attainment; **status:** previously encountered; **function:** marks the restoration of determinate cognition after cessation.

## Voice

- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — identifies the eighth Deliverance with cessation-attainment and explains its entry and emergence.
- **Voice: alternative doctrinal position (`apare`)** — `vimokṣa` means release from the obstruction to attainment.

## Doctrinal Determination

The eighth Deliverance differs categorically from the first seven. Earlier Deliverances transform or transcend an object-field while cognition remains operative. Here perception and feeling themselves cease.

Yet the attainment is not therefore doctrinally unstructured. Vasubandhu supplies:

- a determinate predecessor (`bhavāgra`),
- a determinate operation (making subtle perception still subtler),
- immediate succession into cessation,
- and determinate modes of emergence.

The structure is therefore:

```text
bhavāgra
    → subtle perception
    → perception made still subtler
    → nirodhasamāpatti
    → determinate emergence
```

## Translation Consequences

The first-pass phrase “the most subtle of the subtle” remains acceptable if it is understood dynamically. The Bhāṣya shows that `sūkṣmasūkṣma` is not merely a static label for the highest formless attainment: the practitioner takes already subtle perception and makes it still subtler immediately before cessation.

The compressed compound `svaśuddhakādharāryeṇa` should not be expanded freely from the Kārikā alone. The Bhāṣya controls the interpretation by specifying the actual emergence alternatives: pure cognition of `bhavāgra` or contaminated cognition of `ākiñcanyāyatana`.

## Logical Determination

Cessation here is produced through progressive determination rather than through an unexplained jump into nothingness.

```text
subtle
    → subtler
    → cessation
```

The operative cognition is progressively deprived of the determination that sustains its activity until the attainment of cessation becomes possible.

This gives a useful distinction:

```text
absence of cognition
    ≠
indeterminate absence
```

`Nirodhasamāpatti` is an achieved condition whose genesis and emergence are themselves rule-governed.

## Light Organon Contact

For the later transcendental reconstruction, this passage is valuable precisely because Vasubandhu refuses to treat cessation as an ineffable hole in the system. The transition has a predecessor, a genetic operation, a terminal condition, and rules of re-emergence.

That makes `nirodha` suitable for system-building analysis: the field ceases to operate, but the cessation belongs to an intelligible architecture of the field.

We should not yet identify this directly with Yoga's final `nirodha` or with a transcendental Absolute. The Bhāṣya is describing a specific attained cessation within its own practical taxonomy.
