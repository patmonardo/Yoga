# VAK_8.01 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

> dvidhā dhyānāni catvāri proktās tadupapattayaḥ /
> samāpattiḥ śubhaikāgryaṃ pañcaskandhās tu sānugam // VAK_8.01 //

**Best current translation:**  
The four dhyānas are declared in two ways: as meditative attainments and as the rebirths corresponding to them. Samāpatti is wholesome one-pointedness; together with its accompaniment, it consists of the five aggregates.

**Kārikā analysis:** [`VAK_8.01.md`](./VAK_8.01.md)

## Source Caution

The working e-text reads `śubhaikāgyraṃ` and later `kuśalamaikāgyraṃ`; as in the Kārikā pass, these are treated as transmitted typographical defects for `śubhaikāgryaṃ` and `kuśalam aikāgryaṃ`. The source is not a critical edition, so the repository preserves the transmitted readings in the quoted source while using normalized forms only in analysis.

The opening sentence `asya svabhāvānāṃ tu kartavya` is compressed and textually awkward. Its argumentative function is nevertheless clear from context: after Chapter 7 has treated qualities under the heading of knowledge, the present chapter turns to the meditative bases or natures through which those qualities are supported.

## Natural Argument

### 1. Why Chapter 8 begins with dhyāna

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

Chapter 7 has explained qualities consisting in or governed by knowledge (`jñānamayā guṇāḥ`). Vasubandhu now begins with the dhyānas because they are the **support of all qualities** (`sarvaguṇāśrayatvāt`). This is the explicit bridge from Jñāna to Samāpatti.

The four dhyānas are twofold in a compressed sense:

```text
upapatti-dhyāna
    dhyāna as corresponding rebirth

samāpatti-dhyāna
    dhyāna as meditative attainment
```

The rebirth-side was already explained in the Lokanirdeśa. Chapter 8 therefore takes the attainment-side as its proper task.

### 2. Samāpatti-dhyāna is wholesome one-pointedness

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

In general, dhyāna is `kuśala-citta-ekāgratā`, wholesome one-pointedness of mind, because its nature is samādhi (`samādhi-svabhāvatvāt`). Taken together with its retinue or accompaniment (`saparivāra`), however, it has the nature of the five aggregates.

This confirms and sharpens the first-pass distinction:

```text
defining nature
    kuśala-citta-ekāgratā

concrete accompanied constitution
    pañca-skandha
```

### 3. What is ekāgratā?

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

Vasubandhu immediately asks: what is this `ekāgratā`?

His first answer is exact:

```text
ekāgratā
    = ekālambanatā
    = having one object-support
```

This definition creates a problem rather than closing the discussion.

**Voice: Objector**

If one-pointedness simply means having one object-support, then minds themselves are one-objected. Samādhi would therefore not be a distinct mental factor (`caitasika dharmāntara`).

**Voice: Vasubandhu / doctrinal reply**

The reply distinguishes the mind from the determination by which it operates one-pointedly:

```text
not:
    minds themselves = samādhi

rather:
    that dharma by which minds operate as one-pointed
        = samādhi
        = cittaikāgratā
```

Samādhi is therefore not simply the bare numerical fact that one moment of mind has one object. It is the operative dharma through which the mind is collected upon one object-support.

### 4. The momentariness objection

**Voice: Objector**

Because every mind is momentary, is not every mind already one-pointed, since it cannot scatter from that moment toward a second object? If so, an associated samādhi becomes redundant.

**Voice: doctrinal reply**

The reply asks why the mind's single-objectedness should not be attributed precisely to samādhi. But another difficulty follows: samādhi is a `mahābhūmika`, a universally occurring mental factor. Would this not imply that every mind is equally one-pointed?

The answer is that samādhi can be weak (`durbala`). Its universal occurrence does not entail equal strength or the perfected one-pointedness characteristic of dhyāna.

### 5. An alternative position: the one-pointed minds themselves are samādhi

**Voice: Other teachers (`apare`)**

Others identify samādhi with the one-pointed minds themselves: `cittāny evaikāgrāṇi samādhiḥ`.

They appeal to sūtra language in which the four dhyānas are taught as the training in higher mind (`adhicittaṃ śikṣā`) and as principally concerned with purification of mind (`cittapariśuddhipradhāna`).

The Bhāṣya thus preserves an actual doctrinal dispute about whether samādhi is:

```text
a distinct associated mental dharma
    by which mind is one-pointed

or

the one-pointed mind itself
```

The passage does not license us to erase this disagreement.

### 6. What does dhyāna mean?

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

Vasubandhu next asks for the meaning of the word `dhyāna`:

```text
dhyāyanti anena
    by means of it they dhyāna

prajānanti ity arthaḥ
    that is: they know
```

The reason is decisive:

```text
samāhita-citta
    a collected mind
        ↓
    yathābhūta-prajñāna
    knowing as it is
```

He then makes the lexical-doctrinal connection explicit: the verbal root has the sense of `cintana`, and `cintana` is, according to the established doctrine, `prajñā`.

Dhyāna therefore cannot be reduced in this passage to mere attentional fixation. Its excellence is measured by the fact that collectedness supports knowing-as-it-is.

### 7. Why is not every samādhi called dhyāna?

**Voice: Objector**

If dhyāna is explained through samādhi and knowing, would every samādhi then count as dhyāna?

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

No. The name is applied to the samādhi possessing excellence or intensity (`prakarṣa`), just as a particularly luminous thing receives the name `bhāskara`.

What samādhi possesses this excellence?

```text
aṅga-samāyukta samādhi
    samādhi conjoined with the dhyāna factors
```

Its decisive feature is that **śamatha and vipaśyanā proceed yoked together** (`yuganddha-vāhitva`). For this reason it is called a pleasant abiding in the present life (`dṛṣṭadharmasukhavihāra`) and a pleasant path of practice (`sukhā pratipad`). Through such samādhi one knows or dhyānas in a superior degree (`sutarāṃ tena dhyāyanti`).

### 8. How can a defiled state be called dhyāna?

**Voice: Objector**

How can something defiled (`kliṣṭa`) still be called dhyāna?

**Voice: reply**

Because it involves a false or distorted contemplation (`mithyopanidhyāna`). The objection that this would make the designation excessively broad is answered by resemblance: the name is imposed on what resembles genuine dhyāna, like a rotten seed still being called a seed. Vasubandhu adds that the Buddha himself speaks of unwholesome dhyānas.

This closes the natural argument attached to VAK 8.01. VAK 8.02 then begins the internal differentiation of the four wholesome dhyānas.

## New Technical Terms

- **sarvaguṇāśraya** — support of all qualities
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only formulation in this opening
  - **function:** explains why the chapter turns from knowledge to dhyāna

- **kuśala-citta-ekāgratā** — wholesome one-pointedness of mind
  - **status:** prose expansion of `śubhaikāgrya`
  - **function:** definitional unpacking of samāpatti-dhyāna

- **ekālambanatā** — having one object-support
  - **status:** absent from the Kārikā
  - **function:** definition of `ekāgratā`

- **caitasika dharmāntara** — a distinct associated mental dharma
  - **status:** prose argumentative vocabulary
  - **function:** states the ontological issue raised by the definition of samādhi

- **durbala** — weak
  - **status:** absent from the Kārikā
  - **function:** explains how samādhi can occur generally without every mind exhibiting perfected one-pointedness

- **adhicitta-śikṣā** — training in higher mind
  - **status:** scriptural expression cited by the alternative position
  - **function:** evidence for identifying samādhi with the purified one-pointed mind itself

- **yathābhūta-prajñāna** — knowing according to how things are
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only formulation here
  - **function:** explains the cognitive meaning of dhyāna

- **prakarṣa** — excellence, intensification, superior degree
  - **status:** absent from the Kārikā
  - **function:** restricts the technical name `dhyāna` to superior samādhi

- **aṅga-samāyukta** — conjoined with the constituent factors
  - **status:** absent from VAK 8.01
  - **function:** specifies the samādhi possessing `prakarṣa`

- **yuganddha-vāhitva** — proceeding yoked together
  - **status:** absent from the Kārikā
  - **function:** defines the coordinated operation of śamatha and vipaśyanā in excellent dhyāna

- **mithyopanidhyāna** — false or distorted contemplation
  - **status:** absent from the Kārikā
  - **function:** explains the derivative use of `dhyāna` for defiled states

- **pratirūpa** — resemblance, likeness
  - **status:** Bhāṣya argumentative vocabulary
  - **function:** controls the extended naming of defiled states as dhyāna

## Doctrinal Determination

VAK 8.01 becomes considerably richer under the Bhāṣya.

First, Chapter 8 is explicitly generated from Chapter 7. The transition is not simply topical:

```text
knowledge-qualities
    require a support
        ↓
dhyānas
    are the support of all qualities
```

Second, `samāpatti` is not introduced as a mysterious higher faculty. The text gives a very technical initial determination:

```text
samāpatti-dhyāna
    = wholesome one-pointedness of mind
    = samādhi in its defining nature
```

Third, `ekāgratā` is not left metaphorical. It is defined as `ekālambanatā`, one-object-supportedness. The Bhāṣya then tests whether this one-objectedness belongs to mind itself or to a distinct samādhi factor. This is a genuine Abhidharma ontological dispute, not merely a verbal gloss.

Fourth, the meaning of `dhyāna` is explicitly cognitive. Vasubandhu glosses `dhyāyanti` by `prajānanti` and explains the relation through `yathābhūta-prajñāna`. Collectedness matters because it enables knowing things as they are.

Fifth, technically excellent dhyāna is not bare samādhi. It is samādhi conjoined with its factors and characterized by the yoked functioning of `śamatha` and `vipaśyanā`.

The opening doctrinal structure is therefore:

```text
samādhi
    collectedness / one-pointed operative determination

excellent samādhi
    aṅga-samāyukta
    śamatha-vipaśyanā yoked together

thereby
    yathābhūta-prajñāna
    knowing as it is

this superior form
    receives the name dhyāna
```

## Translation Consequences

**Clarification and partial revision recommended.**

The existing Kārikā translation of `śubhaikāgryaṃ` as “wholesome one-pointedness” remains sound, but the Bhāṣya gives it a precise technical expansion:

> `śubhaikāgrya` = `kuśala-citta-ekāgratā`, wholesome one-pointedness of mind, where `ekāgratā` is `ekālambanatā`, having one object-support.

The first-pass philosophical wording “the attained state is ... a complete five-aggregate configuration” should be kept under tighter doctrinal control. Vasubandhu says `saparivāraṃ ... pañcaskandhasvabhāvam`: **with its retinue/accompaniment, it has the nature of the five aggregates.** The point is association and full accompanied constitution, not an independently asserted theory of “complete configuration.”

Most importantly, the Bhāṣya requires a correction to any reading that treats dhyāna as mere concentration. In this passage:

```text
dhyāyanti
    = prajānanti
```

and the ground is the capacity of a collected mind for `yathābhūta-prajñāna`.

## Logical Determination

The first Bhāṣya unit produces a sequence of determinations rather than merely defining a term:

```text
Knowledge-qualities
    require support
        ↓
Dhyāna
    support of qualities
        ↓
Samāpatti-dhyāna
    wholesome one-pointedness
        ↓
Ekāgratā
    one object-support
        ↓
Samādhi problem
    mind itself?
    or dharma making mind one-pointed?
        ↓
Strength distinction
    universal presence of samādhi
    ≠ perfected collectedness
        ↓
Excellent samādhi
    factors conjoined
    śamatha-vipaśyanā yoked
        ↓
Dhyāna
    knowing-as-it-is in collected mind
```

The crucial logical gain is that **unity of cognition is treated as operative, not merely numerical**. The object is one, but this alone does not yet explain dhyāna. The text searches for the determination by which cognition remains gathered upon that object and then distinguishes ordinary or weak samādhi from the intensified, factor-complete form deserving the name dhyāna.

A second gain is the reciprocal relation of collectedness and knowing:

```text
samāhita-citta
    enables
        yathābhūta-prajñāna

and dhyāna receives its name
    through this knowing
```

This is stronger than a simple sequence “concentrate first, know later.” The Bhāṣya defines the excellence of the meditative state through the cognitive actuality it makes possible.

## Light Organon Contact

This passage strongly disciplines the Chapter 8 interpretation.

We should not begin from “third eye,” Buddhi, or any preselected architecture. Vasubandhu gives us a more precise native structure to follow:

```text
ālambana
    determinate object-support

samādhi
    operative gathering of mind upon that support

śamatha + vipaśyanā
    yoked operation in excellent samādhi

prajñāna
    knowing-as-it-is made actual in the collected mind
```

The important Organon contact point is therefore not yet a special faculty of vision. It is the relation between **unity of cognitive operation and objective knowing**.

A merely static one-object state is insufficient. Dhyāna is excellent where the gathering of the field supports the discriminative actuality of knowing. The text itself therefore prevents both reductions:

```text
samāpatti ≠ passive trance
samāpatti ≠ bare intellectual analysis
```

The operative middle is already named by Vasubandhu:

```text
śamatha
    +
vipaśyanā
    ↓
yuganddha-vāhitva
    ↓
excellent dhyāna
    ↓
yathābhūta-prajñāna
```

That relation should be allowed to develop through the rest of Chapter 8 before we identify it with a larger Organon architecture.
