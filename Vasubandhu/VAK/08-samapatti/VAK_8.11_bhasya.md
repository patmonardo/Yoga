# VAK_8.11 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

> aṣṭāpakṣālamuktatvād āniñjaṃ tu caturthakam /
> vitarkacārau śvāsau ca sukhādi ca catuṣṭayam // VAK_8.11 //

**Best translation:**  
Because it is free from eight disturbances, the fourth dhyāna is immovable: vitarka and vicāra, inhalation and exhalation, and the four beginning with pleasure.

**Kārikā analysis:** [`VAK_8.11.md`](./VAK_8.11.md)

## Bhāṣya Source

**Reference:** VAkK_8.11; Pradhan 441|11–441|19.

```text
trīṇi ca dhyānāni señjitāni uktāni bhagavatā /
sāpakṣālatvāt /

aṣṭāpakṣālamuktatvādāniñjaṃ tu caturthakam /

ke punaste 'pakṣālāḥ /

vitarkacārau śvāsau ca sukhādi ca catuṣṭayam // VAkK_8.11 //

vitarkavicārau sukhaduḥkhe saumanasyadaurmanasye śvāsapraśvāsāśca /
eṣāmaṣṭānāmeko'pyapakṣālaścaturthe nāstyatastadāneñjyamuktam /
vitarkavicāraprītisukhairakampanīyatvādāneñjyaṃ caturthaṃ dhyānaṃ sūtre nirvāta pradīpanidarśanādityapare /
dvayordhyānayoḥ saumanasyamuktaṃ prītivacanāt /
tṛtīye sukhaṃ caturthe upekṣā /
```

## Source Caution

The e-text contains the form `señjitāni`, evidently representing the technical contrast with `āniñja`; this file preserves the source reading rather than silently normalizing it. The natural argument is clear even though the exact transmitted form is uncertain.

## Natural Argument

Vasubandhu begins from a scriptural contrast: the first three dhyānas are described as subject to movement or disturbance because they still possess `apakṣāla`, whereas the fourth is `āniñja`, immovable.

He then asks what the disturbances are and gives an exhaustive eightfold list:

```text
vitarka
vicāra
sukha
duḥkha
saumanasya
daurmanasya
śvāsa
praśvāsa
```

The fourth dhyāna contains none of these eight. Its immovability is therefore not an abstract predicate but the result of a determinate exclusion.

A second doctrinal voice is then recorded: some explain the fourth as immovable because it cannot be shaken by vitarka, vicāra, prīti, or sukha, citing the sūtra image of a lamp protected from wind.

The passage closes by fixing the feeling-tone of the four dhyānas: the first two are associated with `saumanasya` through the term `prīti`, the third with `sukha`, and the fourth with `upekṣā`.

## Argument Reconstruction

### 1. Why are the first three not immovable?

Because they still contain determinate sources of movement (`apakṣāla`).

### 2. Why is the fourth called `āniñja`?

Because not even one of the eight disturbances is present there:

```text
cognitive movement
    vitarka, vicāra

affective movement
    sukha, duḥkha, saumanasya, daurmanasya

vital movement
    inhalation, exhalation
```

### 3. Alternative explanation

**Voice: uncertain / alternative authorities**

The fourth is immovable because vitarka, vicāra, prīti, and sukha cannot shake it; the comparison is to a lamp in a windless place.

### 4. Result

The fourth dhyāna is defined through completed freedom from the classes of disturbance still characteristic of lower dhyānas.

## New Technical Terms

- **apakṣāla** — disturbance, shaking, destabilizing factor
  - **status:** rare in the Kārikās
  - **function:** definition / criterion distinguishing movable from immovable dhyāna

- **āniñja / āneñjya** — immovable, unshaken; the state of immovability
  - **status:** previously encountered in the broader Kośa as a technical class, but newly decisive here for dhyāna structure
  - **function:** doctrinal determination of the fourth dhyāna

- **akampanīyatva** — incapacity to be shaken
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only in this local passage
  - **function:** alternative explanatory gloss of immovability

- **nirvāta-pradīpa-nidarśana** — illustration of a lamp in a windless place
  - **status:** absent from the Kārikā
  - **function:** scriptural/example support for the alternative account

## Doctrinal Determination

The Bhāṣya confirms the Kārikā's eightfold analysis and makes its structure explicit.

| Class | Disturbances excluded in fourth dhyāna |
|---|---|
| cognitive | vitarka, vicāra |
| affective | sukha, duḥkha, saumanasya, daurmanasya |
| vital | śvāsa, praśvāsa |

The fourth dhyāna is therefore not merely the highest member of a numerical sequence. It is the first form-dhyāna in which these three classes of disturbance are simultaneously absent.

The closing prose also clarifies the feeling distribution:

```text
first and second dhyānas
    saumanasya, expressed through prīti

third dhyāna
    sukha

fourth dhyāna
    upekṣā
```

This prepares the transition to VAK 8.12, which distinguishes the feeling-tones of meditative attainment from those of rebirth in the corresponding dhyāna worlds.

## Translation Consequences

**Clarification only.**

The existing translation of `āniñja` as "immovable" or "unshaken" is strengthened. The Bhāṣya shows that this is a technical predicate defined by the absence of eight specific disturbances.

`apakṣāla` should not be weakened to a vague "fault" where the context requires active destabilization. "Disturbance" or "shaking factor" better preserves the contrast with `āniñja`.

## Logical Determination

The verse and Bhāṣya define a positive state through exhaustive determinate negation:

```text
fourth dhyāna
    = not vitarka
    = not vicāra
    = not sukha
    = not duḥkha
    = not saumanasya
    = not daurmanasya
    = not inhalation
    = not exhalation

therefore
    āniñja
```

The crucial point is that the result is not indeterminate absence. The excluded field is finite and explicitly enumerated. Immovability is therefore a constituted state whose stability is intelligible from the precise disturbances no longer operative.

## Light Organon Contact

Kept deliberately close to the text: VAK 8.11 is an unusually clean example of a determination becoming positive through a complete specification of its negations.

```text
not disturbed
    by itself = abstract

free from these eight disturbances
    = determinate meditative state
```

That is enough for the present pass. The stronger systems interpretation can remain reserved.
