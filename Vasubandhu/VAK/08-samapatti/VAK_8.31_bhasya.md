# VAK_8.31_bhasya

## Kārikā

**IAST**

`dhyānayor muditā anyāni ṣaṭsu kecit tu pañcasu /`
`na taiḥ prahāṇaṃ nṛṣv eva janyante tryanvito dhruvam // VAK_8.31 //`

**Best Current Translation**

Sympathetic joy occurs in the first two Dhyānas; the other three immeasurables occur on six levels, though some hold that they occur on five. The afflictions are not abandoned through these practices. They are first generated only among humans. One who has attained the immeasurables is certainly endowed with three.

## Natural Argument

Vasubandhu first distributes the four immeasurables across meditative levels. Muditā is confined to the first and second dhyānas because it is constituted by gladness (`saumanasya`). Maitrī, karuṇā, and upekṣā occur on six levels: anāgamya, dhyānāntara, and the four dhyānas. Alternative Abhidharma positions reduce the number to five by excluding anāgamya, or expand it to ten by including kāmadhātu and the neighboring attainments.

The Bhāṣya then asks whether, since the apramāṇas were called counterforces to ill will and related afflictions, they actually abandon those afflictions. Vasubandhu answers no. They belong to fundamental dhyāna levels, operate through intentional resolution (`adhimukti-manaskāra`), and take sentient beings as their object-support. Their practical cultivation suppresses or holds down (`viṣkambhaṇa`) ill will and related afflictions, but actual abandonment is effected by the paths of abandonment (`prahāṇamārga`). The apramāṇas therefore prepare and stabilize the field in which abandonment can occur without themselves being the abandoning cognition.

Vasubandhu next gives a practical account of how a beginner cultivates maitrī. One begins from an already intelligible case of happiness: oneself, another known to be happy, or exemplary figures such as Buddhas, Bodhisattvas, and noble disciples. One forms the resolution, “May beings thus be happy.” If strong affliction prevents universalization immediately, the field is divided into progressively more difficult classes. Friendliness is first stabilized toward strongly liked friends, then middling and mildly liked friends; next toward neutral persons; then toward mildly, moderately, and strongly hostile persons. When the same resolution toward happiness no longer recedes even in relation to the strongest enemy, its scope is expanded successively to village, region, one direction, and finally the whole world.

Karuṇā and muditā are cultivated analogously, through the resolutions that beings be freed from their many forms of suffering and that they greatly rejoice. Upekṣā begins instead from the neutral class. Thus the four apramāṇas are not merely affective dispositions but deliberately constructed fields of relation.

Their initial generation occurs only among humans. Finally, Vasubandhu clarifies the compressed `tryanvito dhruvam`: possession of one apramāṇa does not imply possession of all four. A being born in the third or fourth dhyāna can lack muditā, because muditā belongs only to the first two dhyānas; nevertheless one who has attained the apramāṇas is always endowed with three.

## Argument Reconstruction

1. The apramāṇas are meditative qualities and therefore require determinate supporting levels.
2. Because muditā is gladness, its range is limited to levels where gladness is available.
3. Maitrī, karuṇā, and upekṣā have a wider meditative range.
4. A practice may be a counterforce to an affliction without being the cognition that abandons it.
5. The apramāṇas suppress hostile and possessive tendencies by reorganizing the relational field.
6. Abandonment requires the distinct path-cognition whose function is prahāṇa.
7. Universal relation is generated progressively: easy relation → neutral relation → hostile relation → spatially universal relation.
8. Therefore the “immeasurable” is not given immediately as an abstract infinity; it is produced through disciplined expansion and equalization of a determinate field.
9. The practices are first generated in human existence.
10. Attainment guarantees possession of three, though muditā can be absent in the higher dhyānas.

## New Technical Terms

- **adhimukti-manaskāra** — intentional or resolutive attention; here the cognitive mode through which the practitioner projects the welfare-form onto beings. **Status:** Bhāṣya-explicit. **Function:** explains why the apramāṇas suppress affliction without directly abandoning it.
- **viṣkambhaṇa** — suppression, blocking, holding down. **Status:** Bhāṣya-only clarification in this unit. **Function:** distinguishes antidotal stabilization from final abandonment.
- **prahāṇamārga** — path of abandonment. **Status:** established path vocabulary. **Function:** cognition that actually abandons affliction, contrasted with apramāṇa practice.
- **mitrapakṣa** — class or side of friends. **Status:** Bhāṣya pedagogical vocabulary. **Function:** first relational field for graduated maitrī cultivation.
- **udāsīnapakṣa** — neutral class. **Status:** Bhāṣya pedagogical vocabulary. **Function:** middle stage in equalizing friendliness.
- **śatrūpakṣa** — enemy class. **Status:** Bhāṣya pedagogical vocabulary. **Function:** difficult field through which maitrī becomes non-partial.
- **guṇagrāhin** — one who apprehends qualities. **Status:** Bhāṣya-only in this unit. **Function:** explains rapid generation of equal friendliness through attention to virtues rather than defects.

## Doctrinal Determination

The decisive distinction is:

```text
apramāṇa-practice
    → viṣkambhaṇa
    → suppression / stabilization

prahāṇamārga
    → prahāṇa
    → abandonment
```

The four immeasurables therefore belong to liberation without being reducible to the abandoning path. They construct a non-hostile, non-cruel, non-envious, and non-appropriative relation to beings, while the path-cognition removes the afflictive ground itself.

The training sequence also shows that `apramāṇa` means more than indefinitely large extension. Its immeasurability is generated by overcoming graded partiality. The practice reaches universality only when the same relational determination remains stable across friend, neutral person, and enemy, and is then expanded without remainder across the inhabited world.

## Translation Consequences

- `na taiḥ prahāṇam` should be translated strongly: **“abandonment is not effected by them,”** not merely “they do not remove much.”
- `viṣkambhaṇa` must remain distinct from `prahāṇa`: suppression is not eradication.
- `adhimukti` here is best rendered as resolutive or intentional cognition, not as a claim that the imagined happiness is already descriptively actual.
- `tryanvito dhruvam` is clarified by the Bhāṣya as a minimum conjunction rule: the attainer is invariably endowed with three, not necessarily all four.

## Logical Determination

This passage gives one of the clearest procedural models in the chapter:

```text
local relation
    → graded equalization
    → neutralization of preference
    → inclusion of opposition
    → unrestricted extension
```

Universality is therefore **generated**, not presupposed.

The Bhāṣya also sharply distinguishes two transformations of a field:

```text
regulation of the field
    ≠
removal of its afflictive ground
```

The apramāṇas regulate the field of cognition and relation so that hostile determinants no longer dominate it. Prahāṇa removes the underlying afflictive condition through a different path-act.

## Light Organon Contact

This is a particularly strong candidate for later treatment as a transcendental technē. The practitioner does not merely entertain a universal proposition such as “all beings should be happy.” The universality is constructed genetically by transforming a finite relational field until the same rule remains valid through differences of attraction, neutrality, and opposition.

That gives a provisional formal pattern:

```text
friend
neutral
enemy
    ↓
equalized relational determination
    ↓
local field
regional field
directional field
world-field
```

The result is a regulated field of cognition and relation. This is highly relevant to a later Theory of Māyā, but the present Bhāṣya file leaves that larger systematic interpretation subordinate to Vasubandhu's explicit procedure.
