# VAK_8.30_bhasya

## Kārikā

**IAST**

`upekṣālobhaḥ ākāraḥ sukhitā duḥkhitā vata /`
`modantām iti sattvāś ca kāmasattvās tu gocaraḥ // VAK_8.30 //`

**Best Current Translation**

Equanimity is non-greed. Their modes are: “May beings be happy,” “Alas, they suffer,” “May they rejoice,” and simply “beings.” Beings of the desire realm are their object-field.

See: [VAK_8.30.md](./VAK_8.30.md)

## Natural Argument

Vasubandhu first completes the constitutional analysis of the four immeasurables by identifying **upekṣā** with **alobha**, non-greed or non-attachment. He immediately anticipates an objection: if equanimity is constituted by non-greed, how can it function as an antidote to ill will? His answer is relational rather than taxonomic: ill will is drawn or conditioned by greed (`lobhākṛṣṭatvāt`). He adds that a dual constitution would therefore also be intelligible.

He then turns from constitution to **ākāra**, the determinate mode under which each immeasurable operates. The practitioner enters maitrī while attending to beings as “may they be happy”; karuṇā as “alas, beings suffer”; muditā as “may beings rejoice”; and upekṣā by attending simply to “beings,” because equanimity is characterized by neutrality (`mādhyasthya`).

An objection follows. If beings are not in fact happy, does resolving upon their happiness amount to distorted cognition (`viparītatva`)? Vasubandhu answers that the intention is optative—“may they be so” (`santu`)—and therefore the aspiration is not mistaken. Alternatively, the disposition or intention (`āśaya`) is not erroneous because the cognition is one of resolve or imaginative determination (`adhimukti`). Even granting some divergence from present fact, this would not make the state unwholesome, because it is rooted in wholesome roots and functions as antidote to ill will and related afflictions.

Finally, Vasubandhu specifies the **object-field** (`gocara`, `ālambana`) of the immeasurables: beings of the desire realm. This follows from their antidotal function, because the ill will and related distortions that they oppose arise with those beings as object. The scriptural instruction to extend the immeasurable in one direction is explained through the relation between the containing world and the beings contained within it.

## Argument Reconstruction

1. Upekṣā is constituted by alobha.
2. This can oppose ill will because ill will is entangled with or drawn by greed.
3. The immeasurables are not vague moods; each has a determinate ākāra.
4. Maitrī attends to beings under the aspiration for happiness.
5. Karuṇā attends to beings under suffering.
6. Muditā attends to beings under rejoicing.
7. Upekṣā attends simply to beings, expressing neutrality rather than appropriation or aversion.
8. Aspirational cognition is not false merely because its wished-for state is not presently actual.
9. Its ethical status is secured by its wholesome root and antidotal function.
10. The immediate object-field of these practices is desire-realm beings, because those are the beings toward whom the opposed afflictions arise.

## New Technical Terms

- **lobhākṛṣṭa** — drawn or conditioned by greed; **status:** Bhāṣya-specific formulation in this passage; **function:** explains how alobha can counter ill will.
- **mādhyasthya** — neutrality, impartial middle-standing; **status:** explanatory Bhāṣya term; **function:** grounds the minimal object-mode of upekṣā.
- **āśaya** — intention, disposition, underlying orientation; **status:** broader Buddhist technical term; **function:** distinguishes aspirational correctness from present-state description.
- **adhimukti** — resolve, determination, imaginative commitment; **status:** previously relevant contemplative vocabulary; **function:** explains the cognitive form of the apramāṇa aspiration.
- **gocara** — object-field, range; **status:** Kārikā term; **function:** specifies the beings toward whom the immeasurables are immediately directed.

## Voice

- **Vasubandhu:** upekṣā as alobha; explanation through greed; specification of the four ākāras; defense of aspirational cognition; desire-realm beings as object-field.
- **Objector:** asks how alobha can oppose ill will; challenges the apparent falsity of imagining happiness for beings who are not happy; suggests such falsity would make the state unwholesome.
- **Vasubandhu:** replies that the cognition is optative/resolutive and rooted in wholesome antidotal factors.

## Doctrinal Determination

The four immeasurables are not defined merely by affective tone. Their practice requires a conjunction of:

- a constitutive mental factor,
- a determinate ākāra,
- an intentional or aspirational orientation,
- a specific object-field,
- and an antidotal function.

Upekṣā is especially instructive. Its neutrality is not indifference. As alobha, it is non-appropriative relation; as mādhyasthya, it attends to beings without the preferential intensification that characterizes attachment and aversion.

The Bhāṣya also blocks a crude representational reading of meditation. “May beings be happy” need not describe an already actual state. Its truth-condition is not identical with ordinary assertoric description because it operates as aspiration or resolve.

## Translation Consequences

- **upekṣā** should not be rendered as emotional indifference; “equanimity” remains preferable when understood as impartial, non-appropriative relation.
- **alobha** is better translated here as “non-greed / non-attachment” than merely “absence of desire,” because its function concerns the loosening of possessive determination.
- **ākāra** should remain “aspect” or “determinate mode,” not be weakened to “feeling.”
- **gocara** is best kept as “object-field” or “range,” while `ālambana` in the gloss remains “object-support.”
- The optative force of `santu` must be preserved: maitrī is not the false assertion that all beings are presently happy.

## Logical Determination

VAK 8.30 exposes the immeasurables as a compact system of **relation-design**.

The practice does not merely replace one feeling with another. It determines:

```text
object-field
    +
mode of apprehension
    +
intentional orientation
    +
antidotal function
```

The passage also distinguishes **assertoric cognition** from **practical or optative cognition**. “May beings be happy” is not falsified by the fact that beings presently suffer, because it does not claim that present happiness is already actual. Its form is purposive determination.

This is important for any rational reconstruction of Buddhist contemplative technique: a cognition may be normatively and practically valid without functioning as a descriptive proposition about an already-given state of affairs.

## Light Organon Contact

This is one of the clearest places where the Kośa looks like a technical discipline for system-building. Vasubandhu separates dimensions that an undisciplined account would collapse:

```text
what the act is
what aspect it applies
what object it takes
what intention governs it
what distortion it counters
```

That separation is exactly what makes later synthesis possible without conceptual sloppiness.

Upekṣā is particularly valuable for the Organon vocabulary. It is not an empty universal that ignores differences. It is a universal relation whose form excludes appropriation while leaving the determinate object-field intact.

The larger apramāṇa movement can therefore be stated cautiously as:

```text
finite affective determination
    → identified as restriction
    → counter-determination cultivated
    → relation universalized across beings
```

The Bhāṣya remains practical and psychological; the transcendental use of this structure belongs to our later reconstruction, not to Vasubandhu's explicit claim.
