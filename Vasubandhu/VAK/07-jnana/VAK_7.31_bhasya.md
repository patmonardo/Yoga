# VAK_7.31 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

> nārāyaṇabalaṃ kāye saṃdhiṣv anye daśādhikam /
> hastyādisaptakabalaṃ spraṣṭavyāyatanaṃ ca tat // VAK_7.31 //

**Best current translation:**

The Buddha is said to possess Nārāyaṇa-strength in his body; according to others, in each bodily joint. Its measure is given through a seven-stage ascending series beginning with elephant-strength, each grade increasing tenfold. This bodily strength belongs to the tangible sphere.

**Kārikā analysis:** [`VAK_7.31.md`](./VAK_7.31.md)

### Source cautions

The reference e-text has several minor typographical corruptions (`āna taṃ`, `sahiṣṇu naṃ`, etc.). They are left visible in the source excerpt rather than silently normalized. The sense is clear from context.

The claim that the Buddha's bodily strength is infinite is explicitly attributed to **Bhadanta**; it should not be presented as Vasubandhu's unqualified conclusion.

## Natural Argument

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

Having completed the analysis of the Buddha's cognitive powers, the text turns to bodily power. The Buddha is said to possess Nārāyaṇa-strength in his body.

**Voice: Other authorities**

Some hold that Nārāyaṇa-strength is present in each bodily joint (`sandhau sandhau`).

**Voice: Bhadanta**

Bhadanta makes a stronger claim: just as the Buddha's mental power is unlimited, so too is his bodily power. The reason given is functional: otherwise the body would not be capable of supporting an unlimited power of knowledge.

**Voice: Vasubandhu / transmitted scholastic account**

A comparison-series is then supplied to give a measure of Nārāyaṇa-strength. The strength of ten ordinary elephants equals that of one `gandhahastin`; successive named grades increase tenfold, culminating in Nārāyaṇa-strength.

A rival reckoning gives a different numerical construction of the same culmination. Vasubandhu remarks that the larger reckoning is more fitting (`yathā tu bahutara tathā yujyate`).

Finally, the ontological classification is stated: bodily strength belongs to the tangible sphere. The primary account identifies it as a special determination/configuration of the great elements (`mahābhūtaviśeṣa`). Other authorities instead classify it as derived form (`upādāyarūpa`) distinct from the seven tangibles previously recognized.

## Argument Reconstruction

The verse and Bhāṣya distinguish three questions that must not be conflated:

1. **Where is the strength located?**
   - in the Buddha's body generally;
   - according to others, in each joint.

2. **How great is it?**
   - measured through an ascending comparative series;
   - rival numerical constructions are preserved.

3. **What kind of dharma is it?**
   - it belongs to `spraṣṭavyāyatana`, the tangible sphere;
   - on the principal account, it is a special configuration of the great elements;
   - on another account, it is derived form distinct from the seven tangibles.

Thus `bala` here is no longer a cognitive title. It is a material determination that receives its own support, magnitude, and ontological classification.

## New Technical Terms

- **nārāyaṇabala** — Nārāyaṇa-strength, the extraordinary bodily strength attributed to a Buddha.
  - **status:** new in this local section.
  - **function:** bodily-power classification and comparative magnitude.

- **sandhi** — bodily joint or articulation.
  - **status:** rare in the Kārikās.
  - **function:** specifies a rival localization of bodily power.

- **mahābhūtaviśeṣa** — a special determination/configuration of the great elements.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-level technical expression.
  - **function:** primary ontological explanation of bodily strength.

- **upādāyarūpa** — derived form.
  - **status:** previously encountered doctrinal category.
  - **function:** rival classification of bodily strength as a distinct derived material quality.

- **arthāntara** — another real thing / a distinct item.
  - **status:** common scholastic logical expression.
  - **function:** marks the rival claim that bodily strength is distinct from the seven tangibles.

## Doctrinal Determination

### Cognitive power and bodily power are categorically distinct

VAK 7.28–7.30 treated the ten `bala` as unobstructed knowledges. VAK 7.31 deliberately uses the same word for a different category:

```text
cognitive bala
    → jñāna
    → unobstructed determination of a knowable field

bodily bala
    → rūpa
    → tangible material determination
```

The shared name does not imply a shared ontological class.

### The support argument belongs to Bhadanta

The Bhāṣya's strongest claim is not Vasubandhu's own unmarked doctrine:

```text
unlimited cognitive power
    requires
body capable of supporting it
```

Bhadanta therefore infers unlimited bodily power as well.

This is philosophically interesting, but voice attribution is essential. Vasubandhu records the position; he does not simply identify cognition and body.

### Bodily power remains material

Despite its extraordinary magnitude, the Kośa refuses to make bodily power mysterious. It is classified within the tangible sphere.

The principal account is especially precise:

```text
bodily strength
    = mahābhūtaviśeṣa
    = special determination of the great elements
```

The rival view moves it into derived form, but neither account makes it a cognitive or immaterial power.

## Translation Consequences

The first-pass statement that the Bhāṣya 'preserves the view that the Buddha's extraordinary body must be capable of supporting extraordinary cognition' should be narrowed:

- the support argument is specifically **Bhadanta's position**;
- `ananta` applies there to bodily power by analogy with unlimited mental power;
- the passage's settled categorical point is not infinity but material classification within `spraṣṭavyāyatana`.

`nārāyaṇabala` should therefore remain a technical name rather than being generalized as merely 'superhuman strength.'

## Logical Determination

This verse is a useful exercise in avoiding equivocation.

```text
same lexical predicate: bala

but

bala₁ = cognitive capacity
bala₂ = bodily/material capacity
```

Their determinations differ by:

```text
substrate
object/function
measure
ontological category
```

The Bhāṣya also presents a classical inference from adequacy of support:

```text
if cognitive power is unlimited
and embodied operation requires a suitable support
then the bodily support must be adequate to that power
```

But because this inference is attributed to Bhadanta, it belongs to the recorded doctrinal field rather than to Vasubandhu's own unqualified voice.

## Organon Contact Point

The useful contact here is very restrained: a realized cognitive operation and its concrete support must not be collapsed into one category, even when one must be adequate to the other.

```text
rational/cognitive determination
    ≠
material support

but embodied realization
    requires an adequate relation between them
```

This is structurally relevant to a Rational–Empirical architecture, but the Kośa passage itself remains a material taxonomy of the Buddha's body.

## Transition

The Bhāṣya closes: `uktāni balāni` — the powers have been explained.

VAK 7.32 therefore moves to the next group among the eighteen distinctive Buddha-dharmas: the **four fearlessnesses (`vaiśāradya`)**.
