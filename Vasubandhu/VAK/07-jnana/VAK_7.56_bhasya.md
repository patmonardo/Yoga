# VAK_7.56 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

> cetojñānaṃ tu tattredhā tarkavidyākṛtaṃ ca yat /
> jānate nārakā ādau nṛṇāṃ notpattilabhikam // VAK_7.56 //

**Best current translation:**  
Knowledge of another mind acquired by birth is threefold in ethical status; corresponding knowledge may also be produced through inference or a learned science. Hell-beings possess birth-acquired other-mind knowledge initially, while humans do not possess this class of capacity merely by birth.

## Source Caution

The working e-text is based principally on Pradhan with corrections from Shastri and is not a critical edition. The compound `tarkavidyākṛtam` is unpacked by the Bhāṣya into two routes: `tārkika` knowledge associated with `naimittika` interpreters and knowledge produced by `vidyā`. The prose, rather than speculative expansion of `tarka` or `vidyā`, should control the reading here.

## Natural Argument

**Voice: Vasubandhu**

The qualification `tredhā` applies first to birth-acquired knowledge of another mind: it can be wholesome, unwholesome, or ethically indeterminate.

Vasubandhu then extends the same threefold ethical classification to two further non-cultivational forms of other-mind knowledge: inferential or sign-based knowledge (`tārkika`) of the `naimittikas`, and knowledge produced by a `vidyā`.

These are explicitly contrasted with the cultivation-result form (`bhāvanāphala`), which is wholesome only.

Birth-acquired knowledge of another mind is paired with birth-acquired recollection of former lives. Hell-beings possess these at the beginning of their existence, until overwhelming painful feeling strikes them. Beings situated in other destinies possess them continually.

Humans do not possess the previously described extraordinary capacities merely through birth. The apparent counterexample of humans who naturally remember former lives is answered by distinguishing karmic provenance: their recollection is produced by a special karma, not by ordinary human birth-acquisition.

The Bhāṣya closes with a threefold provenance for former-life recollection: cultivation-result, birth-acquired, and karma-produced.

## New Technical Terms

- **naimittika** — an interpreter or knower through signs/indications in this passage
  - **status:** absent from the Kārikā
  - **function:** specifies the bearer of `tārkika` other-mind knowledge

- **prakṛtijātismara** — a person who appears naturally to recollect former lives
  - **status:** absent from the Kārikā
  - **function:** apparent counterexample to the denial of human birth-acquired extraordinary cognition

- **karmaviśeṣaja** — produced by a special or distinctive karma
  - **status:** absent from the Kārikā
  - **function:** resolves the apparent counterexample by assigning a different causal provenance

## Doctrinal Determination

The verse does not present one generic faculty called “mind-reading.” Vasubandhu classifies similar cognitive outcomes according to acquisition source and ethical status.

The decisive contrast is:

```text
non-cultivational routes
    birth
    inference/sign-reading
    vidyā
        → three ethical possibilities

cultivation-result
        → wholesome only
```

This prevents the content of a cognition from determining its ethical or path status by itself.

Human beings provide a further caution. A human who remembers a former existence does not thereby instantiate the birth-acquired class denied to humans; the same observable capacity may instead be `karmaviśeṣaja`.

## Translation Consequences

`tredhā` should be understood through the Bhāṣya as a threefold ethical classification—`kuśala`, `akuśala`, `avyākṛta`—not as three epistemic modes by itself.

`tarka` here should remain close to inferential/sign-based knowing because Vasubandhu identifies the relevant bearers as `naimittikas`.

`vidyā-kṛta` should be left broad as “produced by a vidyā / learned technique or science” in this local passage; it should not automatically be identified with the three liberative vidyās of 7.45.

## Logical Determination

The closing verse yields a precise rule:

> **Cognitive content does not uniquely determine cognitive type. Provenance remains constitutive of classification.**

```text
same apparent cognition
    knowledge of another mind
        ↓
may differ by
    provenance
    ethical status
    bearer
    realm
    temporal availability
```

A second rule is equally important:

> **A native capability can be conditionally masked or disabled without changing the class of capacity by which it was acquired.**

Hell-beings possess the relevant birth-acquired cognition initially, but overwhelming painful feeling prevents its continued operation.

## Chapter Closure

Pradhan 431|04 explicitly closes the section:

```text
abhidharmakośabhāṣye jñānanirdeśo nāma saptamaṃ kośasthānam
```

Chapter 7 therefore concludes not with unrestricted omniscience, but with a final discrimination of how apparently similar knowledges differ by source, status, and bearer.
