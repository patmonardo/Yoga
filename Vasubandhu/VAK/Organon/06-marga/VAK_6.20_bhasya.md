# VAK 6.20 Bhāṣya — The Four Roots Conducive to Penetration

## Kārikā Anchor

> iti nirvedhabhāgīyaṃ caturdhā bhāvanāmayam |
> anāgamyāntaradhyānabhūmikaṃ dve tv adho 'pi vā || 6.20 ||

> Thus the factors conducive to penetration are fourfold and born
> of cultivation. They occur on the grounds of preliminary and
> intermediate concentration and the dhyānas; according to another
> view, the first two also occur below.

See the [official kārikā study](./VAK_6.20.md). The four are Heat,
Summits, Acceptance, and the Highest Mundane Dharmas.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

> These are the four wholesome roots conducive to penetration:
> Heat, Summits, Acceptances, and Highest Mundane Dharmas. The first
> two are weak, since they are unstable and subject to decline;
> Acceptances are medium; the Highest Mundane Dharmas are strongest.
> What does “conducive to penetration” mean? The root *vidh* concerns
> division. Determinate penetration is the Noble Path: by it doubt
> is abandoned and the Truths are distinguished as “this is
> suffering,” and so on through the Path. A portion of this
> penetration is the Path of Seeing. These roots are beneficial
> because they bring it on.
>
> All four are born of cultivation, not of hearing or reflection.
> Their grounds are preliminary concentration, intermediate
> concentration, and the four dhyānas. They are not placed higher,
> since they belong to the entourage of the Path of Seeing, which
> takes the desire-domain as an object to be known and abandoned
> first. In the form-domain their maturation comprises five
> aggregates. They complete rather than project a further existence,
> because they turn against continued becoming.
>
> “The first two may also occur below” states another opinion: the
> particle `vā` marks a doctrinal alternative. According to
> Bhadanta Ghoṣaka, Heat and Summits occur on seven grounds, also
> including the desire-domain.

The phrase about the portion of penetration and the Path of Seeing
is compressed in the witness; the translation keeps its immediate
function—explaining how these roots bring on that Path—without
equating the roots with the Path itself.

## Movement of the Commentary

`Iti` gathers the preceding graded sequence under one functional
class. The four roots differ in strength and reversibility, but all
are `nirvedhabhāgīya`: they serve the decisive penetration that
abandons doubt and discriminates the Four Truths. The Bhāṣya defines
their relation through `āvāhakatva`, bringing on, which preserves
the difference between cultivated preparation and the Noble Path.

Their mode of genesis is `bhāvanāmayam`; hearing and reflection alone
do not constitute these roots. The principal ground enumeration is
six: preliminary concentration, intermediate concentration, and
four dhyānas. The explanation excludes higher grounds in relation
to the Path of Seeing's desire-domain object. The karmic note adds
that their form-domain maturation completes without projecting a
new existence.

The final `vā` preserves a second position rather than weakening
the first into uncertainty. Bhadanta Ghoṣaka admits the first two
roots also in the desire-domain, making seven grounds for those two.
The Bhāṣya does not extend his alternative to Acceptance or the
Highest Mundane Dharmas.

## Organon Response — Cultivation Oriented to Seeing

The class is defined by what it can bring about. Four distinct
wholesome roots acquire one Path meaning through their orientation
to determinate penetration, while each retains its degree and
ground. “Penetration” is not a vague intensification: the Bhāṣya
ties it to the abandonment of doubt and discrimination of the
Truths.

For our Science of Knowing, this specifies a necessary relation
between preparation and Seeing. A truth received or reasoned must
become cultivated prajñā capable of bringing on a different Path
operation. Calling that ultimate operation knowledge of the
Absolute is our Organon proposal; Vasubandhu here gives the exact
Abhidharma conditions and the named doctrinal alternative.
