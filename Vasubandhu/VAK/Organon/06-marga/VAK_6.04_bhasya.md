# VAK 6.04 Bhāṣya — Origin and the Two Truths

## Kārikā Anchor

> yatra bhinnena tadbuddhir anyāpohe dhiyā ca tat |
> ghaṭārthavat saṃvṛtisat paramārthasad anyathā || 6.04 ||

> That whose cognition ceases when it is divided, or when other dharmas are
> excluded in thought, is conventionally real, like the object “pot.” The
> ultimately real is otherwise.

See the [official kārikā study](./VAK_6.04.md). The Bhāṣya reaches this
verse only after completing a question about the truth of origin left by
6.03's account of suffering.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

> One may object that calling this whole field the truth of origin goes
> beyond the sūtra, which names craving alone. Craving is named there
> because of its primacy; other factors also belong to origin. How is
> that known? Other scriptural statements name action, craving, and
> ignorance as causes of future existence, and describe appropriated
> consciousness and its supporting stations through the images of seed
> and field. Thus a sūtra may speak with a particular intention, while
> the Abhidharma states the defining extension.
>
> More precisely, the teaching that identifies craving alone concerns
> the cause of renewed becoming as such. A verse that mentions action,
> craving, and ignorance describes the production of a particular birth
> together with its causes. *Upapatti* is the arising of a particular
> embodied existence distinguished by domain, destiny, and mode of birth;
> *abhinirvṛtti* is reconnection to renewed becoming without specifying
> that kind. Action and craving are respectively causes of these two.
> A seed determines the kind of sprout, whereas water supports sprouting
> more generally. Craving is a cause of renewed becoming because those
> without craving are not seen to be born again, because craving bends the
> mental continuum toward its object, and because nothing clings to an
> embodied existence as strongly as attachment to self.
>
> The Blessed One has taught four truths, and also two: conventional
> truth and ultimate truth. What distinguishes them? When an object is
> divided into parts and cognition of *that object* no longer occurs,
> it is conventionally real. When a pot is broken into shards, pot-cognition
> no longer occurs. Likewise, when other dharmas are excluded through
> thought and cognition of that object no longer occurs, it is
> conventionally real. Water is the example: exclude dharmas such as
> visible form in analysis, and water-cognition no longer occurs.
>
> Conventional designation is established upon those very dharmas.
> Accordingly, those who say “a pot exists” or “water exists” speak
> truly, not falsely. That is conventional truth. Ultimate truth is
> otherwise. Cognition of the object remains even when it is divided,
> and remains when other dharmas are excluded by thought. Visible form
> is the example: even when the object is divided atom by atom and
> associated dharmas are excluded, cognition of visible form's own
> nature remains. Feeling and the other dharmas should be understood
> similarly. This is called ultimate truth because it exists ultimately.
>
> The former teachers offer a further criterion: that which is apprehended
> in the same manner by supramundane knowledge, or by the subsequent
> mundane knowledge acquired from it, is ultimate truth; what is
> apprehended otherwise is conventional truth.

## Movement of the Commentary

The lead-in prevents a gap between 6.03 and 6.04. Having established the
scope of suffering, Vasubandhu tests the scope of origin. A sūtra that
privileges craving does not exclude action and ignorance from the causal
account. The distinctions among the particular kind of birth, renewed
becoming as such, and their respective causes explain why different
scriptural formulations can all have a proper use. The seed and water
simile distinguishes specificity from general support, rather than
presenting rival single causes.

The two-truth inquiry then tests two ways an object-cognition can cease.
The pot is divided materially; water is analyzed by excluding its
constituent dharmas in thought. Both are conventionally real because
cognition of the configured object does not survive the relevant test.
Crucially, the assertion that such an object exists remains **true**.
Conventional designation depends upon dharmas; it is not a falsehood.

For `paramārtha-sat`, the Bhāṣya gives the opposite result: cognition of
the own nature persists through division or exclusion. Visible form is
its example, and feeling is included by extension. Finally, the
`pūrvācāryāḥ` offer a criterion in terms of the cognition that apprehends
the object. Their view is explicitly attributed and should not be
silently fused with the preceding analytical test.

## Doctrinal Determination

`Saṃvṛti-satya` and `paramārtha-satya` distinguish modes of truthful
designation and analysis. They do not replace the Four Truths just
discussed. Nor does the pot example say that a pot cannot be truthfully
said to exist. The discriminating question is whether the *same*
object-cognition survives decomposition. Pot and water fail under
different operations, while visible form's own-nature cognition is said
to remain. This is the analytic account presented here, not a claim
about every Buddhist account of ultimate truth.

The Sanskrit of the clause concerning the associated dharmas of visible
form is damaged in the running witness (`rasārhānapi`). The translation
“associated dharmas” follows the local research construal and remains
provisional. The transcription below does not silently replace the
damaged letters with an invented reading.

## Organon Response — Determination Through Analysis

The passage gives our synthesis a useful discipline. A unity can be
genuinely meaningful and true at the conventional level even when its
particular cognition depends on a configuration that analysis can undo.
The distinction between the configured object and the dharmas on which
its designation rests can guide later Yoga–Kośa work on *artha*.
That is a comparative use of the passage; Vasubandhu here establishes
the two tests and their doctrinal results.
