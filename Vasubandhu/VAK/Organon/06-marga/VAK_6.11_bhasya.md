# VAK 6.11 Bhāṣya — Beyond the Image, Within Its Limits

## Kārikā Anchor

> atikrānta-manaskāro bhrūmadhye citta-dhāraṇāt |
> alobho daśabhūḥ kāma-dṛśyālambā nṛjāśubhā || 6.11 ||

> One whose attention has passed beyond [the skeletal support] holds
> citta between the eyebrows. Contemplation of the unattractive has
> non-greed as its nature, occurs on ten grounds, takes desire-domain
> visible form as its object, and arises among humans.

See the [official kārikā study](./VAK_6.11.md). The first half completes
the three-stage practice begun in 6.10; the second half classifies the
practice itself.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

> Through holding citta between the eyebrows, one is called a
> practitioner whose attention has passed beyond. Having relinquished
> even the remaining half of the skull, the practitioner holds citta
> between the eyebrows. Such a practitioner is said to have passed
> beyond attention *with respect to the unattractive*.
>
> Contemplation of the unattractive admits four cases according to
> whether its object is limited in extent and whether mastery is
> limited. The cases are distinguished by mastered or unmastered
> attention and by an object extending only over one's own body or as
> far as the ocean.
>
> What is this contemplation's own nature? On how many grounds does it
> occur? What is its object? Where does it arise? In order: it has
> non-greed as its own nature; it occurs on ten grounds, including the
> desire domain and the stated dhyāna levels and their neighboring
> levels; it takes visible form of the desire domain as object. The
> object is the configuration of a visible thing. It arises only among
> humans, in no other destiny, and not even in Uttarakuru. Its
> unattractive aspect is already evident from its name.
>
> When the practice belongs to a given time, its object belongs to
> that time; when it is of a kind that will not arise, it can have an
> object across times. Because it operates through resolute attention,
> it is contaminated. It may be acquired through detachment or
> produced through application, according to prior habituation or its
> absence. The characteristics of contemplation of the unattractive,
> including its divisions, have now been stated.

The witness is especially rough in the line defining the visible
object. The translation gives its apparent doctrinal direction, not a
claim that every transmitted syllable there has been resolved.

## Movement of the Commentary

The third practitioner stage removes the final half-skull from the
constructed image. Citta is then held between the eyebrows. The
qualification `aśubhāyām` restricts `atikrāntamanaskāra`: this
practitioner has gone beyond a particular support within this
exercise, not beyond attention or cognition as such.

The four cases separate two variables: extent of the represented
object and degree of mastery. A wide field does not by itself prove
mastery, and a narrow field does not prove its absence. The subsequent
four questions classify the practice by nature, level, object, and
place of arising. Its nature is `alobha`, non-greed, consistent with
its counteraction of desire. Its grounds, desire-domain visible
object, and human locus delimit its application.

The later qualifications matter as much as the stage-name. Even a
well-mastered aśubha practice is `sāsrava` because it employs
`adhimukti-manaskāra`, resolute construction of its object. The
distinction between acquisition through detachment and production
through application further prevents one generic account of how the
practice arises. The commentary closes this topic before taking up
breath recollection.

## Organon Response — The Limit of Mastery

The practitioner's freedom with respect to the skeletal image is
real: the image can be formed, extended, contracted, and finally
released. Yet the Bhāṣya explicitly determines the whole practice as
contaminated. Thus mastery over a constructed appearance and direct
knowledge of ultimate truth cannot be identified merely because the
image has been relinquished.

For our Science of Knowing, this is a necessary discipline of the
claim “I can know the Absolute.” One must be able to identify the
status of the very operation one has mastered. Here the operation is
preparatory and resolutive; its result is not yet the absolute
knowledge we seek. This is an Organon question drawn from the
Bhāṣya's distinctions, not Vasubandhu's own assertion of our thesis.
