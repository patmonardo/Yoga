# VAK 6.34 Bhāṣya — Stream-Entry and Its Bounded Future

## Kārikā Anchor

> akṣīṇabhāvanāheyaḥ phalasthaḥ saptakṛtparaḥ |
> tricaturvidhamuktas tu dvitrijanmā kulaṃkulaḥ || 6.34 ||

> One established in the fruit whose cultivation-removable
> afflictions are not exhausted is seven-at-most. One freed from
> three or four grades, with two or three births remaining, is
> family-to-family.

See the [official kārikā study](./VAK_6.34.md). The ninefold
grading of 6.33 now determines distinct positions within the
first fruit, stream-entry.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

> With nine grades of affliction in each ground, one established
> in the fruit who has not exhausted what is abandonable through
> cultivation is “seven-at-most.” Even if a single cultivation-
> removable grade remains, such a person is a stream-enterer.
> “Seven times” concerns births; “at most” sets the upper limit.
> The scriptural reading is “seven times at most,” rather than
> exactly seven in every case. The Path is the stream toward
> Nirvāṇa, and one who has entered or attained it is called a
> stream-enterer.
>
> Does merely obtaining the first Path make one such? Then the
> “eighth person” would qualify. Does merely obtaining the first
> fruit suffice? Then one already detached from desire would also
> qualify. The designation is explained with reference to one
> who attains the fruit-Path, both Seeing and Cultivation Paths,
> and full realization of the stream at Path-Anvaya-Knowledge.
>
> The Bhāṣya next counts up to seven human rebirth-existences,
> seven intermediates, and similarly among gods. The Vaibhāṣikas
> explain the name by the common sevenfold number. Scripture,
> however, says that one endowed with right view will not produce
> an eighth existence. The reply construes this by one destination.
> A literal reading is challenged by the case of intermediate
> existence and by the one whose course rises to the summit of
> existence. Another reply restricts the statement to the desire-
> domain. The text asks for scriptural or rational evidence that
> the seven should be counted separately among gods and humans,
> instead of jointly. One reading says “seven times among gods
> and humans”; the Kāśyapīyas also read “seven times among gods,
> seven times among humans.” The discussion warns against
> insisting on one such reading. A human stream-enterer reaches
> parinirvāṇa among humans; a divine one among gods.
>
> Why no eighth existence? By then the continuum necessarily
> matures. The Path has that causal character, as the cited
> comparisons with venom and recurring fever suggest. Seven
> fetters remain, two lower and five upper. The text adds that
> such a person does not attain parinirvāṇa without bringing the
> Noble Path into presence; karmic force is involved in the
> remaining births. Even without a Buddha's appearance, the
> person may attain arhatship as a householder but does not again
> inhabit a household; the monastic appearance is acquired by
> the nature of things, while another opinion names a different
> ascetic appearance.
>
> Why is this person not subject to descent into a bad destiny?
> The relevant karma is not strengthened, the continuum is
> unsuitable for an old result, and wholesome dispositions,
> application, and intention have strength. Were bad-destiny
> karma about to ripen, the person could not even give rise to
> Acceptance. A cited verse contrasts uncontained and contained
> water to illustrate this difference in support.
>
> What is meant by “making an end of suffering”? It means that
> beyond that end no suffering involving another rebirth-link
> occurs. Or the end is Nirvāṇa. Nirvāṇa is not produced: the
> obstacle to attaining it is removed, just as one can speak of
> “making space” by removing what blocks it. Seven births are a
> maximum, not a required number. This completes the first
> classification of the fruit-established stream-enterer.
>
> “Freed from three or four grades, with two or three births,
> family-to-family”: the same stream-enterer is determined by
> three things—abandonment of three or four afflictive grades,
> acquisition of the opposing uncontaminated faculties, and
> two or three births remaining. The verse names the first and
> third; acquisition of the antidotal faculty follows from
> abandonment. The birth count may in some cases be smaller,
> so the text specifies its upper bound. Why not define this
> person by abandoning five grades? Abandoning the fifth
> necessarily entails abandoning the sixth, since one remaining
> grade cannot obstruct the next fruit. This family-to-family
> person may be divine, reaching parinirvāṇa after two or three
> divine families, or human, doing so after two or three human
> families.

The translation follows the entire argumentative course while
condensing compressed similes and the long cited verse. It
distinguishes Vasubandhu's questions and replies from the named
Vaibhāṣika and Kāśyapīya readings. The precise counting of
intermediate existences and several damaged Sanskrit forms
require collation; the stable point is that seven is an upper
bound, with the counting itself debated.

## Movement of the Commentary

**Fruit and residue.** A real first fruit can coexist with
unabandoned `bhāvanā-heya`. The expression *saptakṛtvaḥparama*
sets a maximum of seven births; it does not prescribe seven
births for everyone. The stream is the Path toward Nirvāṇa.
Vasubandhu tests whether its entry means the first Path moment
or first fruit alone, then locates the full designation in the
completed stream-realization at Path-Anvaya-Knowledge.

**Number and source voice.** The precise scope of “seven” is
argued, not merely reported. The Vaibhāṣikas explain a distributed
sevenfold count; sūtra wording raises an eighth-existence
objection. A Kāśyapīya reading distinguishes seven among gods
and seven among humans. The Bhāṣya warns against fixation on
one verbal construal. Its further account of continuum-maturation,
fetters, karma, and protection from bad destinies gives causal
content to the bound. These details should not be turned into
one unqualified arithmetic formula.

**End of suffering.** The expression does not mean making an
unconditioned Nirvāṇa come into being. It means ending rebirth-
linked suffering or removing the obstruction to Nirvāṇa's
attainment. The distinction between producing an object and
removing an obstacle matters for any claim about liberation.

**Family-to-family.** The second half correlates three axes:
three or four grades abandoned, the corresponding uncontaminated
faculty acquired, and two or three births remaining. The Bhāṣya
explains why five is not a stable class here: removal of the fifth
necessitates removal of the sixth, which a single remaining grade
cannot obstruct. Divine and human forms distinguish the domain
of the remaining births. The classification is thus a causal
profile, not merely a label for a person.

## Organon Response — A Fruit That Limits Possible Futures

For our Science of Knowing, fruit-establishment changes the
range of what can still happen. The limit of seven is a bound,
while further cultivation narrows the remaining course. The
three-or-four-grade family-to-family case connects an achieved
inner transformation with a shorter objective future. This is
our reconstruction of a Path-position's effect on the field of
possible experience; the detailed rebirth doctrine is the
Bhāṣya's own claim.

The line about Nirvāṇa is philosophically decisive here. The
Path can remove an obstruction to attainment without producing
the unconditioned as an effect. That distinction is given by
Vasubandhu. Any further identification with the Absolute in
our Kosa architecture remains a separate argument.
