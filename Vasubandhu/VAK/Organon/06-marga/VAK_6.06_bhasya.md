# VAK 6.06 Bhāṣya — The Practical Conditions of Cultivation

## Kārikā Anchor

> vyapakarṣa-dvayavato nāsantuṣṭa-mahecchayoḥ |
> labdhe bhūyaḥ-spṛhātuṣṭir alabdhēcchā mahecchatā || 6.06 ||

> Cultivation succeeds for one possessing twofold withdrawal, not for the
> discontented or one of great desire. Longing for more with respect to
> what has been obtained is discontent; desire for what has not been
> obtained is great desire.

The [official kārikā study](./VAK_6.06.md) supplies its grammar and
translation. Vasubandhu asks what allows the cultivation of 6.05 to succeed.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

> For one thus engaged in cultivation, how does cultivation succeed? It
> succeeds for a person possessing twofold withdrawal. One is withdrawn
> in body and mind through distance from association and unwholesome
> thought. For whom is such withdrawal easy? For one of few wishes and
> one who is content; not for the discontented and those of great desire.
> What are discontent and great desire? The verse says: longing for more
> with respect to what is obtained is discontent, and desire for what is
> unobtained is great desire.
>
> According to the Abhidharmikas, discontent is wanting more of fine
> robes and similar articles already obtained; great desire is wanting
> such things while they remain unobtained. But the desired increase is
> itself unobtained, even in the first case. What then differentiates
> the two? This account is more fitting: discontent is vexation because
> what one has obtained is inferior or insufficient; great desire is
> wanting excellent and abundant things one has not obtained.

## Movement of the Commentary

The shift from 6.05 is exact. After showing how Prajñā born from
samādhi arises, Vasubandhu asks what makes cultivation feasible. The
answer joins bodily withdrawal from entangling association to mental
withdrawal from unwholesome thought. Fewness of wishes and contentment
make this double withdrawal practicable. The passage therefore locates
the preparation for seeing the truths in the organization of conduct
and desire, not only in a classification of cognitions.

The inherited account distinguishes discontent and great desire by
whether the wanted goods have been obtained. Vasubandhu presses the
overlap: wanting *more* of what one has is still wanting something one
does not yet have. His proposed repair separates vexation about a
present possession from desire projected toward excellent, abundant
goods not possessed. The distinction now concerns two different
relations to an object rather than a merely verbal opposition of
“obtained” and “unobtained.”

The Bhāṣya has already named fewness of wishes and contentment as
practical supports for withdrawal. Their formal classification and
own nature are taken up in 6.07.

## Organon Response — The Ground of Path-Capacity

The Path's `śakti` in 6.01 now has a practical prehistory: the person
must be able to sustain cultivation. The two withdrawals are reciprocal
in the work of discipline: bodily distance reduces entangling occasions,
while mental withdrawal addresses the thought activity that can
reproduce them. The source establishes the two withdrawals and their
relation to fewness of wishes and contentment; their role in our wider
Yoga–Kośa Path synthesis remains an Organon determination to test.
