# VAK 6.02 Bhāṣya — The Order of Realizing the Truths

## Kārikā Anchor

> satyāny uktāni catvāri duḥkhaṃ samudayas tathā |
> nirodho mārga ity eṣāṃ yathābhisamayaṃ kramaḥ || 6.02 ||

> Four truths have been stated: suffering, origin, cessation, and Path. Their
> order accords with direct realization.

See the [official kārikā study](./VAK_6.02.md). The question arises from
6.01's “seeing the truths”: which truths, how many, and why this order?

## Continuous Conventional Translation

> “Seeing the truths” has been mentioned. What are these truths, and how
> many are there? Four truths have already been stated in the exposition of
> contaminated and uncontaminated dharmas: the truth of the Path by its own
> name as uncontaminated; the truth of cessation as analytical cessation,
> disconnection; and the truths of suffering and origin where the world was
> described as suffering and origin. Is their order here the order in which
> they were previously stated? No. It is suffering, origin, cessation, and
> Path. “Likewise” indicates that their natures remain as previously
> explained. Their present order follows direct realization.
>
> The truth realized first is taught first. Were this an order of causes,
> the cause would have been stated before its result. Some teachings follow
> an order favorable to arising, as with the establishments of mindfulness
> and the meditations. Others follow an order favorable to their exposition,
> as with the right efforts: there is no fixed rule that one first produces
> the wish to abandon what has arisen and only afterward the wish to prevent
> what has not arisen. The truths, however, are taught in an order favorable
> to direct realization.
>
> Why are they realized in this order? At the stage of examination, one
> first investigates as suffering the condition in which one stands, by
> which one is afflicted, and from which one seeks release. One then asks
> for its cause, its cessation, and the Path to its cessation. This is like
> seeing a disease and seeking its cause, removal, and medicine. A medical
> example is also given in a sūtra. As the truths are examined in this
> order, so are they directly realized in this order, through prior
> penetration; the horse simile illustrates a movement following a direction
> already discerned.
>
> What does *abhisamaya* mean? Full awakening or understanding. Why is it
> uncontaminated rather than contaminated? It is right understanding
> directed toward nirvāṇa; “right” means according to reality. The
> appropriated aggregates as results are the truth of suffering; as causes
> they are the truth of origin, since things arise from them. Their
> distinction as result and cause is one of designation, not distinct
> substance. Cessation and Path differ also in real determination.
>
> Why are these called “noble truths”? They are truths for the noble ones.
> Are they therefore false for everyone else? No: they are true for all,
> because they are not inverted. Noble ones see them as they are; others do
> not. A cited verse contrasts what noble and ordinary persons call
> pleasant and painful. Others offer a further division: two are truths of
> noble ones, and two are both noble and truths of noble ones.

## Movement and Doctrinal Determination

The verse's `krama` is a sequence of realization, not a sequence of causal
production. Within the afflicted pair, origin is cause and suffering is
result; in examination, suffering is encountered first and its cause is
then sought. Vasubandhu distinguishes an order favorable to arising, one
favorable to exposition, and one favorable to direct realization. The
Four Truths belong to the last kind.

Examination (`vyavacāraṇā`) and direct realization (`abhisamaya`) are
distinct moments. Prior penetration (`pūrvavedha`) links them without
making them identical. The Bhāṣya then characterizes *abhisamaya* as right
knowing according to reality and directed toward nirvāṇa. Its liberating
orientation helps explain why it is `anāsrava`.

The appropriated aggregates can be called suffering as result and origin
as cause. Their difference is `nāmataḥ`, according to designation, rather
than `dravyataḥ`, as two separate realities. Cessation and Path are also
distinct in the latter respect. Finally, “noble” describes the vision of
the truths; their truth is not restricted to noble persons. The closing
alternative interpretation is reported as another view, not silently
adopted as Vasubandhu's own.

## Organon Response — Truth and Path

The Path begins from what is suffered and works back to its origin, then
determines cessation and the way to it. This is a possible structure for
our Middle Way reading: causal order and the order of liberation cross
without collapsing into one sequence. The result of Chapter 5,
*parijñā*, now meets the truth-order through which Seeing becomes
intelligible. That synthesis is our reconstruction; the Bhāṣya itself
establishes the more specific rule `yathābhisamayam`.
