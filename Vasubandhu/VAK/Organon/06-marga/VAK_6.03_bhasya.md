# VAK 6.03 Bhāṣya — Three Modes of Suffering and the Reality of Pleasure

## Kārikā Anchor

> duḥkhās triduḥkhatāyogād yathāyogam aśeṣataḥ |
> manāpā amanāpāś ca tadanye caiva sāsravāḥ || 6.03 ||

> Agreeable, disagreeable, and other contaminated formations are suffering
> without remainder, according to their connection with the three modes of
> suffering in each case.

See the [official kārikā study](./VAK_6.03.md). After naming suffering as
the first truth in 6.02, Vasubandhu asks why that truth extends to *all*
contaminated formations when only some feeling is painful in its own nature.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

> If only a portion of feeling is painful by its own nature, why are all
> contaminated formations called suffering? Because there are three modes
> of suffering: the suffering of pain, the suffering of conditioned
> formation, and the suffering of change. Through these, according to what
> applies, all contaminated formations are suffering without remainder.
> Agreeable formations are subject to suffering through change;
> disagreeable ones to the suffering of pain; the others to the suffering
> of formation. Pleasant, painful, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feelings
> correspond to these classes, and formations that occasion these feelings
> receive the same designations. Some explain the three modes in a different
> way. Change and pain are stated separately because they distinguish the
> agreeable and disagreeable; all formations are also suffering through
> their conditioned character.
>
> Noble ones alone see that latter suffering. Just as a small hair is hardly
> felt on the palm but painfully felt in the eye, ordinary people fail to
> discern the suffering of formation that noble ones discern. The contrast
> extends even to the aggregates of Avīci and those at the summit of
> existence. An objection follows: if conditionedness alone made something
> suffering, the Path too would be suffering. But suffering here is what is
> adverse. The arising of the Path is not adverse to noble ones, because it
> brings about the exhaustion of suffering. When they see cessation as
> peaceful, they see the cessation of what they judged suffering, not the
> cessation of the Path.
>
> Some account for the designation “suffering” by the smallness of the
> pleasure mixed into it, or by pleasure's association with many pains and
> its tendency to produce pain. The governing answer is that noble ones
> see the whole of becoming, including pleasure, as suffering through the
> common character of conditioned suffering. Thus suffering, rather than
> pleasure, is established as the noble truth. How can a feeling pleasant
> in its own nature be seen as suffering? Because impermanence renders it
> adverse. Impermanence is discerned by arising and passing away; suffering
> by adversity. Seeing impermanence draws the aspect of suffering after it.
>
> Others now deny that any pleasant feeling exists. They appeal to sayings
> about all feeling being suffering and argue that supposed sources of
> pleasure, such as food, drink, heat, and cold, become painful through
> excess or unsuitable timing. What seems pleasant is, they say, merely
> relief from hunger, thirst, fatigue, or another pain; changing posture
> similarly exchanges one discomfort for another.
>
> The Abhidharmikas reply that pleasant feeling does exist. Ask the denier
> what pain means: if it is harmful, what benefits is pleasant; if it is
> unwanted, what is wanted is pleasant. A feeling wanted in virtue of its
> own characteristic does not later become unwanted *in that same respect*.
> Noble ones regard it under other aspects: it is a basis for heedlessness,
> difficult to secure, changing, and impermanent. They do not thereby
> deny its pleasant own characteristic. Were it inherently unwanted, no
> one would become attached to it, and there would be no need to cultivate
> dispassion by seeing its faults. Pleasant feeling therefore exists by
> its own characteristic.
>
> Sayings that call every feeling suffering concern the impermanence and
> change of formations, rather than making every feeling painful by its
> own nature. Scripture also distinguishes pleasant, painful, and neutral
> feelings, and speaks of the pleasant and joyful faculties as pleasant
> feelings to be known correctly. Pleasant feeling is pleasant by its own
> nature as agreeable, yet suffering under another aspect because it
> changes and is impermanent. Seen as simply pleasant and savored, it
> conduces to bondage; seen under suffering's aspect, it conduces through
> dispassion to liberation. The instruction to see it this way serves that
> liberating purpose.
>
> Nor can the three feelings be reduced to three intensities of pain. That
> account fails to explain pleasure arising from particular smells, tastes,
> and tangibles, or pleasure in meditation. The proposed instability of
> pleasure's causes also fails: an object causes pleasure or pain in
> dependence on the particular condition of its support, just as fire
> produces different results according to what is cooked. Pleasure on
> changing posture likewise arises in a new bodily condition; it is not
> merely the cancellation of an old pain. The discussion concludes that
> pleasant feeling really exists. All contaminated formation is called
> suffering through its relation to the three modes of suffering.

## Argument and Voices

The opening formula has two controls. `Aśeṣataḥ` makes the extension
exhaustive: no contaminated formation is omitted. `Yathāyogam` distributes
the modes according to the case; it does not require every formation to be
painful in its own characteristic. The Bhāṣya first pairs agreeable,
disagreeable, and other formations with change, pain, and formation. It
then deepens the account: conditioned suffering applies to the whole
contaminated field, while the other two modes mark distinctive cases.

The objection about the Path tests whether the argument has become too
broad. The Path is conditioned, yet its arising brings the exhaustion of
suffering. Vasubandhu therefore uses `pratikūla`, adverse, to distinguish
the first truth from mere conditionedness. This preserves the force of
the kārikā's `sāsravāḥ`.

The long controversy introduces another voice, which denies pleasant
feeling altogether. The explicit Abhidharmika answer, `astyevety
ābhidhārmikāḥ` in the research construal, affirms its existence. The
decisive distinction is between pleasure by `svalakṣaṇa` and suffering
under a further aspect (`paryāya`) of change and impermanence. These are
different determinations of the same feeling. The Buddha's instruction
to see pleasure as suffering is directed toward dispassion and liberation;
it does not erase the feeling's pleasant own characteristic.

The source also reports alternative explanations of how pleasure and
suffering relate. They remain attributed views rather than being fused
with the final Abhidharmika reply. The short cited verses and medical
examples should be checked individually before quotation in an edition.

## Organon Response — Determination and Liberation

This unit is a strong test for our Path synthesis. A pleasure can be
genuinely pleasant and still be seen, within the Path, as unstable and
liable to appropriation. The second determination changes the practical
relation to it: savoring sustains bondage; seeing its change permits
dispassion. This is not a verbal reversal or an identity of pleasure and
pain. The Path's knowledge preserves the own characteristic while
determining its place in the conditioned whole.

For the Yoga comparison, this supplies an exact Kośa analysis of how
afflictive attachment can be undone without denying what appears. That
comparative application is an Organon proposal; the source argument here
concerns the extension of suffering as the first noble truth.
