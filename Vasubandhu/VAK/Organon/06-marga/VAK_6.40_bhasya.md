# VAK 6.40 Bhāṣya — Seven Courses of the Good Person

## Kārikā Anchor

> ūrdhvasrotur abhedena sapta sadgatayo matāḥ |
> sadasadvṛttyavṛttibhyāṃ gatāpratyāgateś ca tāḥ || 6.40 ||

> By not subdividing the upward-streamer, seven good-person
> courses are recognized. They are so called because of activity
> in the wholesome, non-activity in the unwholesome, and no
> return once those courses have been entered.

See the [official kārikā study](./VAK_6.40.md). The verse answers
why the sūtra gives seven courses after the Bhāṣya has generated
a finer non-returner taxonomy.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

> How, then, does the sūtra teach seven courses of the good
> person? “Because the upward-streamer is not subdivided, seven
> good courses are recognized.” The upward-streamer is one whose
> course flows upward. The sūtra states seven good-person courses
> by naming that type without internal division. Why are these
> called good-person courses rather than the other courses of
> trainees? For those to whom these courses belong, activity
> occurs in the wholesome and does not occur in the unwholesome.
> Having entered these courses, they do not return. Those
> conditions do not obtain elsewhere in the same strict manner.
> Hence the verse says that they are seven because of wholesome
> activity, unwholesome non-activity, and non-return after entry.
>
> But a sūtra also calls a trainee endowed with right view a
> good person. Others too may be called good persons in a
> qualified sense: they acquire restraint against performing
> five kinds of evil and have for the most part abandoned the
> unwholesome. The present discussion concerns those to whom
> the designation belongs without qualification.

`Sat` and `asat` are translated through the Bhāṣya's explicit
`kuśala` and `akuśala`, not as an abstract opposition of being
and non-being. The sūtra quotation is abbreviated in the
running witness; its full external context is not reconstructed.

## Movement of the Commentary

The opening objection sets two textual scales against each
other: fine Abhidharma divisions of non-returners and a sūtra's
seven `satpuruṣa-gatayaḥ`. The answer is an enumeration rule.
The upward-streamer, previously divided by route and destination,
is counted **without subdivision**. This explains the seven
without retracting the more detailed analysis.

The commentary then asks why these are specifically courses
of the good person. Its answer has a positive operation,
**wholesome activity**; an exclusion, **non-activity in the
unwholesome**; and a directional condition, **no return after
entry**. The second half of the verse compresses these
conditions. The Bhāṣya gives their practical, rather than
ontological, sense.

A further sūtra seems to extend “good person” to a trainee
with right view. Vasubandhu grants a qualified usage for
other trainees, grounded in restraint and extensive removal
of unwholesome conduct. The current seven use the term
`niṣparyāyeṇa`, without qualification. This distinction between
qualified and strict usage prevents a wider scriptural phrase
from erasing the local classificatory criterion.

## Organon Response — A Controlled Change of Resolution

For our Science of Knowing, 6.40 supplies a disciplined way
to move between a fine causal classification and a coarser
one. When the issue is the good-person course, the internal
routes of the upward-streamer can be gathered under one
designation because the relevant operation and irreversibility
are shared. That is an Organon account of the relation between
the two textual resolutions.

The source's strict criterion is itself significant: a course
is determined by what can operate, what cannot operate, and
where return is excluded. This is a precise practical
determination. It is not yet a general claim that every
wholesome course is Absolute Reason.
