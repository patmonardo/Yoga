# VAK 6.22 Bhāṣya — Regression, Loss, and New Acquisition

## Kārikā Anchor

> ādye dve parihāṇyā ca maules tatraiva satyadṛk |
> apūrvāptir vihīneṣu hānī dve asamanvitiḥ || 6.22 ||

> The first two may also be lost through regression. One whose
> preparation belongs to a principal dhyāna sees the Truths in that
> very life. After loss, acquisition is new. Both kinds of loss
> have non-possession as their nature.

See the [official kārikā study](./VAK_6.22.md). This unit refines
6.21's contrast between Noble and ordinary modes of relinquishment.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

> An ordinary practitioner can lose the first two roots, Heat and
> Summits, through regression as well as through death. A Noble
> practitioner does not regress from them in those ways. Even an
> ordinary practitioner does not regress from Acceptance or the
> Highest Mundane Dharmas.
>
> One who produces roots conducive to penetration on a principal
> dhyāna ground necessarily sees the Truths in that very birth,
> because of intense impetus. If roots that were lost are obtained
> again, newly arising ones are obtained, not the very ones formerly
> relinquished. The commentary compares this with renewed
> prātimokṣa discipline and briefly distinguishes renewed production
> from a remaining basis from production afresh.
>
> What is the nature of relinquishment and regression? Both are
> non-possession. Regression is caused by a defect; relinquishment
> need not be, and can arise from a difference in attained quality.
> Even one who has regressed after obtaining Heat is determined
> toward final nirvāṇa. Compared with one possessing merely a root
> conducive to liberation, such a practitioner is nearer to seeing
> the Truths, provided no obstacle intervenes.

The brief sentence about renewed production at [347|16–17] is
textually difficult. Its broad contrast is given here, while its
precise syntax remains provisional.

## Movement of the Commentary

The first two roots remain regressible for an ordinary practitioner;
the latter two do not. The qualification `maula` then assigns a
same-life consequence to roots produced on a principal dhyāna
ground. The Bhāṣya gives `tīvra-saṃvega`, intense impetus, as its
reason. This claim has the stated doctrinal conditions; it is not a
general guarantee attached to any concentration experience.

The next distinction is numerical. Reacquisition after loss is
`apūrva-āpti`: newly attained dharmas arise. The former instance
does not return. Loss itself has two causal forms. `Parihāṇi` is
fault-conditioned regression; `vihāni` is relinquishment and need
not be fault-conditioned. Their shared status is
`asamanvāgama`, non-possession. Identical present status therefore
does not establish identical cause or Path significance.

The closing qualification makes this concrete. A person who has
regressed from Heat no longer possesses that attainment, yet the
Bhāṣya still calls such a person destined for final nirvāṇa and
nearer to Truth-seeing, if no obstacle intervenes. The argument
does not posit the survival of the numerically same Heat-dharma;
it assigns a changed future orientation to the continuum.

## Organon Response — Loss Does Not Erase Genesis

The Path's history matters even when an acquired state ceases. A
weaker root may regress; a later acquisition is numerically new;
and non-possession can result either from defect or from a change
in quality. The source thus refuses a simple equation between
present absence and nullification of prior cultivation.

For our Science of Knowing, this demands precision about what a
knower has achieved and what remains possible after loss. It is a
useful test of our claim to know the Absolute, but the passage
itself speaks of preparatory roots and Truth-seeing. The extension
to Absolute Knowing is Organon reconstruction.
