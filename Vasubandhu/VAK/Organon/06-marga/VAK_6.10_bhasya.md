# VAK 6.10 Bhāṣya — Expansion, Contraction, and Mastery

## Kārikā Anchor

> āsamudrāsthi-vistāra-saṃkṣepād ādikarmikaḥ |
> pādāsthna ā kapālārdha-tyāgāt kṛtajayaḥ smṛtaḥ || 6.10 ||

> The beginner extends the skeletal representation as far as the ocean
> and contracts it again. One who relinquishes the represented bones
> from the feet until half the skull remains is considered to have
> gained mastery.

See the [official kārikā study](./VAK_6.10.md). The preceding unit,
6.09, assigned contemplation of the unattractive to desire and
distinguished suppression of affliction from its abandonment.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

> A practitioner cultivating the unattractive is said to be of three
> kinds: a beginner, one who has acquired familiarity, and one whose
> attention has passed beyond. Of these, the beginner is described by
> expansion and contraction of the skeleton as far as the ocean.
>
> The practitioner who wishes to cultivate the unattractive first
> fixes the mind on a part of the practitioner's own body: the big toe,
> the forehead, or wherever attention readily settles. Resolving upon
> the bones there while removing flesh and other coverings in stages,
> the practitioner sees the complete skeleton. The practitioner then
> resolves upon a second skeleton and, in succession, upon monastery,
> park, and field, until the earth as far as the ocean is resolved upon
> as filled with skeletons. This expands `adhimokṣa`, determinate
> resolution. Contracting the field again, the practitioner resolves
> upon just one skeleton, the practitioner's own, to gather the mind.
> After a certain time, it is said, cultivation of the unattractive
> becomes established. This is the beginner practitioner.
>
> One who has gained mastery is described by relinquishing the bones
> from the feet up to half the skull. For a more refined gathering of
> mind, the practitioner sets aside the foot bones in that skeleton
> and attends to the remainder. Step by step, the practitioner sets
> aside one half of the skull and attends to the other half. This is
> the one who has acquired familiarity through progressive removal.

The witness is irregular at the point describing the removal of flesh
and related coverings. The translation follows the clear direction of
the practice sequence without claiming a secure reading of every
syllable there. The prose, rather than the transmitted `kapālārtha`,
establishes the two halves of the skull.

## Movement of the Commentary

Vasubandhu first names three practitioner stages, then explains the
first two by their operations. The beginner establishes a skeletal
object, extends it over an increasingly wide field, and contracts it
back to one skeleton. The two directions have distinct stated purposes:
expansion increases `adhimokṣa`; contraction gathers `citta`.

The second stage refines contraction. The practitioner can subtract
parts of the represented skeleton while keeping attention on what
remains. At the limit described here, half the skull is set aside and
half remains attended. The complete release of that remainder belongs
to the third stage in 6.11. The Bhāṣya's `kṛtaparijaya` explains the
kārikā's `kṛtajaya` in terms of acquired familiarity or mastery.

The transition from 6.09 remains decisive. This is training in a
counterpractice that suppresses desire; 6.10 does not say that control
over the representation has already abandoned the affliction. Its
repeated `kila` also marks points in the inherited presentation rather
than a first-person report of realization.

## Organon Response — Knowing Through a Governed Appearance

The Path works here upon an appearance it deliberately produces. The
practitioner learns its formation, range, concentration, and release.
That makes the skeletal image a governed counterobject rather than a
picture of ultimate truth. The ability to relinquish even this useful
image matters: the means of loosening desire cannot become an
unexamined object of attachment.

For our Science of Knowing, 6.10 is a precise preparatory test of
*how* cognition can command its own determinate presentation. It does
not yet establish knowledge of the Absolute. The further claim must be
earned through the Path's later distinctions between cultivated
appearance, suppression, abandonment, and direct knowing. That last
sentence is Organon reconstruction, not the Bhāṣya's own thesis.
