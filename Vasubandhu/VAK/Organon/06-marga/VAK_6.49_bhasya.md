# VAK 6.49 Bhāṣya — Peaceful Heights and Coarse Grounds

## Kārikā Anchor

> vimuktyānantaryapathā laukikās tu yathākramam /
> śāntādyudārādyākārā uttarādharagocarāḥ // 6.49 //

> But the mundane liberation-paths and uninterrupted-paths have,
> respectively, aspects beginning with peacefulness and [aspects
> beginning with coarseness], and the higher and lower [grounds] as
> their object-fields.

See the [official kārikā study](./VAK_6.49.md). The kārikā's own second
series, `udārādi`, is compressed on its face; the Bhāṣya immediately
glosses it as `audārikādi`, "coarse-etc.," and that reading is followed
here. VAK 6.48 established that supramundane liberation and
uninterrupted paths operate in truth-forms because the truths are
their object-support; 6.49 now turns to the mundane case.

## Continuous Sanskrit Witness

> vimuktyānantaryapathā laukikāstu yathākramam /
> śāntādyudārādyākārāḥ
> vimuktimārgāḥ śāntādyākārā ānantaryamārgā audārikādyākārāḥ /
> te punaryathākramam
> uktarādharagocarāḥ // VAkK_6.49 //
> vimuktimārgā uttarāṃ bhūmiṃ śāntataḥ praṇītato niḥsarṇataścākārayanti
> saṃbhavataḥ /
> ānantaryamārgā adharāṃ bhūmimaudārikato duḥkhilataḥ sthūlabhittikataśca /
> aśāntatvādaudarikato mahābhisaṃskārataratvāt /
> apraṇītatvāt duḥkhilato bahudauṣṭhulyataratvena pratikūlabhāvāt /
> sthūlabhittikatastayaiva tadbhūmyaniḥsaraṇāt bhittyaniḥsaraṇavat /
> eṣāṃ viparyayeṇa śāntapraṇītaniḥsaraṇākārāḥ /
> gata mānuṣaṅgikam /

This is the natural unit from Pradhan [368|08] through [368|17] in the
[running source](../../../Sources/kosabhasya.txt). It begins with the
kārikā's first half and ends with the closing remark `gata
mānuṣaṅgikam`, "the incidental digression is finished," which marks the
boundary with the next topic.

The witness shows several irregularities, retained without silent
correction: `niḥsarṇataḥ` for the expected `niḥsaraṇataḥ`; `audarikato`
for `audārikataḥ`; and, most notably, `uktarādharagocarāḥ` where the
kārikā itself plainly requires `uttarādharagocarāḥ`, matching its own
title-word and the Bhāṣya's later `uttarāṃ bhūmim`. This is a clear
scribal slip in this witness. This source is a working reference text,
not a critical edition; no collation is claimed.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

> “But the mundane liberation-paths and uninterrupted-paths [have
> aspects beginning with] peacefulness and coarseness, respectively.”
> The liberation-paths have aspects beginning with peacefulness; the
> uninterrupted-paths have aspects beginning with coarseness. “They,
> again respectively, have the higher and lower [grounds] as their
> object-fields.”
>
> The liberation-paths regard the higher ground as peaceful, excellent,
> and an escape, wherever these aspects apply. The uninterrupted-paths
> regard the lower ground as coarse, painful, and like a thick wall.
>
> [The lower ground is regarded] as coarse because it is not peaceful
> and because it requires a greater exertion of conditioning. [It is
> regarded] as painful because it is not excellent and because it is
> adverse through being more affected by rigidity. [It is regarded] as
> like a thick wall because there is no escape from that ground by that
> very ground, just as there is no escape through a wall. By reversal of
> these [three determinations, the higher ground is regarded] as
> peaceful, excellent, and an escape. The incidental matter is finished.

The translation preserves the source's order: the two path-types; their
respective object-fields; the higher ground's three aspects; the lower
ground's three aspects; then the three reasons for the latter and their
reversal. `Saṃbhavataḥ` is translated conservatively as "wherever these
aspects apply." `Mahābhisaṃskārataratva` is rendered literally enough as
"greater exertion of conditioning," without deciding whether the phrase
primarily names an effort of cultivation or a ground's greater degree of
construction. `Dauṣṭhulya` is rendered "rigidity" provisionally.

## Movement of the Commentary

**Two mundane path-types, two aspect-series.** Where 6.48 fixed the
supramundane liberation and uninterrupted paths to the truth-forms,
6.49 turns to their mundane counterparts and differentiates them by
what they cognize and where. The liberation-path takes the higher
ground under three positive aspects; the uninterrupted-path takes the
lower ground under three negative aspects. The distinction is
therefore evaluative and directional, not merely sequential.

**Why the lower ground is coarse, wretched, and wall-like.** Vasubandhu
supplies a reason for each of the three negative aspects: the lower
ground is coarse because it is not peaceful and demands greater
formative effort; wretched because it is not excellent and is
comparatively more defective; and wall-like because, from within that
very ground, there is no escape from it, exactly as a wall blocks a
way out. He then notes that the higher ground's three positive aspects
are simply the reversal of these three reasons, rather than requiring
separate argument.

**A closed digression.** The passage ends by naming itself as
`ānuṣaṅgika`, incidental — a self-marked boundary rather than an
open transition, distinguishing this unit cleanly from what follows.

## Organon Response — Directional Cognition of Ground

For the Organon, this passage shows that the Path's counteractive
cognition is not a neutral registration of its object but a
directional determination: the same act that abandons the lower
ground also cognizes it as coarse, defective, and closed, while the
ground toward which the Path moves is cognized as peaceful, excellent,
and open.

```text
uninterrupted-path
    -> lower ground: coarse / wretched / wall-like (negative aspects)

liberation-path
    -> higher ground: peaceful / excellent / exit (reversed aspects)
```

This is a bounded reading of the Bhāṣya's own reasoning by reversal
(`viparyayeṇa`). It does not extend the three-and-three aspect scheme
beyond what Vasubandhu here assigns to mundane cultivation, nor does it
claim this passage settles the supramundane case already fixed in 6.48.
