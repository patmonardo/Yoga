# VAK_4.34

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

```sanskrit
मृषावादप्रसङ्गाच्च सर्वशिक्षाव्यतिक्रमे ।
प्रतिक्षेपणसावद्यान्मद्यादेवान्यगुप्तये ॥ ४.३४ ॥
```

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

```iast
mṛṣāvādaprasaṅgāc ca sarvaśikṣāvyatikrame /
pratikṣepaṇasāvadyān madyād evānyaguptaye // 4.34 //
```

Padapāṭha-style separation:

```iast
mṛṣā-vāda-prasaṅgāt ca sarva-śikṣā-vyatikrame /
pratikṣepaṇa-sāvadyāt madyāt eva anya-guptaye //
```

The two halves answer different questions. The first explains why false
speech is selected among verbal faults for the lay restraint. The second
explains why intoxicants are selected among acts regarded by the
Ābhidharmikas as blameworthy because prohibited.

## 3. Padaccheda and Lexical Analysis

```text
mṛṣā-vāda-prasaṅgāt
ca
sarva-śikṣā-vyatikrame

pratikṣepaṇa-sāvadyāt
madyāt
eva
anya-guptaye
```

| Form | Morphology | Lexical value here |
|---|---|---|
| `mṛṣāvādaprasaṅgāt` | ablative singular compound | because an occasion or liability for false speech arises |
| `ca` | conjunction | and; additionally |
| `sarvaśikṣāvyatikrame` | locative singular compound | upon transgression of any training rule |
| `pratikṣepaṇasāvadyāt` | ablative singular compound | among or on account of what is blameworthy by prohibition |
| `madyāt` | ablative singular | from intoxicants; abstention from intoxicants |
| `eva` | restrictive particle | specifically; alone |
| `anyaguptaye` | dative singular compound | for guarding or protecting the others |

**`mṛṣāvādaprasaṅga`**

`Prasaṅga` here is an occasion, liability, or likely consequence. The Bhāṣya
describes the sequence:

```text
transgression of a training rule
    → questioning or accountability
    → thought: “I did not do that”
    → occasion for false speech
```

False speech is therefore related structurally to every other training-rule
violation.

**`sarvaśikṣāvyatikrama`**

`Sarva` is distributive in the Bhāṣya’s explanation: whenever any training
rule is transgressed, questioning may provoke denial. The phrase does not
mean only a single catastrophic repudiation of the entire discipline.

**`pratikṣepaṇasāvadya`**

This is a technical classification:

```text
pratikṣepaṇa-sāvadya
    blameworthy because explicitly prohibited

prakṛti-sāvadya
    naturally or intrinsically blameworthy
```

It should not be translated as “repudiation is blameworthy.” The Bhāṣya’s
question is why one of the five lay rules concerns an act classified as
blameworthy through prohibition rather than another naturally blameworthy
act.

**`madyāt eva`**

The governing idea of abstention is supplied from the surrounding discussion:
among such prohibited faults, abstention from intoxicants specifically is
established as a training rule.

**`anyagupti`**

`Anya` refers to the other limbs or training rules. `Gupti` is guarding or
protection. A person who becomes intoxicated may leave the remaining limbs
unguarded.

## 4. Grammar

**Additional reason for selecting false speech**

```text
sarva-śikṣā-vyatikrame
mṛṣā-vāda-prasaṅgāt ca
```

> And because an occasion for false speech arises upon transgression of any
> training rule.

The conjunction `ca` adds this reason to the three already used in the
discussion of VAK 4.33:

```text
exceptional blameworthiness
practicability
acquisition through fixed non-performance
```

The Bhāṣya supplies the omitted question: why is abstention from false speech,
rather than from divisive speech and the other verbal faults, included among
the five lay rules?

**Selection of intoxicants**

```text
pratikṣepaṇa-sāvadyāt
madyāt eva
anya-guptaye
```

> Among what is blameworthy by prohibition, [abstention] specifically from
> intoxicants [is established], for guarding the others.

The ablatives are compressed around an understood abstention or rule. The
dative `anyaguptaye` states the purpose of selecting the intoxicant precept.

The argument must not be joined to the first half as though false speech were
the thing classified as blameworthy by prohibition. The Bhāṣya explicitly
changes questions before introducing intoxicants.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> And because an occasion for false speech arises upon transgression of any
> training rule. Among what is blameworthy by prohibition, [abstention]
> specifically from intoxicants [is prescribed], for guarding the others.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> Abstention from false speech is singled out among the verbal rules because
> violation of any training rule can create an occasion for lying when the
> transgressor is questioned and tempted to deny the act. Among acts regarded
> by the Ābhidharmikas as blameworthy because prohibited, abstention from
> intoxicants is specifically included in lay restraint because intoxication
> may leave all the other training limbs unguarded.

The questioning situation and the Ābhidharmika attribution are supplied by
the Bhāṣya. The latter attribution matters because the Vinayadharas dispute
the classification of intoxicants.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> The lay discipline contains rules that protect its own continued integrity.
> Truthfulness preserves accountable acknowledgement when another rule has
> already been breached; abstention from intoxicants preserves the
> recollection and heedfulness through which the other restraints remain
> practically guardable. The five rules therefore do not form a flat list:
> some directly prohibit harmful conduct, while others preserve the
> conditions under which discipline can recognize and sustain itself.

This systemic reading follows the functions stated by the Bhāṣya. It does not
erase the technical disagreement over whether intoxicant use is naturally
blameworthy or blameworthy through prohibition.

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**Why false speech is selected**

False speech is not included merely because it is one serious verbal fault
among several. It has a recurrent relation to every precept:

```text
breach
    → called to account
    → temptation to conceal
    → second breach through false speech
```

The false-speech restraint protects the possibility of truthful recognition
after practical failure.

**`samanuyujyamāna`**

The Bhāṣya describes the transgressor as being questioned, examined, or
called to account. This interpersonal moment mediates between the first
transgression and the temptation to deny it.

**Naturally blameworthy**

On the Ābhidharmika account:

```text
prakṛtisāvadya action
    is performed only with a defiled mind
```

Killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and false speech belong to this direct
ethical class in the five lay rules.

**Blameworthy because prohibited**

An act may become disciplinary fault through explicit prohibition even when
not every possible instance has a defiled motivation. The Ābhidharmikas place
intoxicant use in this class.

This is not the claim that prohibition arbitrarily manufactures evil. The
prohibition responds to intoxication as a basis of heedlessness that threatens
the remaining restraints.

**Medicinal counterexample**

The Ābhidharmika argument allows a non-intoxicating medicinal quantity taken
with an intention of remedy (`pratīkārabuddhi`):

```text
knows the amount will intoxicate
    + drinks it
    → defiled intention

knows the medicinal amount will not intoxicate
    + drinks it as remedy
    → not necessarily defiled
```

This counterexample supports their denial that drinking is naturally
blameworthy in every instance.

**Vinayadhara position**

The Vinayadharas maintain instead:

```text
madyaṃ prakṛtisāvadyam
    intoxicants are naturally blameworthy
```

They appeal to disciplinary evidence, stringent prohibition, and the conduct
of noble persons. The Bhāṣya records a genuine technical disagreement rather
than a single unanimous classification.

**`pramādasthāna`**

A `pramādasthāna` is a basis or occasion of heedlessness. Intoxication
destroys or weakens recollection and thereby makes the other rules difficult
to guard. This is the practical center of the Ābhidharmika explanation.

**Relation to VAK 4.29**

VAK 4.29 classified the intoxicant rule as the single `apramādāṅga`, the limb
of non-heedlessness. VAK 4.34 gives its causal function:

```text
intoxicant restraint
    → guards against heedlessness
    → protects the other limbs
```

## 8. Logical Determination

**Two questions, two answers**

```text
Question 1:
why false speech rather than other verbal faults?

Answer:
every precept violation can occasion false denial

Question 2:
why include intoxicants among the five rules?

Answer:
the intoxicant rule protects all the other limbs
```

Collapsing these questions destroys the grammar and the doctrinal argument.

**Two protective relations**

```text
false-speech restraint
    protects truthful accountability after breach

intoxicant restraint
    protects the capacity to avoid further breach
```

The first operates when another rule has already failed. The second preserves
the conditions under which the other rules remain operative.

**Flat enumeration corrected**

```text
direct restraint
    prohibits a harmful act

protective restraint
    preserves the integrity or operability
    of the restraint-system
```

The five rules are one practical whole with differentiated functions, not
five externally accumulated prohibitions.

**School disagreement preserved**

```text
Vinayadhara:
    intoxicants are naturally blameworthy

Ābhidharmika:
    intoxicants are blameworthy through prohibition
    because intoxication grounds heedlessness
```

Both oppose intoxication in practice. Their disagreement concerns the ground
and technical classification of the fault.

**Non-intoxication is the discriminating condition**

The medicinal counterexample does not make quantity alone the entire moral
principle. Its logical function is narrower: it shows a conceivable instance
of drinking that lacks both intoxication and a defiled intention, thereby
challenging an unrestricted natural-fault classification.

## 9. Interpretive Note

**Discipline protects its own conditions**

VAK 4.34 reveals a reflexive structure within practical discipline:

```text
rules govern actions

some rules also preserve
the agent’s capacity to remain rule-governed
```

The intoxicant restraint is therefore not merely another object-level ban.
It preserves recollection and non-heedlessness, the subjective conditions
through which the remaining restraints can continue to determine conduct.

**Truth after failure**

The false-speech argument is equally powerful. Discipline must contain a way
to remain truthful about its own failures:

```text
first breach
    does not logically require a second breach

truthful acknowledgement
    prevents concealment from reproducing the failure
```

False denial transforms an act into a corruption of the practical relation
to the rule itself. The truthfulness precept protects that relation.

**Second-order practical determination**

The functional hierarchy can be stated without leaving the Bhāṣya:

```text
first-order restraint
    do not perform the prohibited act

second-order protection
    preserve the recollection, heedfulness,
    and truthfulness through which restraint operates
```

This supports the earlier analysis of the eight upavāsa limbs as an
articulated whole. A limb may belong to the same vow while performing a
different logical function within it.

**No moralistic flattening**

The Vinayadhara–Ābhidharmika dispute must remain visible. The point is not to
soften the prohibition by calling intoxicants merely conventional, nor to
erase the medicinal counterexample by declaring every material instance
intrinsically identical. Vasubandhu preserves the problem of determining why
the act is faulty and what the rule is designed to protect.

This is the Organon contact: the practical whole preserves both its outward
determinations and the inward conditions of their continued actuality. The
school positions and concrete causal sequence remain the controlling textual
layer.
