# VAK_4.02 Bhāṣya — Manifest Action, Configuration, and Momentariness

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> te tu vijñaptyavijñaptī kāyavijñaptir iṣyate /
> saṃsthānaṃ na gatir yasmāt saṃskṛtaṃ kṣaṇikaṃ vyayāt // 4.02 //

> Those two actions each have manifestation and non-manifestation. Bodily
> manifestation is held to be configuration, not motion, because the
> conditioned is momentary owing to its perishing.

`Te` resumes the bodily and verbal actions generated by volition in 4.01.
The Bhāṣya makes `pratyekam`, **each**, explicit: both actions have a
`vijñapti` and an `avijñapti` mode. The existing Kārikā study is
`VAK_4.02.md`.

the visibly suspect readings `anantravināśī`, `ṣkṇikatvaṃ`, `vanāśaḥ`,

second witness. The initial `sarva` is likewise displayed as `sarvaṃ`

The local witness reads `daṇḍakavat`; a scholarly transcription of the

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“Those two have manifestation and non-manifestation.” The bodily and verbal
actions are each to be understood as having the natures of manifestation
and non-manifestation. Among them, bodily manifestation is held to be
configuration. Bodily manifestation is the body configured in one way or
another under the power of mind.

Others say that it is motion: bodily action belongs to the moving body,
they argue, not to one that is not moving. They are answered: it is not
motion, because the conditioned is momentary. What is a moment? A coming
into existence characterized by destruction immediately afterward.
What possesses such a moment is called momentary, as one who possesses a
staff is called a staff-bearer. For whatever is
conditioned does not remain after it comes into existence: it perishes
precisely where it arises. Transfer of that same entity to another place
is therefore untenable. Bodily action is consequently not motion.

Someone may say that this follows only if the momentariness of everything
has been established. Know that it has been established. Why? Because
what is conditioned necessarily perishes.

The destruction of things occurs without a separate destroying cause.
Why? A cause is a cause of a product, while destruction is an absence.
What is there to produce in an absence? If that causeless destruction did
not occur as soon as a thing arose, it would not occur later either, since
the thing would be the same. Nor can one simply say that the very same
thing has become different: the same thing cannot also be distinct from
itself.

An opponent replies: we see wood and the like destroyed through contact
with fire, and nothing is a stronger warrant than perception. Destruction
is therefore not always causeless. The answer asks what is actually seen.
One sees the wood no longer. But it still needs examination whether
contact with fire destroyed the wood, or whether the previous wood ceased
of itself and a further one did not arise. The lamp after contact with
wind, and the bell's sound after contact with a hand, pose the same
question. The matter must therefore be established by inference. What
inference? It has already been said that absence is not a product.

The translation of the damaged sentences at [193|08–10] is provisional.
The staff-bearer analogy follows the attested `daṇḍikavat`, while the local
witness retains `daṇḍakavat`. The wood, lamp, and bell examples
make a logical point about what non-perception establishes; they do not
claim that fire, wind, or a hand make no causal difference to a series.

## 4. Argument and Voices

The Bhāṣya first distributes `vijñapti` and `avijñapti` to **each** of
bodily and verbal action. It then defines bodily `vijñapti` as the body's
determinate configuration under the power of mind. `Gatir ity apare`
introduces the rival identification of bodily action with motion.

The reply denies transference of one enduring conditioned entity between
places. An objector challenges the premise that all conditioned things
are momentary. The answer turns to perishing and argues that destruction,
as absence, is not a separately produced positive event. The appeal to
wood burned by fire is another objection. The reply distinguishes what
is perceived—subsequent non-appearance—from the competing causal accounts
of it, which require inference.

The final sentence states the first inferential ground: absence is not a
product. The `punaḥ` that follows starts a further reason in 4.03; the
proof of momentariness continues there.

## 5. The Two Modes of Bodily and Verbal Action

The opening is a fourfold distribution, not a pairing of bodily action
with `vijñapti` and verbal action with `avijñapti`:

```text
bodily action:  vijñapti + avijñapti
verbal action:  vijñapti + avijñapti
```

`Vijñapti` here is action's manifest or making-known aspect; it is
distinct from `vijñāna`, cognition. `Avijñapti` is named but not yet
positively defined in this unit. Its presence as a mode of action must not
be turned into an explanation of karmic continuity before the argument
supplies one.

## 6. Configuration, Motion, and the Moment

`Citta-vaśena` is decisive for the bodily case. `Saṃsthāna` is not merely
any static shape: it is the body configured in a determinate way under
mental direction. The rival's appeal to a moving body presses the ordinary
appearance of action. The reply challenges the account of motion that
requires one numerically identical conditioned thing to cross from one
place to another.

The Bhāṣya defines a `kṣaṇa` through coming into being followed by
immediate perishing, then calls what possesses such a moment `kṣaṇika`.
If the entity ceases where it arises, its own
`deśāntara-saṃkrānti`, transference to another place, cannot occur.
This does not yet explain the appearance of locomotion; the subsequent
argument must account for succession without identity across places.

## 7. Perishing and the Fire Objection

The inference begins from a distinction between a produced thing and
its absence. A cause produces an effect. Destruction understood as
`abhāva` is no positive effect requiring a further producer. The
opponent's wood-and-fire example tests that distinction against ordinary
perception. Vasubandhu's reply limits what perception has decided:
the wood is no longer seen, but its non-appearance alone cannot decide
whether fire destroyed a persisting wood or prevented a similar successor
from arising after a moment ceased.

The argument does not eliminate conditions. It changes what they must
explain: the arising and continuation of a conditioned series rather than
the manufacture of an absence. Further reasons and counterarguments
belong to 4.03.

## 8. Translation and Doctrinal Limits

“Configuration” keeps `saṃsthāna` distinct from `gati`, motion. “Under
the power of mind” translates the Bhāṣya's `citta-vaśena` and should not
be inserted silently into a literal translation of the verse.
“Causeless destruction” is a compact rendering of `akasmika-vināśa` in
this argument: the claim concerns a separate destroying cause, not the
absence of all conditioned relations.

The present witness is visibly corrupt at several points. The
`daṇḍakavat`/`daṇḍikavat` difference has an independent comparison, but
the other rough readings still require checking against further witnesses.
The study does not establish
a critical Sanskrit text or settle the whole `vijñapti`–`avijñapti`
theory from its opening statement.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

**Textual determination.** The passage asks how volition-produced bodily
action becomes manifest. Its answer is a mind-directed bodily
configuration, then an argument against treating action as a thing that
endures and travels. The question of `avijñapti` remains present but open.

**Organon reconstruction.** In the Sun-lit stance, the empirical appearance
of movement is precisely what must be examined, not washed out by a
general rational certainty. The rival's observation has force. The
Bhāṣya's response asks what that observation licenses: perception of
change and non-appearance does not by itself establish the persistence
and destruction of an identical entity. This is a fruitful point of
comparison with transcendental questions about the conditions of
experience, while the momentariness thesis and the karma taxonomy remain
Vasubandhu's own argumentative commitments.
