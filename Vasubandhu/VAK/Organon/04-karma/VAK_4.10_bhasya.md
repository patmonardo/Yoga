# VAK_4.10 Bhāṣya — Initiating and Accompanying Action

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> samutthānaṃ dvidhā hetutatkṣaṇotthānasaṃjñitam /
> pravartakaṃ tayor ādyaṃ dvitīyam anuvartakam // 4.10 //

> The originating condition is twofold: one called causal origination,
> the other origination in that very moment. Of these, the first
> initiates action; the second accompanies its execution.

The Kārikā study is `VAK_4.10.md`. The distinction responds to
questions left by the account of action's ethical quality in 4.09.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

This is to be examined. If bodily and verbal karma are wholesome or
unwholesome by reason of their originating condition, why are the
great elements not classified in the same way? Because the agent's
intention is directed toward the action, not toward the great elements.
But there is no intention directed toward the non-manifest action of
one in concentration, and a non-concentrated mind cannot originate it,
since it belongs to a different level. How, then, is it wholesome?
And would the divine eye and ear not also have to be wholesome? This
point requires effort to resolve.

It was said that a mind abandoned through seeing does not originate
manifest action. Why, then, did the Blessed One say that wrong view
gives rise to wrong resolve, wrong speech, wrong bodily action, and
the like? There is no contradiction.

“Origination is twofold, called causal origination and origination in
that very moment.” There are two kinds of originating condition:
one is a cause, the other is present in the very moment of the action.
“Of those two, the first initiates; the second accompanies.” Causal
origination initiates because it projects the action. Contemporaneous
origination accompanies because it continues with the action while
the action is being performed.

What capacity does this accompanying condition have in the action?
Without it, the action, though already projected, would not occur,
as in the case of a corpse. But how, then, does restraint arise for
someone without mind? The arising of restraint is more evident in
someone with mind; that is the capacity at issue.

The text itself leaves the first cluster of questions with `karttavyo
'tra yatnaḥ`, “effort is needed here.” Its subsequent twofold
distinction directly resolves the scriptural-sequence question; it
should not be made to settle every problem about concentration-born
`avijñapti` without further argument.

## 4. Questions and Voices

The first exchange challenges the rule that action inherits ethical
quality from its originating condition. The answer invokes the agent's
intention, then encounters cases where that answer is not enough:
concentration-born non-manifest action and the divine faculties. The
commentary acknowledges the need for further work.

The next objection contrasts an Abhidharma restriction on the mind
immediately producing manifestation with a scriptural sequence from
wrong view to wrong action. Vasubandhu distinguishes two senses of
`samutthāna`. A final interlocutor asks what the contemporaneous
condition contributes and raises restraint arising without mind.
No separate school affiliation is specified for these brief
questions.

## 5. Two Functions of Samutthāna

The Bhāṣya supplies two causal functions:

```text
hetusamutthāna       → pravartaka  → projects and initiates
tatkṣaṇasamutthāna   → anuvartaka  → accompanies performance
```

The distinction lets wrong view function as a remote causal initiator
without being the mind present at the moment of bodily or verbal
manifestation. `Ākṣepakatva` names the first condition's projecting
capacity; `kriyākālānuvartana` names the second's co-occurrence with
action while it is performed.

The two are causal roles, not two independent substances. Nor does
“accompanying” mean mere later persistence: it is specifically the
condition present during execution.

## 6. The Corpse and Mindless Restraint

The corpse comparison tests whether prior projection alone suffices
for manifest performance. It does not: an accompanying condition
matters. The question about restraint arising without mind
then prevents treating such accompaniment as universally required
in the same way for every kind of karma. The reply says that
the arising of restraint is more evident when mind is present. It
does not say that the restraint of someone without mind is impossible.

The compressed exchange should be read as a local qualification,
not a completed theory of all mindless states. The feminine
`sphuṭatarā` most naturally resumes `saṃvarotpatti` (the arising of
restraint), rather than supplying a new predicate for action in
general. It belongs alongside the earlier distinction between
manifest and non-manifest action.

## 7. Ethical Origin and Material Support

The opening question asks why ethical quality passes from a producer
to action but not automatically to the great elements supporting it.
The initial answer appeals to the object of the agent's intention.
The countercases show the limitation of that answer. This matters
for 4.09's four grounds of predication: `samutthāna` is a specified
relation, not a rule that every product of a wholesome event is
wholesome in exactly the same sense.

## 8. Translation and Doctrinal Limits

“Initiates” renders `pravartaka`; “accompanies” renders `anuvartaka`.
The latter can sustain action in its execution, but the Bhāṣya's
explicit gloss is `kriyākālānuvartana`, accompanying it during the
time of performance. “Projects” renders `ākṣip`, preserving its
technical causal force.

The witness's `kukśalatvaprasaṅgaḥ` is evidently rough, but the
question of the divine eye and ear inheriting wholesomeness is clear.
The passage does not establish a final answer to that question or to
the full ethical status of concentration-born `avijñapti`. In the
last exchange, “more evident” renders `sphuṭatarā` without adding
a claim that the action is more forceful.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

**Textual determination.** An action can have a condition that sets it
going and another that accompanies its performance. The distinction
allows the cited sūtra's causal sequence to coexist with the stricter
claim about which mind is present during outward manifestation.

**Organon reconstruction.** LOKA:KARMA gains a precise distinction
between initiation and execution. It is tempting to call both simply
“mediation,” but their functions differ. A rational model of action
must preserve the difference and remain answerable to cases such as
mindless restraint. This comparison is our reconstruction; the source
speaks through `hetusamutthāna`, `tatkṣaṇasamutthāna`, and their roles.
