## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Why is abstention from false speech, rather than from divisive
speech and the other verbal faults, a training rule of lay
restraint? For the same three reasons just given: false speech is
especially blameworthy, abstention is practicable, and the
restraint of non-performance is acquired. There is also “the
occasion for false speech when any training rule is transgressed.”
Whenever one is questioned about a breach, the response “I did
not take [or do] such a thing” may present itself. Thus a breach of any rule may
occasion false speech, and abstention from it is prescribed so
that a person who has transgressed does not add a false denial
[the source's final clause is compressed].

Why, then, has no training rule been established for something
blameworthy by prohibition? One has: “For intoxicants specifically.” Why intoxicants
and not something else? “To guard the others.” For the person who
drinks intoxicants may leave the other limbs of restraint unguarded.
How is drinking classified as blameworthy by prohibition? It lacks
the mark of what is naturally blameworthy: a naturally blameworthy
act is performed only with a defiled mind. But one can drink a
non-intoxicating amount with the intention of a remedy. The mind
of someone who drinks knowing the amount will intoxicate is
defiled; the mind of someone who knows the amount will not
intoxicate need not be so.

The Vinaya specialists object that intoxicating drink is naturally
blameworthy. They cite a statement about how to care for a sick
person while setting aside what is naturally blameworthy; the lack
of permission for sick Śākyans to drink; an injunction to those
who call the Buddha their teacher not to drink even an amount on
the tip of a blade of grass; the abstention of noble persons even
in other births, as with killing; its mention as bodily misconduct;
and its association with unfortunate rebirth.

The Ābhidharmikas disagree. Their reply distinguishes a general
prohibition and a possible exception for the sick [the source
sentence specifying quantities is corrupt]. The prohibition of
even a tiny amount guards against the occasion for intoxication.
Noble persons abstain out of moral scruple and because drink
destroys recollection. Avoiding even a small amount reflects the
uncertainty of its effect, as with poison. It is called misconduct
because it is a basis of heedlessness. This is why “basis of
heedlessness” is specified for this precept, whereas the other
faults are naturally blameworthy. Unfortunate rebirth is said to
follow excessive drinking because it repeatedly sets an unwholesome
continuum in motion, induces karma leading to a bad destination,
or sustains such a livelihood [the final alternative is uncertain].

What does the rule's expression “surā, maireya, intoxicant, basis
of heedlessness” mean? `Surā` is described as an unfermented
drink, `maireya` as a fermented liquid. Both can occur in states
where their intoxicating character is not straightforward [the
compound is difficult], hence the separate word “intoxicant.”
Areca fruit, kodrava, and other things can also intoxicate, hence
the wording mentioning `surā` and `maireya` [the exact force of
this explanation remains uncertain]. “Basis of heedlessness”
explains why even an act classified as blameworthy by prescription
must be abandoned seriously: it supplies an occasion for every
kind of heedlessness.

## 4. Two Protective Functions

The Kārikā's halves answer two different questions. The
false-speech rule protects the truthfulness with which any other
breach can be acknowledged; it has a role when restraint has
failed. The intoxicant rule guards the conditions under which the
other limbs can continue to operate. The second is the
`apramādāṅga` discussed in 4.29.

The contrast between `prakṛtisāvadya` and
`pratikṣepaṇasāvadya` is a technical classification of faults.
The medicinal, non-intoxicating case supports the Ābhidharmika
classification; the Vinayadharas contest it with disciplinary
evidence and the conduct of noble persons. The Bhāṣya records a
real disagreement, not a unanimous doctrine about every possible
use of intoxicants.

The closing explanation of the precept's full wording matters.
Its `pramādasthāna` identifies the specific threat: drink can
damage recollection and open the way to further breaches. The
source's damaged formulation of beverage classes remains a
philological question, but the function of the final term is
stated plainly at [219|09–10].

## 5. Organon Contact Point

Our practical wheel requires both the cessation of adhyāsa and a
capacity to let the Idea of the True and Good govern what follows.
This Kośa passage offers two concrete tests of that capacity:
truthfully acknowledge a failure, and preserve the recollection
needed to keep the vow operative. Here false speech and
intoxicants are not simply two more items in a list; each can
undermine the practical system that contains it. This is an
Organon reading of their relation, while the Bhāṣya's own terms
remain training rules, their faults, and heedlessness.
