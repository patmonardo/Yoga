# VAK 4.66 Bhāṣya — Ten Courses Selected from Conduct

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
viparyayāt sucaritaṃ tadaudārikasaṃgrahāt /
daśa karmapathā uktā yathāyogaṃ śubhāśubhāḥ // 4.66 //
```

> Good conduct is the converse [of bad conduct]. By selecting the
> salient forms of those conducts, ten courses of karma are taught,
> wholesome or unwholesome as applicable.

The ten are a selection from a wider ethical field, not an
exhaustive list of everything good or bad one can do.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“Good conduct is the converse.” It comprises desirable, wholesome
bodily, verbal, and mental karma, together with non-covetousness,
non-hostility, and right view. How can right and wrong view be
wholesome and unwholesome when neither contains an intention to
benefit or harm another? Because they are roots of such conduct.
Of the good and bad conducts just described, “by selecting their
salient forms, ten courses of karma are taught, wholesome or
unwholesome as applicable.” The sūtra states ten courses by taking
salient forms of these very conducts: wholesome ones from good
conduct and unwholesome ones from bad conduct.

What bad conduct, then, is not included, and what good conduct is
not included? Among the unwholesome courses, part of bodily bad
conduct is left out: conduct in preparatory or subsequent phases,
and other afflicted conduct such as binding someone. These are
not sufficiently salient. Bodily bad conduct that deprives others
of life, possessions, or spouses is stated as a course of karma
in distinction from those other cases. Similarly, the verbal bad
conduct described in the witness as `yadeva praśādaṃ` is stated
as a course for the same purpose; the phrase's exact sense here
is uncertain. A part of mental bad conduct, namely volition, is
also left out.

Among wholesome conducts as well, parts of bodily good conduct
are outside the ten: preparatory and subsequent phases, abstention
from liquor and similar acts, giving, and offering or worship.
Pleasant speech and similar verbal good conduct are outside them,
as is volition among forms of mental good conduct.

## 4. What the Ten Select

The Bhāṣya moves through two ranges. `Sucarita` and `duścarita`
name wider fields of good and bad conduct. The sūtra's ten
`karmapatha` collect their more salient forms (`audārika`).
Consequently, conduct can be ethically qualified without itself
being one of the ten courses. Preparatory and subsequent phases,
binding someone, abstention from liquor, giving, pleasant speech,
and mental volition illustrate the excluded portions.

The bodily examples show why “salient” involves the determined
object and result of an act, not merely how visible it is. Taking
life, possessions, or a spouse is given as the set that the bodily
courses mark off. The verbal example is textually uncertain, so
it should not be used to derive a more exact selection rule until
another witness has been checked.

Right and wrong view raise a different question. Their ethical
quality does not require a present intention to benefit or harm
someone. The concise answer, `tanmūlatvāt`, assigns them the role
of **roots** from which such conduct can arise. This preserves
the distinction in 4.65 between a mental condition and karma
strictly understood as volition.

## 5. Organon Contact Point

For our Rational Moral Agent, this supplies a disciplined way to
form a practical classification: begin with the full range of
conduct, identify the especially consequential courses, and keep
the unselected cases available for judgment. A right or wrong
view can matter as a root of later action even when it is not
itself a completed outward act. These are project uses of the
Kośa's distinctions; the ten-course selection remains its own
sūtra-based teaching.
