# VAK 4.77 Bhāṣya — Residual Discourse and Covetousness

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
ato 'nyat kliṣṭamityanye lapanāgītanāṭyavat /
kuśāstravaccābhidhyā tu parasvaviṣamaspṛhā // 4.77 //
```

> Others say that afflicted speech other than these is disordered
> discourse, as with flattering talk, song, drama, and corrupt treatises.
> Covetousness, however, is wrongful longing for another's possession.

The first half records an alternative, residual definition of the fourth
verbal course. The second turns to the first mental course and identifies
its practical content: the wish to make another's possession one's own.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“Others say that afflicted speech other than these is disordered
discourse.” According to this position, afflicted speech other than false
speech and the other specified verbal actions is `saṃbhinnapralāpa`.
Examples are flattering talk, song, drama, and corrupt treatises.

For example, a monk pursuing wrong livelihood engages in ingratiating talk;
some people under passion sing; and actors speak for the entertainment of
others. Proponents of bad treatises also expound their views. Lamentation,
social chatter, and other afflicted verbal action distinct from false,
divisive, and harsh speech likewise belong to disordered discourse.

How can song exist in the age of a wheel-turning monarch without being
disordered discourse? Songs then are connected with renunciation, not with
sensual or vulgar savor. Others say that talk concerning marriage and
similar matters still exists then, so disordered discourse exists, though
it does not become a course of karma.

“Covetousness, however, is wrongful longing for another's possession.” The
wish, “Ah, may what belongs to others become mine,” is an improper and
unjust longing toward another's possession, a wish to make it one's own
whether by force or by some device. This is covetousness as a course of
karma.

Others call all desire-realm craving covetousness. Thus, in the teaching on
the hindrances and with reference to sensual desire, the sūtra says, “Having
abandoned covetousness concerning the world,” and so forth. Another view
answers that although all such craving may be called covetousness, not all
of it is a karmapatha, because the courses select the gross forms of bad
conduct. Otherwise wheel-turning monarchs and inhabitants of Northern Kuru
would also possess covetousness as a course of karma.

## 4. Broad and Residual Taxonomies

```text
BROAD ACCOUNT — VAK 4.76
    every afflicted statement
        → saṃbhinnapralāpa

RESIDUAL ACCOUNT — VAK 4.77
    afflicted statement
    minus false speech
    minus divisive speech
    minus harsh speech
        → saṃbhinnapralāpa
```

The broad account permits overlap; the residual account creates an exclusive
fourth class. `Ityanye`, “so say others,” marks the latter as an attributed
alternative.

The examples establish that disordered discourse need not be grammatically
or inferentially shapeless. Flattery, performance, and a corrupt treatise
can possess polished organization. Their defect lies in afflicted genesis
and orientation.

The wheel-turning monarch example separates expressive Form from governing
Power: song governed by sensual-afflictive savor is afflicted discourse;
song governed by renunciation is not afflicted in that manner. Afflicted
verbal conduct may also exist without acquiring karmapatha degree.

## 5. Covetousness as a Mental Course

`Abhidhyā` has determinate objective and practical content:

```text
actual relation
    possession belongs to another

projected relation
    may that possession become mine

means contemplated
    force or stratagem

governing determination
    wrongful appropriation
```

The bodily appropriation has not occurred, yet the mental course is already
practical. It projects a transformation of the objective relation and adopts
that transformation as its end.

```text
lobha
    general unwholesome root
        ↓
abhidhyā
    mental course of projected appropriation
        ↓
adattādāna
    bodily course of completed appropriation
```

This sequence is genetic rather than necessary. Covetousness need not
proceed to theft, and theft remains a separately constituted course.

## 6. Selection by Practical Degree

The dispute returns to the `audārika` principle of VAK 4.66:

```text
broad abhidhyā
    desire-realm craving

abhidhyā as karmapatha
    gross and practically decisive wrongful longing
```

A term may have a broad scriptural extension while the ten-course
classification selects only the form powerful enough to govern a practical
configuration. This is the qualitative degree of a Course of Action.

## 7. Organon Contact Point

VAK 4.77 gives two rules for agent design:

```text
formal coherence
    does not establish valid governing orientation

mental occurrence
    does not automatically reach Course-of-Action degree
```

A fluent systematic artifact can function like `kuśāstra` when its ground,
provenance, inference, and practical end are corrupt. Conversely, ordinary
desire should not be promoted into the decisive class of covetousness.

The mental course can be represented as a projected transition:

```text
<Current Relation,
 Projected Relation,
 Authorization,
 Intended Means,
 Governing Root,
 Practical Degree>
```

This is the Organon reconstruction. The speech taxonomy, examples, competing
extensions of `abhidhyā`, and gross-conduct selection belong to the Bhāṣya.
