# VAK 4.104 Bhāṣya — Intention, Preparation, and the Path

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
buddhe na tāḍanecchasya prahārān nordhvam arhati /
nānantaryaprayuktasya vairāgyaphalasaṃbhavaḥ // 4.104 //
```

> In the Buddha's case, the person who intends only to strike does not
> incur the immediate offense. Nor does a blow against a person who
> becomes an arhat only afterward acquire that status. For someone
> engaged in preparing an immediate-result action, dispassion or a Path
> fruit cannot arise while that preparation remains in force.

The first two cases continue the Bhāṣya's discussion of exceptional
victims in 4.103. The last line introduces a distinct question about
preparation and attainment.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Does causing a Tathāgata's blood to flow with a hostile mind necessarily
constitute an immediate-result action? It does for someone intending to
kill. It does not for someone intending only to strike.

If someone strikes a person who is not yet an arhat, and that person
becomes an arhat after the blow, does the blow then count as an
immediate-result action? It does not. The assailant did not undertake
the action against an arhat.

If someone has begun preparing an immediate-result action and has not
turned away from that preparation, can dispassion or a Path fruit arise?
For one so engaged, neither can arise. By contrast, if someone has
prepared another course of action and the noble Path arises, that course
cannot then come into being: the agent's transformed support is entirely
opposed to it.

## 4. Two Limits on Retrospective Classification

The first two cases keep the intended action, the victim's status, and
the resulting classification distinct. Intending merely to strike the
Buddha does not meet the Bhāṣya's condition of intent to kill in its
discussion of hostile bloodshed. Likewise, the victim's later attainment
of arhatship cannot turn an earlier blow into an action against an arhat.
The relevant preparation was not directed at an arhat then.

The first-pass kārikā study reads the opening as a general claim that
an assailant cannot kill a Buddha. Whatever may be said elsewhere about
that question, the local Bhāṣya explains this line by contrasting
`vadhābhiprāya` (intention to kill) with `tāḍanābhiprāya` (intention to
strike). That contrast governs the present translation.

## 5. Preparation and Attainment

`Prayoga` is preparation or active undertaking. The question explicitly
adds `tasminn avyāvartite`: the undertaking has **not been abandoned**.
The obstruction to dispassion and Path fruit belongs to this continuing
engagement. The passage does not establish that someone who once began
such an action can never turn away or attain a fruit.

The closing contrast runs in the other causal direction. For an ordinary
course of action, the arising of the noble Path changes the `āśraya`, the
supporting continuum, so that the prepared course cannot subsequently
complete. Thus the Bhāṣya considers both an undertaking that obstructs
attainment and an attainment that precludes an undertaking's completion.

## 6. Organon Contact Point — Project Reconstruction

For the Organon's account of agency, this is a precise constraint on a
course rather than a moral label attached to an isolated event:

```text
intended object + preparation + present status of the field
    → classification of the act

unabandoned immediate-result preparation
    → excludes attainment while it remains active

noble Path arising in the supporting continuum
    → excludes completion of a contrary ordinary course
```

This is our reconstruction of the causal pattern. The Bhāṣya itself
speaks in terms of `prayoga`, `āśraya`, `vairāgya`, and `phala`; it does
not formulate an Organon theory of agents.
