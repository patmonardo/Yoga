# VAK 4.45 Bhāṣya — Karma as Security, Merit, and Felt Result

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
kṣemākṣemetarat karma kuśalākuśaletarat /
puṇyāpuṇyam aniñjaṃ ca sukhavedyādi ca trayam // 4.45 //
```

> Karma is secure, insecure, or neither: wholesome, unwholesome,
> or indeterminate respectively. It is also meritorious,
> demeritorious, or immovable, and is threefold according to
> whether its result is experienced as pleasure, pain, or neither.

At [227|04] in `Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt`, Vasubandhu
explicitly begins explaining the karmas named in the sūtras.
The complete opening unit runs through [227|12–13]. The phrase
`tatra tāvat` at [227|13] introduces the explanation of the
merit triad taken up in 4.46.

Word division, punctuation, and verse numbering are editorial.
The running source reads `aviñjaṃ` in the third pāda, while the
Kārikā witness reads `aniñjaṃ` and the explanatory prose has
`āneñjaṃ`. The prose supports the technical sense “immovable,”
but the variation is not hidden. Other compressed forms in the
source, including `kameti`, are left visible.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Now, in the course of the exposition of karma, the karmas named
in the sūtras will be explained. There are three karmas:
wholesome, unwholesome, and indeterminate. First: “Karma is
secure, insecure, or other—that is, wholesome, unwholesome, or
other.” These are the defining marks of the wholesome and the
remaining classes.

Wholesome karma is secure: its maturation is desirable, and it
leads toward the attainment of nirvāṇa, because it protects
from suffering. Unwholesome karma is insecure, opposed to
security both at the relevant time and ultimately; its
maturation is undesirable. Karma that is other than these two
is neither secure nor insecure. It is also other than wholesome
and unwholesome karma: that is, it is indeterminate
(`avyākṛta`).

Further, there are three karmas: meritorious, demeritorious,
and immovable. And there are three more: karma to be experienced
as pleasure, karma to be experienced as pain, and karma to be
experienced as neither pain nor pleasure.

## 4. The First Triad Gives Defining Marks

`Idaṃ kuśalādīnāṃ lakṣaṇam` prevents a common misreading of
the Kārikā. `Kṣema / akṣema / itarat` is not a further list
placed beside `kuśala / akuśala / avyākṛta`. The former three
characterize the latter three, in order. The relation is:

| Defining mark | Karma class | Bhāṣya explanation |
|---|---|---|
| `kṣema`, secure | `kuśala`, wholesome | Desirable maturation and the direction toward nirvāṇa; protection from suffering |
| `akṣema`, insecure | `akuśala`, unwholesome | Contrary to security; undesirable maturation |
| Neither secure nor insecure | `avyākṛta`, indeterminate | Neither wholesome nor unwholesome |

The `kṣema` determination joins a karmic result to a liberative
direction. The passage does not define wholesome karma merely
as approved conduct or as pleasant feeling in the moment of
acting. Conversely, the “other” class is specified by its
position outside the first opposition; the text does not
attribute the same result or direction to it.

## 5. Two Further Triads Announced

The latter half opens two more classificatory axes. `Puṇya`,
`apuṇya`, and `āneñja` distinguish meritorious, demeritorious,
and immovable karma. This verse enumerates them; 4.46 begins
their explanation. `Sukhavedanīya`, `duḥkhavedanīya`, and
`aduḥkhāsukhavedanīya` distinguish karma by what is to be
experienced as its result, not simply by the feeling present
while the action is performed. Their further explanation also
lies ahead.

The source therefore changes method at this point. After the
Abhidharma analysis of acquired and lost standing restraints,
it begins with sūtra-given karma classifications and asks for
their defining characteristics. That transition is stated
explicitly in the opening sentence, not merely inferred from
our chapter map.

## 6. Organon Contact Point

For our Rational Moral Agent, this opening insists that an
action's classification be tied to what it produces and where
it leads. The Idea of the True and Good cannot be reduced to
the immediate pleasantness of an act; the Kośa likewise
distinguishes desirable maturation, protection from suffering,
and eventual liberation. This is a project comparison, while
the source's own predicates are `kṣema`, `kuśala`, and their
specified opposites.
