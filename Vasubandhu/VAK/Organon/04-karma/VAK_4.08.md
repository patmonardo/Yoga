# VAK_4.08

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

> कामेऽपि निवृता नास्ति समुत्थानमसद्यतः ।
>
> परमार्थशुभो मोक्षः स्वतो मूलह्य्रपत्रपाः ॥ ४.८ ॥

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

> kāme 'pi nivṛtā nāsti samutthānam asad yataḥ /
>
> paramārthaśubho mokṣaḥ svato mūlahryapatrapāḥ // 4.8 //

VAK 4.07 established where manifest and non-manifest action can occur.

The first half of VAK 4.08 completes that inquiry. It explains why
obscured-indeterminate manifestation does not occur in the desire domain.

The second half opens a new and philosophically substantial inquiry:

```text
When a dharma is called wholesome,
by what relation does goodness belong to it?
```

The Bhāṣya answers that ethical predication operates in four ways:

```text
ultimately
intrinsically
by association
by originating condition
```

The present verse determines the first two. VAK 4.09 will determine the
remaining two.

## 3. Padaccheda and Lexical Analysis

```text

```

| Form | Morphology | Lexical force here |
|---|---|---|
| kāme | locative singular | in the desire domain |
| api | particle | even; despite the desire domain's otherwise broad range of action |
| nivṛtā | nominative feminine singular adjective | obscured; Bhāṣya: `nivṛtāvyākṛtā`, obscured-indeterminate |
| asti | third-person singular present of `√as` | exists; occurs |
| samutthānam | nominative neuter singular action noun | originating condition; activating source |
| asat | nominative neuter singular present participial adjective | nonexistent; not present |
| yataḥ | causal relative adverb | because; on account of which |
| paramārtha | compound member | ultimate sense; final or highest truth of the predicate here |
| śubhaḥ | nominative masculine singular adjective | wholesome; good |
| mokṣaḥ | nominative masculine singular | liberation; Bhāṣya: nirvāṇa |
| svataḥ | indeclinable ablatival adverb | from itself; by its own nature |
| mūla | compound/list member | roots; Bhāṣya: the three wholesome roots |
| hrī | feminine noun | ethical self-respect; moral shame before oneself |
| apatrāpāḥ | nominative plural form in the compressed list | moral concern or circumspection before others |

**Two corrections to the initial construe**

The understood subject of `nivṛtā` is `vijñapti`, not `avijñapti`.

The Bhāṣya states this explicitly:

```text
nivṛtā tu vijñaptiḥ
kāmadhātau api na asti

obscured-indeterminate manifestation
does not exist even in the desire domain
```

The word `asat` must also be corrected.

Here it does not mean “bad” or “unwholesome.” It means that the relevant
`samutthāna` is absent:

```text
samutthānam asat
    → the originating condition does not exist
```

The prose explains that there is no obscured-indeterminate desire-domain mind
of the class capable of producing this outward manifestation.

**Resolution of the final compound**

The transmitted `mūlahryapatrapāḥ` is compressed.

The Bhāṣya resolves it unambiguously as a list:

```text
trīṇi kuśalamūlāni
hrīḥ
apatrāpyaṃ ca

the three wholesome roots,
ethical self-respect,
and moral circumspection
```

**Witness cautions**

The prose witness reads `nivṛktāvyākṛtaṃ`, evidently where
`nivṛtāvyākṛtaṃ` is expected. The normalized form is used in analysis, but the
transmitted reading remains preserved in the research witness.

The exact graphic segmentation of `mūlahryapatrapāḥ` is difficult at the
kārikā level. Its doctrinal segmentation is secure because the Bhāṣya supplies
the list.

## 4. Grammar and Construe

The first statement carries `vijñaptiḥ` forward from VAK 4.07:

```text
kāme api
nivṛtā [vijñaptiḥ]
na asti

even in the desire domain,
obscured-indeterminate manifestation
does not exist
```

The causal clause follows:

```text
yataḥ
[tasyāḥ] samutthānam
asat

because
its originating condition
does not exist
```

The Bhāṣya supplies the causal structure behind this compression.

Manifest action must be activated by a mind possessing both `vitarka` and
`vicāra`:

```text
savitarka-savicāra citta
    → produces vijñapti
```

That kind of mind is absent from the second dhyāna upward.

The productive mind is also said to be abandoned through cultivation rather
than through seeing:

```text
bhāvanā-prahātavya citta
    → may activate outward manifestation

darśana-prahātavya citta
    → proceeds inwardly
    → does not activate such outward manifestation
```

In the desire domain, no obscured-indeterminate mind of the required
productive class exists. Hence no obscured-indeterminate `vijñapti` occurs
there.

The third pāda is a complete nominal predication:

```text
mokṣaḥ
    paramārtha-śubhaḥ

liberation
    is wholesome in the ultimate sense
```

The Bhāṣya identifies `mokṣa` with nirvāṇa and explains the predicate through
the complete pacification of suffering and supreme security.

The fourth pāda has an understood predicate:

```text
[kuśalāḥ santi] svataḥ
    trīṇi kuśalamūlāni
    hrīḥ
    apatrāpyaṃ ca

[wholesome] from themselves are
    the three wholesome roots,
    ethical self-respect,
    and moral circumspection
```

The prose renders `svataḥ` doctrinally as `svabhāvena`: by intrinsic nature.

## 5. Translation

### Close syntactic construe

> Even in the desire domain there is no obscured-indeterminate
> [manifestation], because its originating condition does not exist.
> Liberation is wholesome in the ultimate sense; the roots, ethical
> self-respect, and moral circumspection are [wholesome] from themselves.

The understood `vijñapti`, the interpretation of `asat`, and the full list in
the last pāda are established by the Bhāṣya.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> Obscured-indeterminate manifest action does not occur in the desire domain,
> because the kind of mind capable of originating it is absent there. Such
> manifestation is attested only in Brahmaloka: above the first dhyāna the
> thought containing both `vitarka` and `vicāra` that could activate manifest
> action no longer exists. Liberation—nirvāṇa—is wholesome in the ultimate
> sense because it is the complete pacification of suffering and supreme
> security. The three wholesome roots, ethical self-respect, and moral
> circumspection are wholesome by intrinsic nature because their goodness does
> not depend upon association with, or production by, something else
> wholesome.

The Brahmaloka distribution, the `vitarka-vicāra` explanation, the distinction
between what is abandoned by cultivation and by seeing, the identification of
liberation with nirvāṇa, and the independence criterion for intrinsic goodness
are Bhāṣya determinations.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> A manifested act requires an originating consciousness capable of expressing
> itself outwardly. Where that originating form of consciousness is absent,
> the corresponding mode of manifestation cannot occur. But the ethical
> predicate itself is not reducible to origin. Goodness belongs to different
> determinations through different grounds: liberation is good as the final
> pacification of suffering, while the wholesome roots and the two forms of
> moral regard are good through what they themselves are.

The transition between the two halves is philosophically decisive.

`Samutthāna` explains the genesis of a particular manifestation. Yet when the
interlocutor asks whether the wholesome and unwholesome character of all
dharmas should therefore be understood solely from their originating
conditions, Vasubandhu answers no.

Origin is one ground of predication, not the only ground.

```text

samutthānataḥ
    → because of what originates it
```

## 8. Logical Determination

**Distribution of obscured-indeterminate manifestation**

The first argument has two sides.

The lower-domain restriction is:

```text
obscured-indeterminate vijñapti in the desire domain
    requires
obscured-indeterminate productive citta of the relevant class

that productive citta does not exist in the desire domain

therefore
    no obscured-indeterminate vijñapti exists there
```

The upper-level restriction is:

```text
vijñapti
    requires savitarka-savicāra citta

from the second dhyāna upward
    that citta is absent

therefore
    vijñapti does not occur above Brahmaloka / first dhyāna
```

The remaining location is:

```text
Brahmaloka within the first dhyāna
    → possesses the required cognitive condition
    → permits obscured-indeterminate vijñapti
```

The Bhāṣya cites Mahābrahmā's deceit-generated bodily action concerning
Āyuṣmat Aśvajit as the reported example.

**Causal origin does not exhaust ethical truth**

The interlocutor generalizes too quickly:

```text
this manifestation is classified through its samutthāna

therefore perhaps
    every dharma is wholesome or unwholesome
    solely through its samutthāna
```

Vasubandhu rejects the inference.

Ethical predication has four grounds:

| Ground | Question answered | Example introduced here |
|---|---|---|
| `paramārthataḥ` | What is good in the ultimate liberative sense? | liberation / nirvāṇa |
| `svabhāvataḥ` | What is good through its own intrinsic character? | three wholesome roots, `hrī`, `apatrāpya` |
| `saṃprayogataḥ` | What is good through association? | completed in VAK 4.09 |
| `samutthānataḥ` | What is good through its originating condition? | completed in VAK 4.09 |

Therefore:

```text
same predicate: kuśala

does not imply

same ground of predication
```

This is not accidental polysemy. The Bhāṣya deliberately determines the
relation through which the predicate belongs.

**Ultimate goodness**

The argument is:

```text
nirvāṇa
    → complete pacification of all suffering
    → supreme security
    → wholesome in the ultimate sense
```

The comparison is with health.

Health is not merely one beneficial item among other beneficial items. It is
the condition in which illness has been pacified. Nirvāṇa is ultimately good
as the complete pacification of suffering.

**Intrinsic goodness**

The argument is:

```text
three wholesome roots
+ hrī
+ apatrāpya

do not depend for their goodness upon
    association with another wholesome factor
    or origination by another wholesome factor

therefore
    they are wholesome by intrinsic nature
```

The comparison is with beneficial medicine.

Medicine bears its curative character in itself rather than receiving that
character merely by proximity to something else curative.

**Four relations, not four substances**

The Bhāṣya is classifying grounds of ethical attribution.

It is not positing four hidden kinds of goodness as independently existing
objects.

```text
one ethical predicate
    → four determinate modes of belonging
```

This distinction is especially important for the Transcendental reading. The
understanding must determine the relation under which a predicate is valid. It
must not convert that relation into a transcendent entity called “Goodness.”

## 9. Interpretive Note

**The Dharma Talk: how does a predicate belong?**

The major Dharma Talk of VAK 4.08 begins when the interlocutor asks whether the
ethical character of dharmas is known solely from their originating condition.

The question is natural because the first half of the verse has just explained
a missing form of `vijñapti` through the absence of its `samutthāna`.

But a successful causal explanation in one case does not establish that every
predicate belongs causally in the same way.

Vasubandhu's answer is methodologically exact:

```text
Do not ask only:
    Is this dharma wholesome?

Also ask:
    Through what ground is it wholesome?
```

This moves Abhidharma beyond the construction of inventories.

It becomes discrimination of the relations through which determinations
belong to their subjects.

**Attribute and modality**

Our immediately preceding inquiry concerned the movement:

```text
Absolute
    → Attribute
        → Mode
            → Appearance
```

VAK 4.08 gives this inquiry a concrete ethical form.

`Kuśala` is not a flat attribute stamped identically upon every wholesome
dharma. Its predication has modes:

```text
ultimate
intrinsic
associative
originative
```

The predicate remains one, but the relation of inherence differs.

This is exactly the kind of discrimination required before speaking of Dharma
modality. A mode is not a second substance hidden behind the predicate. It is
the determinate way in which the content belongs and becomes actual.

The fourfold scheme should not yet be forced into a one-to-one identification
with a Hegelian or Sāṃkhya sequence. Its textual achievement is already
substantial: it prevents ethical determination from collapsing into a single
causal relation.

**Genesis and intrinsic nature**

The distinction between `samutthānataḥ` and `svabhāvataḥ` is particularly
important.

```text
samutthānataḥ
    → the determination belongs because of its source

svabhāvataḥ
    → the determination belongs without borrowing it
      from association or source
```

This does not require an eternal substance called `svabhāva`.

It distinguishes an underived ethical function within the classified field.
The wholesome roots, `hrī`, and `apatrāpya` serve as sources or conditions of
goodness for other factors precisely because their own goodness is not first
received from those relations.

This is a structure of grounding.

```text
derivative predication
    → requires a ground

intrinsically wholesome factors
    → function as proximate grounds

ultimate goodness
    → names completion in the pacification of suffering
```

**Abhidharma as discrimination rather than atomism**

The verse also supports our positive reconstruction of Abhidharma.

An atomizing understanding asks which item possesses the property “wholesome.”

Absolute Discrimination asks how the predicate is valid of each determination.

```text
inventory:
    x is wholesome
    y is wholesome
    z is wholesome

Abhidharma discrimination:
    x is wholesome ultimately
    y is wholesome intrinsically
    z is wholesome by association
    w is wholesome through origination
```

The second form preserves differences of ground within the unity of the
predicate.

That is closer to the positive sense of Abhidharma we are seeking: not a heap
of ultimate particles, but mastery of the determinate relations through which
Dharma articulates itself.

**A control for the positive theory of `avijñapti`**

We have left open a Kant-clean and Yoga-clean account of `avijñapti`.

VAK 4.08 supplies a methodological control for that future account.

We must not ask only whether `avijñapti` exists. We must discriminate:

```text
through what originating condition it arises

through what support it continues

through what association its ethical character is determined

through what practical function it belongs to Dharma
```

Such an account would treat `avijñapti` as a determinate modality within the
field of possible practical experience. It would not promote it into a hidden
object beyond that field.

That positive reconstruction remains open. The present verse gives us part of
the method by which it can eventually be made rigorous.
