## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“Non-restraint ends through acquiring restraint, death, or the
arising of both sexual characteristics.” There are three causes
of its cessation. It ends when one undertakes restraint or obtains
meditation-born restraint upon gaining samādhi through the strength
of conditions: the opposing determination is stronger. It ends
at death because the support is relinquished, and upon the arising
of both sexual characteristics because the support is disrupted.
Even if one gives up weapons and nets with the intention not to
act, non-restraint is not cut off without acquiring restraint.
Avoiding a disease's cause does not cure an established illness
without medicine; so too here.

If a person in non-restraint takes up the temporary upavāsa, does
that person return to non-restraint afterward, or become neither
restrained nor non-restrained? Some say the person returns to
non-restraint because the intention to give it up was never
lasting. Others introduce the comparison of a glowing iron ball
returning to dark when no longer heated. The text adds that
acquisition of non-restraint depends on `vijñapti`, manifest
action. The exact force and speaker alignment of these final
two sentences are compressed; they do not securely settle the
question in this witness.

How is avijñapti outside both restraint and non-restraint lost?
“The intermediate [avijñapti] ends through severance of force,
undertaking, activity, purpose, life, or root.” When the force
of confidence or affliction that projected it is cut off, it
ends, like the motion of a potter's wheel [or an arrow] when
its impulse is spent. It also ends when the undertaking is
relinquished—“Enough of this undertaking”—or when the action
stops, as when one no longer does what one undertook. It ends
when its `artha` is cut off: the enduring object involved, such
as a shrine, park, monastery, bed or seat, mechanism, or net.
It ends when the person's life ends or when the severance of
wholesome roots begins. These are the six causes for abandoning
intermediate avijñapti.

## 4. Non-restraint Requires a Contrary Form

The medicinal comparison distinguishes **ceasing a harmful
activity** from **losing the standing non-restraint**. Merely
discarding instruments of harm and resolving not to use them
does not, in this account, cut off an already acquired
`asaṃvara`. Acquisition of an opposing `saṃvara` does, because
the contrary form has greater force. Death and the bodily-support
change are different causes again; the Bhāṣya does not portray
them as moral improvement.

The temporary upavāsa problem tests whether the old standing
form returns when a time-limited contrary restraint expires.
`Eke` and `apare` mark at least two reported positions. The
analogy of iron and the assertion that reacquisition depends on
`vijñapti` need further comparison with another witness before
one can confidently assign a complete theory to each voice.

## 5. Six Endpoints of Intermediate Avijñapti

`Madhyamā` is avijñapti neither comprehensive restraint nor
comprehensive non-restraint. Its six endpoints show several ways
a continuing practical determination can lose support:

| Endpoint | Bhāṣya explanation |
|---|---|
| Force (`vega`) | The projected momentum of confidence or affliction ends |
| Undertaking (`ādāna`) | One relinquishes the adopted practice |
| Activity (`kriyā`) | One stops doing what was undertaken |
| Purpose-object (`artha`) | The enduring `vastu` through which the deed operates ends |
| Lifespan (`āyus`) | The bearer's life ends |
| Wholesome root (`mūla`) | Its severance begins |

The examples for `artha` include both meritorious constructions
and harmful devices. Here `artha` is a purposive determination
realized in a concrete `vastu`; it should not be rendered as
free-floating “meaning” or reduced to an arbitrary physical
object. The project's continuing efficacy depends on that
relation between purpose and enduring result.

## 6. Organon Contact Point

Our vow also needs an account of how a determination persists:
through its sustaining force, undertaking, activity, and realized
purpose. The Kośa's intermediate avijñapti gives a particularly
sharp test of the Idea of the True and Good emerging into a
world of enduring works. If the work or its sustaining practice
ends, the standing relation may change. This is our systematic
use of the passage; Vasubandhu's explicit object is the loss
of karmic avijñapti.
