# VAK 4.67 Bhāṣya — Manifest and Non-Manifest Courses

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
aśubhāḥ ṣaḍ avijñaptir dvidhaikaḥ te 'pi kurvataḥ /
dvividhāḥ sapta kuśalāḥ avijñaptiḥ samādhijāḥ // 4.67 //
```

> Six unwholesome courses are non-manifest; one is twofold. Those six
> too are twofold for one who performs them personally. The seven
> wholesome courses are twofold; those born from concentration are
> non-manifest.

The verse classifies the seven bodily and verbal courses according to
their mode of karmic existence: `vijñapti`, `avijñapti`, or both.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Of these courses of karma, “six unwholesome courses are non-manifest.”
Six unwholesome courses necessarily have non-manifest action as their
nature: killing, taking what is not given, false speech, divisive speech,
harsh speech, and disordered speech. This is because, when one causes
another person to perform them, there is no manifest action belonging to
the principal course.

“One is twofold.” Sexual misconduct always has both manifest and
non-manifest action as its nature because its completion must be one's
own. One who causes another to do it does not experience pleasure of
that kind.

“Those too are twofold for one who performs them.” Those same six
courses become twofold—manifest and non-manifest—when one performs them
oneself and death occurs at that time. If death occurs later, however,
there is only non-manifest action at the moment of the principal course.

Concerning the wholesome courses of karma, “the seven wholesome courses
are twofold.” The seven wholesome courses that possess material form are
necessarily of two kinds, manifest and non-manifest, because undertaken
discipline depends upon a manifest action.

“Those born from concentration are non-manifest.” Restraints included in
meditative absorption and uncontaminated restraint are called born from
concentration. They are only non-manifest because they depend upon mind
alone.

## 4. How Completion Determines the Mode

The six unwholesome courses are called necessarily `avijñapti` because
their responsible agent can instigate another person's manifest deed.
The Bhāṣya is therefore classifying the principal completion of the
course, not denying that the executor produces a visible bodily or verbal
act. The instigator lacks a principal `vijñapti` of that kind while the
course can nevertheless become complete for that continuum.

Sexual misconduct is the exception because its defining completion is
personal. It consequently possesses both modes. The commentary explains
this through the pleasure constitutive of that course rather than through
a general claim that nothing sexual can be delegated.

Personal performance of the other six introduces a temporal distinction:

```text
manifest act and completion coincide
    → vijñapti + avijñapti

manifest act occurs but death follows later
    → avijñapti alone at principal completion
```

The killing example shows why a visible movement and a completed karmic
course cannot be identified without qualification. The bodily act may
already have ceased when the result that completes the course occurs.

The wholesome side has a parallel genetic distinction. Undertaken
discipline (`samādānaśīla`) depends upon a manifest undertaking and thus
has both modes. Restraint generated by absorption or uncontaminated
concentration depends upon mind alone and is `avijñapti` only. “Mind
alone” explains the mode of generation here; it does not redefine
`avijñapti` as an unexpressed intention.

## 5. Organon Contact Point

For our Rational Moral Agent, the passage separates four coordinates that
a practical judgment must retain:

```text
responsible continuum
visible executor
principal completion
mode of generation
```

A course cannot be classified from its visible event alone. Delegation
can divide responsibility from execution, delayed completion can divide
the principal course from its preceding manifestation, and concentration
can generate stable restraint without an outward undertaking. In project
terms, `avijñapti` supplies an operative practical determination that is
not reducible to the currently displayed event. This is an Organon use of
the distinction; the Bhāṣya itself argues within the Sarvāstivāda
classification of material karma.
