# VAK 4.43 Bhāṣya — Who Can Bear a Standing Restraint?

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
nṛṇām asaṃvaro hitvā śaṇḍhapaṇḍadvidhākṛtīn /
kurūṃś ca saṃvaro 'py evaṃ devānāṃ ca nṛṇāṃ trayaḥ // 4.43 //
```

> Non-restraint occurs among humans, excepting the categories
> named here and the Kurus. Restraint likewise occurs among
> eligible humans, and also among gods. Among humans, all three
> kinds of restraint occur.

The natural unit runs from the opening question at [226|03]
through [226|24] in `Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt`.
The specification of restraint among gods starts with 4.44 at
[226|25]. The categories `śaṇḍha`, `paṇḍaka`, and
`dvidhākṛti` belong to the text's inherited bodily and Vinaya
classifications; they are not identities inferred about people
today.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

For which beings does non-restraint occur, and for which does
restraint occur? “Non-restraint occurs among humans, excepting
the śaṇḍhas, paṇḍakas, people of twofold bodily constitution,
and the Kurus.” On this account, non-restraint occurs only in
the human mode of birth, and even there the named categories
and the inhabitants of Uttarakuru are excluded. “Restraint is
likewise [found there], and among gods.” Thus restraint occurs
in two modes of birth: eligible humans and gods.

How is the exclusion of the named human categories established?
The response appeals to sūtra and Vinaya. The cited Mahānāma
passage describes a white-clothed householder endowed with the
male faculty, going to the Buddha for refuge and declaring,
“Regard me as an upāsaka”; it says this is sufficient to be an
upāsaka. The Vinaya also contains an instruction to remove a
person of the specified kind [the citation is compressed]. Why,
according to this account, is restraint unavailable to the
excluded categories? The text attributes this to a strong
affliction affecting the support, inability to exercise the
required reflective discernment, and lack of intense moral
shame and concern. Why, then, is non-restraint also unavailable?
Because even harmful intention is said to be unstable. Restraint
and non-restraint are contraries and are assigned to a common
field of eligible supports.

For the people of Uttarakuru, the text says neither undertaking
nor meditative attainment is present, nor the intention toward
harmful action; hence neither standing form occurs. Beings in
unfortunate modes of birth are also said to lack the intense
moral qualities whose presence or destruction would condition
the two forms. The support itself is likened to barren ground
where neither restraint nor non-restraint takes root [the
simile's closing words are damaged].

But a sūtra describes an egg-born nāga observing the eight-limbed
upavāsa on the eighth day of the fortnight [the middle phrase
is uncertain]. The answer is that this is good conduct only,
`sucaritamātra`, not technically acquired restraint. Thus the
specific restraint in this example is assigned to humans.
“Among humans, there are three”: all three restraints,
beginning with prātimokṣa, are found among humans.

## 4. Act, Standing Form, and Eligible Support

The question is about the **acquisition of standing saṃvara or
asaṃvara**, not whether a being can perform a good or harmful
act. The nāga example makes this explicit: an observance may
count as `sucarita` without counting as acquired restraint in
the Kośa's technical taxonomy. The same distinction was already
important in 4.36 when a restricted undertaking could yield
good conduct without full prātimokṣa restraint.

The inherited eligibility claims are made within this
Abhidharma and Vinaya framework. They depend on its account
of bodily support (`āśraya`), reflective capacity, and stable
intention. The passage gives us evidence for *that framework's
classification*; it does not supply evidence for applying
these categories to contemporary people or judging anyone's
actual capacity for ethical practice.

`Nṛṇāṃ trayaḥ` closes the verse by locating all three known
restraints—prātimokṣa, dhyāna-born, and uncontaminated—among
eligible humans. `Devānāṃ ca` is not yet fully distributed.
The Bhāṣya specifies which restraints gods and formless beings
can possess in 4.44.

## 5. Organon Contact Point

For our System, the useful distinction is between an action
and a continuing determination borne by a support. A vow's
formal persistence cannot be inferred merely from an isolated
act that resembles its outward performance. We can preserve
that distinction without importing the Kośa's inherited
eligibility exclusions into our account of the Rational Moral
Agent. The text's own conclusion here remains the distribution
of the three restraints among beings.
