# VAK_4.03 Bhāṣya — Perishing, Configuration, and Verbal Manifestation

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> na kasyacid ahetoḥ syād hetuḥ syāc ca vināśakaḥ /
> dvigrāhyaṃ syān na cāṇau tad vāgvijñaptis tu vāgdhvaniḥ // 4.03 //

> Otherwise destruction would never be without a cause; a productive cause
> would itself become a destroyer. Configuration would be apprehended by
> two faculties, yet it is not present in an atom. Verbal manifestation,
> however, is speech sound.

The first two pādas continue the proof of momentariness opened by 4.02. The
last two test the reality of bodily configuration and identify verbal
manifestation. The Kārikā study is `VAK_4.03.md`.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“Otherwise destruction would never be without a cause.” If destruction
required a cause as arising does, nothing would perish without a cause.
Yet cognitions, sounds, and flames, which are momentary, are seen to cease
without a separately producing cause of destruction. Someone may say that
one cognition destroys another, or a later sound destroys the earlier
sound. That does not work: doubt and certainty, pleasure and pain, or
attachment and aversion cannot be present together in the relevant way.
When a weaker cognition or sound follows a stronger one, how could the
weaker thing of the same kind destroy the stronger? And what would destroy
the final member of a series? Nor can the absence of a sustaining cause
itself be a cause. An appeal to merit and demerit as alternating causes of
arising and destruction at each moment also fails. The proposed special
cause could be multiplied for every conditioned thing, so the discussion
need not continue on that footing.

“If there were a destroying cause, the cause itself would be a destroyer.”
Suppose contact with fire caused the destruction of wood. When successively
more cooked qualities arise, the same or similar fire-contact that
produced the earlier qualities would also destroy them. But it is
untenable that the very condition of their being, or one just like it,
should also be the condition of their non-being. One might invent a
different cause in each successive flame; how would that account for the
varieties of cooked qualities arising through contact with alkali, frost,
acid, sun, water, or earth?

What, then, does fire do when boiling water dwindles? It increases the
power of the fire element. Under that influence the aggregate of water
becomes progressively attenuated until it no longer continues its series.
That is what contact with fire does here. Things have no separate cause
of destruction; being perishable, they cease as soon as they arise.
Momentary perishing is thereby established, and with it the absence of
the passage of one enduring thing from place to place. The appearance of
motion arises through uninterrupted production in different places, like
fire spreading through grass. With motion excluded, bodily manifestation as
configuration is established on the view just argued.

The Sautrāntikas object that configuration is not a real entity in its own
right. When much visible color appears in one direction, one designates it
“long”; relative to that, a smaller extent is “short.” Extension in four
directions is called “square”; equal extension all around is called
“round.” The same applies elsewhere. A firebrand seen rapidly in
successive places appears as a line or, when seen all around, a circle.
There is no additional kind of entity called configuration. If there
were, “it would be apprehended in two ways.” One judges something long by
sight and also by touch; a single material object of sight would thereby
be grasped by two faculties, which is not admitted.

An objection on behalf of real configuration proposes that the judgment of shape when touching
is memory prompted by an associated tangible quality, not direct touch of
a shape-dharma. Seeing fire can recall heat, and smelling a flower can
recall its color. The Sautrāntika reply grants those dependable associations,
but no specific tangible quality invariably accompanies a given shape. If
recollection could be regular without such association, the same account
would apply just as well to color, or recollection of shape would be
irregular. One also risks making many colors occupy one place, as with a
patterned covering. Thus configuration is not a separate real entity.

Moreover, anything resistant and materially real must be found in an
atom. “But that is not in an atom”: longness and the like are not found
in a single atom. They are designations made upon many visible color atoms
arranged in a certain way. To posit special atoms of configuration is
mere partiality until their own characteristic is established; only then
could their aggregation be invoked. An opponent points to pots of the
same color but different shapes. The reply compares the different
configurations called a line or circle in arrangements of ants. Another
asks why longness is seen in a distant post even when its color cannot
be distinguished. The answer is that indistinct visible material is
seen and construed as long, as one may construe a distant line as an
army. Sometimes only an indeterminate aggregate is seen: “something is
there.”

The question then presses the Sautrāntikas: having rejected bodily motion
and configuration as a real entity, what bodily manifestation do they
allow? They still designate configuration as bodily manifestation, but
not as a separate substance. Bodily karma is action with the body as its
basis: the volition that directs the body here and there. Verbal and
mental karma are understood correspondingly. What, then, of the sūtra's
“volition and action after having willed”? First comes planning volition:
“I shall do this or that.” Afterward comes operative volition, by which
the body is impelled; this is called action after having willed.

An objection says that without real bodily manifestation there would be
no desire-realm non-manifest action, with many unwelcome consequences.
The reply says that consequences of that objection will themselves have
consequences. Another objection says that if non-manifest action arises
from a special volition, it should follow consciousness, as a
concentration-born non-manifest action does. The reply denies the
parallel: the distinctive volition projects it differently. Even where
manifestation exists, its capacity to project non-manifest action looks
to the power of volition, because it is inert on its own.

The Vaibhāṣikas, by contrast, describe configuration as a real entity and
bodily manifestation as having configuration for its nature. “Verbal
manifestation, however, is speech sound”: sound whose nature is speech
is verbal manifestation. Non-manifest action has already been mentioned.

## 4. Argument and Voices

The unit has three linked debates. First, the expository argument denies a
separate cause of destruction and derives momentariness, then explains
apparent movement by successive arising. Second, the explicitly named
Sautrāntikas deny that `saṃsthāna` is a distinct `dravya`, using the
arguments from double apprehension and from the absence of configuration
in a single atom. Third, they reconstruct bodily karma through directing
volition; the Vaibhāṣikas reassert real configuration. The final pāda
identifies vocal sound as verbal manifestation.

The brief challenges within these debates are not all assigned to a
single named school in the text. The Sautrāntika and Vaibhāṣika
attributions are explicit only where the prose marks them.

## 5. The Inference Concerning Destruction

The starting distinction is between a produced effect and absence.
`Vināśa` is not a further positive product requiring a `vināśaka`.
The examples of cognitions, sounds, and flames challenge the idea that a
later member of a series destroys its predecessor. The fire example
challenges the explanatory coherence of making the same condition both
producer and destroyer of cooked qualities. Fire still has causal work:
it changes what succeeds in the series. The argument thus preserves
conditioned succession while denying an enduring traveler or a
separately manufactured absence.

`Gatyabhimāna` names the appearance or conviction of movement when
successive events arise without interruption in adjacent places. The
grass-fire analogy makes the appearance intelligible; it does not by
itself settle all questions about the causal production of the series.

## 6. The Sautrāntika Challenge to Configuration

The crucial distinction is between saying **there is a configuration**
and saying **configuration is a separately real material entity**. The
Sautrāntikas accept the first as designation (`prajñapti`) and reject
the second (`dravya`). Long, short, square, and round depend on ordered
pluralities. `Dvigrāhya` argues that a putatively single shape-dharma
would be grasped both by sight and touch. A defender of real configuration
counters that
touch evokes memory of shape; the Sautrāntika answers that the required
invariable association with a tangible is absent. `Na cāṇau tat` argues that a
materially real resistant dharma should occur in an atom, whereas
longness does not.

The objection from a distant post is especially instructive: an
indeterminate visible aggregate may support a judgment of length even
when a particular color is not discriminated. The Sautrāntika reply
treats that as a judgment made upon indistinct visible material, not
proof of an independently real shape.

## 7. Bodily and Verbal Action After the Dispute

On the Sautrāntika account, bodily manifestation may still be designated
as configuration. Bodily karma is more fundamentally the volition that
directs the body. The prose distinguishes planning volition from the
operative volition that impels bodily action. This is a proposed reading
of the sūtra's `cetayitvā karma`, not an erasure of the body's role.

The challenge about `avijñapti` shows why the ontology of manifest action
matters to vows and continuing restraint. The Sautrāntika answer appeals
to a distinctive volition's capacity to project a different result; the
Vaibhāṣika holds configuration itself to be real. The present unit closes
with the simpler identification of verbal `vijñapti` as speech sound.
The substantial status of `avijñapti` is taken up immediately in 4.04.

## 8. Translation and Doctrinal Limits

“Configuration” renders `saṃsthāna` in both positions, while “designation”
and “separately real entity” preserve the contested ontological status.
“Appearance of motion” renders `gatyabhimāna` without suggesting that
ordinary observation is sheer falsehood. `Saṃkalpa-cetanā` and the later
operative volition must not be collapsed into one timeless intention.

The source is an e-text with visible corruptions and anomalous line
labels. Its school positions are clear in outline, but close decisions
about several objections await a critical witness comparison. This
Study gives a continuous working translation, not an emended edition.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

**Textual determination.** The Kośa's argument contests both an enduring
mover and a separately real configuration. Yet the Sautrāntika still
allows configured appearance and bodily action, now understood through
arrangement, designation, and directing volition. Mediation has not
vanished; the account of what mediates has become more demanding.

**Organon reconstruction.** Your graph analogy can be useful if kept at
its proper level. Nodes can mark determinate appearances, and edges can
represent proposed conditions or relations. A graph as such is a logical
construction; mathematical treatment begins when one gives it formal
properties and operations. Neither a drawn edge nor a smooth interpolated
curve proves that one identical entity passed continuously between its
endpoints. The Kantian question here is what warrants applying the
relation to experienced objects. The Kośa's own answer must be assessed
through its distinctions among perception, inference, succession,
configuration, and volition. This is a comparative placement, not a
translation of `saṃsthāna` into graph theory or Kantian terminology.
