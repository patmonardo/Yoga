# VAK 4.50 Bhāṣya — Whether and When Karma Ripens

## 1. Kārikā Anchor


> That karma is determinate or indeterminate. The determinate is

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“That karma is determinate or indeterminate.” The threefold karma
beginning with karma to be experienced pleasantly is to be
understood as both determinate and indeterminate. Indeterminate
means that its result is not necessarily to be experienced.

“The determinate is again threefold, beginning with what is to be
experienced in this life.” These are karma to be experienced in the
present life, in the next birth, and on a later occasion. These
three determinate classes together with karma whose result is
indeterminate make four kinds.

“Some count karma fivefold.” Others hold that karma has five kinds
by dividing the indeterminate class in two: determinate with respect
to maturation and indeterminate with respect to maturation. Karma
to be experienced in the present life ripens in the birth in which
it was performed; karma to be experienced at the next birth ripens
in the second birth; karma to be experienced on a later occasion
ripens after that.

Others say that even karma called “to be experienced in the present
life” can have its maturation in a different birth; its name is
established according to the circumstance of its initiation. A
following compressed sentence appears to raise a problem about an
undesirable result of a karma whose result was thought desirable;
the exact argument is uncertain in this e-text. The Vaibhāṣikas do
not accept the proposed extension. For some karma bears fruit soon
and not at a distance, while other karma does the reverse, like
external seeds. Thus *suvarcalā* bears fruit in three fortnights,
whereas barley, wheat, and similar crops do so in six months.

## 4. Fourfold and Fivefold Counts

The preceding pleasant, painful, and neutral-result karmas can each
be considered under the new determination. The Bhāṣya explicitly
glosses `aniyata` as `nāvaśyavedanīya`: its fruition is not
inevitable. Its basic count is:

| Class | Maturation time or status |
|---|---|
| `dṛṣṭadharmavedanīya` | In the life in which the karma is done |
| `upapadyavedanīya` | In the next birth |
| `aparaparyāyavedanīya` | In a later birth |
| `aniyatavedanīya` | Not necessarily to be experienced |

The fivefold count is attributed to `apare`, “others.” They split
the last class according to whether `vipāka` is determinate or
indeterminate. This introduces two questions that the opening labels
alone can obscure: **is maturation assured, and is its life fixed?**
The next verse develops the distinction more formally. We should
not silently assign this alternative count to the Vaibhāṣikas.

## 5. The Dispute Over “This Life”

Another view, also marked `apare`, proposes that present-life karma
can ripen in another birth, its name reflecting its beginning
(`ārambhavaśāt`). The Vaibhāṣikas reject that reading. Their seed
analogy claims that near and remote fruition are genuine differences
among causes, not merely flexible labels assigned at their start.
The analogy does not establish that every karmic result is certain;
it is offered specifically against moving the fruition of
`dṛṣṭadharmavedanīya` to another birth.

The text's intervening `mā bhūd ...` sentence may supply an objection
to the alternative reading, but its wording is too unstable here to
make a secure doctrinal inference from it.

## 6. Organon Contact Point

For our OM account of a determination awaiting appearance, this
passage requires two separate coordinates: **whether** a result is
assured and **when** it may appear. The seed analogy adds that
different processes can have different characteristic spans. This
is a project use of the Kośa distinction; the source is classifying
karmic maturation across lives, not establishing a general scheduler
for all events.
