# VAK 4.36 Bhāṣya — Universality, Partial Vows, and Non-restraint

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
saṃvaraḥ sarvasattvebhyo vibhāṣā tv aṅgakāraṇaiḥ /
asaṃvaras tu sarvebhyaḥ sarvāṅgebhyo na kāraṇaiḥ // 4.36 //
```

> Restraint is acquired with respect to all beings; its limbs and
> causes admit different cases. Non-restraint, according to the
> initial account, concerns all beings and all limbs, but not all
> causes at once.

The natural source unit runs from [220|04–05] through [222|07] in
`Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt`. The next question, **how**
non-restraint is acquired, begins at [222|08] and opens 4.37.
The research Bhāṣya omits substantial prose at [220|25–221|07]
and [221|13–222|04]; this study restores it from the running text.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Are restraint and non-restraint acquired with respect to all beings,
all limbs, and all causes, or do they differ? There are determinate
rules. “Restraint is acquired with respect to all beings, but its
limbs and causes admit different cases.” It always extends to all
beings. Some forms involve every limb, such as the monk's restraint;
others involve four. Karmic courses are the limbs of restraint.
As to causes, one may speak of all three if the causes are
non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion; or of one if one means
the weak, medium, or strong degree of the undertaking mind.

Using the second sense of cause, a person may be restrained toward
all beings yet not through all limbs or strengths: one who takes
up lay, one-day, or novice restraint with a mind of a single
strength. Another may be restrained toward all beings and through
all limbs but not every strength: one who takes up monk's
restraint at one strength. One may have all limbs and strengths
across three undertakings; or all strengths but not all limbs
across lay, one-day, and novice undertakings. There is no case
of restraint that does not concern all beings.

Why? Restraint is acquired only through a wholesome intention
extending to all beings; otherwise a harmful intention remains.
Someone who restricts the undertaking by being (“I refrain only
with respect to this being”), limb, place, duration (“for a
month”), or occasion (“except in war”) acquires merely good
conduct, not prātimokṣa restraint. The witness's sentence
introducing these five limits lacks the negative required by
that conclusion. How can restraint concern beings whom one
could not possibly harm? Because one undertakes it with the
intention not to injure the lives of any beings. If it covered
only those presently reachable, it would grow and shrink as
beings moved between reachable and unreachable positions. Its
acquisition and loss could then occur without their proper
causes: this is the Vaibhāṣika argument.

An interlocutor objects that restraint does not grow or shrink
when grass appears or withers, so it need not do so when beings
become reachable or unreachable. The reply says that beings and
grass differ in just the point at issue. The objection presses
again: if extinct beings no longer exist, why would restraint not
shrink? The grass analogy, it says, is no adequate answer. The
earlier answer—an intention extending to all beings—is preferred.
Another objection asks whether later Buddhas would have less
morality because earlier Buddhas had entered final nirvāṇa before
their restraint could be acquired with respect to them. The answer
is that the restraint concerns all beings; had those Buddhas still
been living, they too would have been included.

So much for the bases of restraint. “Non-restraint concerns all
beings and all limbs, but not all causes.” On the initial account,
it concerns all beings and karmic courses. There is no person
possessing only a defective fragment of non-restraint. It does
not arise from weak, medium, and strong mind simultaneously.
Someone who acquired non-restraint with a weak mind still possesses
it at that strength even if a later killing has the stronger
manifestation (`vijñapti`) of killing. The medium and strong cases
are to be understood similarly.

The text lists occupational examples: sheep and poultry slaughterers,
pig slaughterers, bird catchers, fishers, hunters, thieves,
executioners, prison keepers, elephant trappers, dog cookers, and
net trappers. It also discusses kings, agents of punishment, and
legal officials as non-restrained by virtue of the harmful
functions at issue [the source's final designation is defective].
“Sheep slaughterer” means one who kills sheep, and the other
names are to be read in the same way.

Restraint's universality is intelligible because it is received
with an intention for the welfare of all beings. But how can
non-restraint concern *all* beings in a slaughterer who cares for
parents, children, and spouse and would not kill them even to
save a life? A proposed reply says such a person might kill them
if they became sheep, without knowing they were the same people.
The objection answers that noble persons cannot become animals,
and that appeal to future identities cuts both ways: a butcher
might refuse to kill a sheep if it became his child. One cannot
simply infer an intention to kill everyone from an occupation
when a person has no such intention toward family.

Consider also a sheep slaughterer who has never stolen, remains
faithful to a spouse, and is unable to speak. How could such a
person acquire non-restraint through every limb, when the relevant
intentions have not deteriorated? A mute person can, replies the
other side, communicate by bodily gesture what would otherwise
be conveyed by speech. Finally, what of someone who undertakes
only two or three training rules? The Vaibhāṣikas deny a partial
or regionally limited `asaṃvarika` in every case. The Sautrāntikas
allow partial non-restraint and restraint according to what is
actually undertaken, apart from the eight fixed kinds of
restraint: only the corresponding measure of morality or
immorality is bound.

## 4. The Disputed Universal

The verse's first half fixes the **being-range** of formal
prātimokṣa restraint, while allowing authorized variation in
limbs and in the strength of the undertaking mind. Its five
`niyama` examples show what the initial account refuses: a
private restriction by favored beings, place, duration, or
circumstance. This must be read alongside the earlier allowance
for the specified limbs of lay and one-day restraints. The
source's brief `aṅganiyama` example does not erase that
institutional differentiation; how these claims fit together
deserves further textual review.

The extended debate tests whether universality belongs to the
actual intention, a formal class of restraint, or a set of
empirically reachable beings. The grass analogy and the case of
earlier Buddhas matter because they test whether the domain of a
vow changes whenever its possible objects change. The passage
does not end with a simple appeal to every currently reachable
being.

## 5. Non-restraint and the School Dispute

The second half initially treats `asaṃvara` as extending to all
beings and all karmic limbs, though with one causal strength at
a time. The family and selective-conduct objections expose the
cost of that formal claim. The occupational list identifies
practices named in this doctrinal taxonomy; the argument itself
shows why assigning a total moral profile from occupation alone
is contested.

The explicit closing contrast must remain visible. **Vaibhāṣikas**
deny a partial or regional `asaṃvarika`. **Sautrāntikas** allow
partial restraint and non-restraint outside the eight fixed forms,
in accord with the actual undertaking (`yathābhyupagamam`). This
is a disagreement about the scope of standing moral forms, not
merely about how to count individual misdeeds.

## 6. Organon Contact Point

Our vow to know the Self and let adhyāsa cease makes a universal
claim. This passage asks how such a claim obtains a determinate
range while finite agents act among changing beings and
circumstances. The answer cannot be supplied merely by counting
currently reachable objects; the undertaking's intention and
form matter. The Sautrāntika objection also demands that we not
replace an agent's actual commitment with a total status imputed
from a category. This is an Organon use of the Kośa's argument,
not its own formulation of the Self or the Idea of the Good.
