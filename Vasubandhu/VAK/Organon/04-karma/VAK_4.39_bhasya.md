# VAK 4.39 Bhāṣya — Can a Root Offense Leave Restraint Intact?

## 1. Kārikā Anchor


> Some say restraint is lost through a downfall offense; others,
> through disappearance of the true Dharma. The Kāśmīras instead
> hold that the offender has both morality and misconduct, like
> someone who has both wealth and debt.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“Some say [restraint is lost] through a downfall offense.” They
say that any one of the four grave offenses ends a monk's or
novice's restraint. “Others say through disappearance of the true
Dharma,” because when the true Dharma has disappeared, training
rules, monastic boundaries, and formal acts cease to operate.
“The Kāśmīras, however, hold that the offender has both, like
wealth and debt.” The Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣikas say that even someone
who has incurred a root offense does not lose monk's restraint:
a disturbance in one part should not destroy the whole. Nor is
morality cut off by other offenses. The offender possesses both
morality and misconduct, just as someone may have both wealth
and debt. When the offense is disclosed, the person has morality
and no longer misconduct, as one who repays a debt retains wealth
without debt.

But the Blessed One said that such a monk becomes no ascetic, no
son of the Śākyas, and falls from monkhood. In what sense has
his ascetic status fallen and been defeated? The Kāśmīra reply
says this concerns monkhood in the highest sense. The response
calls that an overbold interpretation: it gives another meaning
to what the Buddha stated definitively and supplies occasions
for misconduct and many afflictions. How is the wording
definitive? The Vinaya distinguishes four senses of “monk”: one
so designated, one so professing, one who begs, and one who has
broken the afflictions. In this disciplinary context it specifies
a person fully ordained by the formal act with motion as the
fourth. The offender was never necessarily a monk in the highest
liberated sense, so losing only that status cannot be the point.

The earlier claim that disturbance of one part cannot destroy
the whole is also challenged by the image of a palm tree whose
head is cut off: it cannot sprout or grow. A part that is a
*root* of the whole is different from an incidental part. Cut
it, and the remaining restraint cannot grow. A grave root
offense breaches the boundary of monkhood and, through intense
lack of moral concern, cuts restraint at its root. On this
argument, loss of the entire restraint is reasonable.

The person in question is not permitted even a mouthful of
communal food or a small place in communal lodging and is
excluded from the monks' shared life. A quoted injunction calls
for removing someone who claims to be a monk but is not one.
What sort of monkhood remains? The Kāśmīra side says that some
monkhood remains, whatever its condition. It appeals to the
teaching that there are four ascetics, including one who corrupts
the Path. The reply grants the wording but says that such a
person is called an ascetic only by surviving outward form, like
a burnt or exhausted thing that retains a name while losing its
capacity [the transmitted comparisons are damaged].

Wouldn't a single instance of misconduct, on this reasoning,
make a person cease to be a monk? No: the reply does not say
that everyone becomes defeated immediately upon an offense.
One may avoid the status of a defeated offender because of the
particular condition of one's continuum, even through an
immediate refusal to conceal the offense, as the disciplinary
authority stipulates. But whoever *is* defeated is no longer a
monk in the relevant sense. Why, then, is that person not simply
ordained anew? The reply points to a continuum damaged by
intense shamelessness and incapable of acquiring restraint, not
merely to the name “monk.” Someone who has deliberately laid down
the training is likewise not reordained in that circumstance.
Why insist on preserving the title of monk for a person in such
a condition? The closing remark rejects the value of that
merely nominal monkhood.

As for the disappearance of the true Dharma, without the Vinaya
acts no previously unacquired restraint can arise. But restraint
already acquired is not thereby lost.

## 4. The Two Disputes About Loss

The Kārikā reports three positions; the Bhāṣya argues through
them. The Kāśmīra wealth-and-debt account permits simultaneous
`śīla` and `dauḥśīlya`, with disclosure likened to settling the
debt. The opposing argument distinguishes an ordinary breach
from a `maulī āpatti` that cuts a constitutive root. The palm
tree example shows why “only one part was damaged” is not a
sufficient reply when that part sustains the whole.

The hermeneutical dispute matters as much as the taxonomy. The
attempt to read a definitive Vinaya statement as concerning only
`paramārthabhikṣutva` is called `abhisāhasa`, overboldness. The
counterargument tests which sense of “monk” the Vinaya itself
assigns to the case. Later references to a residual ascetic
form do not by themselves establish that the effective
disciplinary restraint remains.

The disappearance-of-Dharma proposal receives a different
answer: no Vinaya acts means no **new acquisition**. That does
not mean a person already restrained automatically loses what
was acquired. This distinction closes the unit.

## 5. Organon Contact Point

For our account of a vow, 4.39 asks whether an opposed act adds
a debt to a still-standing form or destroys a condition that
constitutes the form itself. It also asks whether the name of
a role can persist after its effective capacity is lost. Those
are useful tests when we say that adhyāsa ceases and the Idea
of the True and Good emerges: the project must distinguish
repairable failure, loss of a constitutive condition, and a
merely nominal claim to an achieved stance. This is our
reconstruction; the passage itself concerns Vinaya restraint
and contested readings of monastic status.
