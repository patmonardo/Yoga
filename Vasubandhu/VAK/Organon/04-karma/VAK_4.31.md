# VAK_4.31

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

```sanskrit
सर्वे चेत् संवृताः एकदेशकार्यादयः कथम् ।
तत्पालनात् किल प्रोक्ताः मृद्वादित्वं यथा मनः ॥ ४.३१ ॥
```

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

```iast
sarve cet saṃvṛtā ekadeśakāryādayaḥ katham /
tatpālanāt kila proktā mṛdvāditvaṃ yathā manaḥ // 4.31 //
```

Padapāṭha-style separation:

```iast
sarve cet saṃvṛtāḥ eka-deśa-kāri-ādayaḥ katham /
tat-pālanāt kila proktāḥ mṛdu-ādi-tvam yathā manaḥ //
```

The verse continues the dispute over whether every upāsaka necessarily
possesses lay restraint. The reportive `kila` is essential: the explanation
based on differing preservation of the rules is presented as a received
answer, not as an uncontested conclusion of the Bhāṣya.

| `cet` | conditional particle | if |

**`ekadeśakāryādayaḥ`**

The compact `ādi` is unfolded by the Bhāṣya into four scriptural
designations:

```text
ekadeśakārī
    practitioner of one part

pradeśakārī
    partial practitioner

yadbhūyaḥkārī
    practitioner of the greater part

pūripūrṇakārī
    fully practicing practitioner
```

The verse itself compresses the latter three under “and so forth.” Their
explicit enumeration belongs to the Bhāṣya.

**`tatpālanāt`**

`Pālana` is guarding, preserving, or maintaining. The initial answer says
that a person is named according to the particular training rules that the
person preserves. The pronoun `tat` therefore refers distributively to the
relevant part or training rule, not simply to restraint as an undivided
whole.

**`kila`**

The particle marks distance from the explanation:

```text
tat-pālanāt proktāḥ
    they are called so because of preserving those rules

tat-pālanāt kila proktāḥ
    they are said, according to the received account,
    to be called so because of preserving those rules
```

This restraint is warranted by the Bhāṣya, which immediately subjects the
answer to the criticism `idam utsūtraṃ vartate`: “this runs contrary to the
sūtra.”

**`mṛdvāditvam`**

The `ādi` is explained as `madhya` and `adhimātra`:

```text
mṛdu       weak
madhya     medium
adhimātra  strong
```

These are degrees of the restraint itself. They must not be identified with
the four designations concerning how much is practiced or preserved.

## 4. Grammar

**The conditional objection**

```text
sarve cet saṃvṛtāḥ
eka-deśa-kāri-ādayaḥ katham
```

The protasis is explicit: “if all are restrained.” The apodosis is an
elliptical question: “how [are there] one-part practitioners and the rest?”
The Bhāṣya supplies `upāsakāḥ` as the group quantified by `sarve` and
`upāsakasaṃvarasthāḥ` as the doctrinal force of `saṃvṛtāḥ`.

The question presses an apparent incompatibility:

```text
all upāsakas possess lay restraint
    versus
the Buddha distinguishes degrees of practice
```

**The reported answer**

```text
tat-pālanāt kila proktāḥ
```

`Proktāḥ` agrees with `ekadeśakāryādayaḥ`. The ablative `tatpālanāt` gives
the ground for the designation. `Kila` qualifies the assertion as reported or
received.

The Bhāṣya states the answer more explicitly:

```text
yaḥ hi yat śikṣāpadam pālayati
saḥ tat-kārī iti uktaḥ
```

> A person is called the practitioner of whichever training rule that person
> preserves.

**The independent comparison**

```text
mṛdu-ādi-tvam yathā manaḥ
```

The expression is compressed. The Bhāṣya supplies the intended subject from
the discussion: all restraints have weakness, medium strength, or great
strength, just as mind does. The comparison concerns gradation by intensity,
not numerical division into parts.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> If all are restrained, how are there one-part practitioners and the rest?
> They are said to be so because of preserving those [parts]; [restraint] has
> weakness and the other degrees, just as mind does.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> If all upāsakas are established in lay restraint, how can the Buddha speak
> of one-part, partial, predominantly practicing, and fully practicing lay
> followers? According to the received explanation, they are designated by
> the particular training rules they preserve. Restraint itself, however, may
> be weak, medium, or strong, just as the mind of the person who bears it may
> be weak, medium, or strong.

The four practitioner classes and the explicit threefold scale are supplied
by the Bhāṣya. “According to the received explanation” renders the force of
`kila`; it prevents the first answer from being presented as Vasubandhu’s
unqualified conclusion.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> Possessing a practical form, preserving its particular rules, and bearing
> it with a certain intensity are different determinations. The received
> account says that every upāsaka possesses lay restraint while practical
> designations vary with the rules actually guarded. Yet the Bhāṣya exposes
> the scriptural difficulty of that solution: the classifications may concern
> not merely later failures of execution, but the person’s relation to the
> known and undertaken measure of the restraint itself. Independently of that
> dispute, the same kind of restraint may be weak, medium, or strong according
> to the mind-continuum in which it operates.

This rendering keeps the Bhāṣya’s dispute open. It does not convert the
reported Vaibhāṣika answer into the settled meaning of the kārikā.

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**Acquisition and preservation**

```text
saṃvara-lābha
    acquisition or possession of restraint

śikṣāpada-pālana
    guarding a particular training rule
```

The initial explanation holds acquisition constant while allowing
preservation to vary. The subsequent objection asks whether this distinction
really accounts for the scriptural classification.

**`utsūtra`**

`Utsūtra` means running outside or contrary to the sūtra. It is not a mild
qualification. The Bhāṣya uses it to challenge the answer that all upāsakas
are equally established in complete restraint and differ only in later
observance.

**`parimāṇa`**

`Parimāṇa` is the measure or full extent of lay restraint. The Bhāṣya
distinguishes two cases:

```text
knowing the full restraint
    but breaking particular rules

not knowing its full measure
    and being capable of only so much training
```

The objector argues that the scriptural question is meaningful in the second
case; in the first, the answer would already be obvious.

**`śāstṛprajñapti`**

The Teacher’s prescription or designation fixes the determinate allocation
of limbs belonging to upāsaka, śrāmaṇera, and bhikṣu discipline. An opposing
position extends this institutional principle to the status itself: someone
might be called an upāsaka by designation even without restraint. The
Kāśmīras reject that extension.

**`prajñaptitaḥ`**

Here `prajñaptitaḥ` means by designation or conventionally. It does not by
itself mean fictitious or causally empty. The dispute is whether institutional
designation can establish upāsaka status without the corresponding restraint.

**Strength of restraint**

The Bhāṣya determines the three strengths through
`sasaṃtānacittavaśāt`—according to the mind belonging to the person’s own
continuum:

```text
one kind of restraint
    weak
    medium
    strong
```

Consequently, an arhat may have weak prātimokṣa restraint while an ordinary
person has strong prātimokṣa restraint. Spiritual rank and the intensity of
this restraint are different scales.

## 8. Logical Determination

**Four determinations**

```text
religious status
    who is designated an upāsaka

possession of restraint
    whether lay restraint has been acquired

extent of preservation
    which training rules are actually guarded

intensity of restraint
    whether the restraint is weak, medium, or strong
```

None of these relations may silently replace another.

**The initial solution**

```text
all upāsakas
    equally possess lay restraint

different rules preserved
    → different practitioner designations
```

This solution preserves the universality of restraint while locating
difference in practice.

**The Bhāṣya’s internal criticism**

```text
if the classes merely name broken and unbroken rules
    the scriptural question is trivial

if the question concerns ignorance of the full measure
    relation to the restraint itself remains disputed
```

The Bhāṣya therefore does not permit the simple opposition between complete
formal acquisition and incomplete empirical performance to close the issue.

**Institutional designation**

```text
Teacher’s prescription
    → fixes the limbs of disciplinary forms

disputed extension:
Teacher’s prescription
    → may fix upāsaka status without restraint

Kāśmīra response:
    no upāsaka status without upāsaka restraint
```

This is a dispute about the sufficient ground of practical status, not merely
about terminology.

**Two independent scales**

```text
one-part | partial | greater-part | complete
    scale of practice or preservation

weak | medium | strong
    scale of intensity
```

The verse places the scales together, but the Bhāṣya keeps their functions
distinct.

## 9. Interpretive Note

**The universal form and its actuality**

VAK 4.31 initially appears to support a clean Organon distinction:

```text
restraint acquired as a whole
    → universal practical form

rules guarded to differing degrees
    → differentiated actuality
```

That distinction remains useful, but Vasubandhu does not let it become an
automatic solution. The sūtra-based objection asks whether the universal was
in fact acquired in its full measure, known only obscurely, incompletely
undertaken, or merely assigned by institutional designation.

The practical form therefore has at least four moments:

```text
designation
acquisition
knowledge of measure
actual preservation
```

Their relation must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

**`Kila` as disciplined distance**

The small particle `kila` prevents doctrinal closure. It allows the kārikā to
state the inherited answer while the Bhāṣya tests it against scripture and
its own consequences. This is not indecision. It is Abhidharma functioning as
discrimination among claims, grounds, and levels of validity.

**Intensity belongs to the operative continuum**

The final determination shifts from the number of rules to the strength with
which restraint operates:

```text
same structural kind
    + different mind-continuum
    → different intensity
```

The arhat and ordinary-person example blocks a prestige hierarchy from
replacing analysis. A higher spiritual rank does not make every practical
determination stronger in every respect.

**Contact with the practical Copula**

The proposition “this person is an upāsaka” can be grounded differently:

```text
by institutional designation
by possession of restraint
by undertaking a determinate measure
by actual preservation
```

The Bhāṣya refuses to treat those grounds as interchangeable. The Copula of
practical identity is therefore not an empty label; it requires a stated
ground and remains subject to doctrinal dispute.

This is an Organon contact point. The scriptural classifications, the
reported answer, the `utsūtra` criticism, the institutional dispute, and the
three degrees of strength belong to the Bhāṣya itself.
