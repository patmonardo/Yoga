# VAK 4.31 Bhāṣya — Partial Practice and the Strength of Restraint

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
sarve cet saṃvṛtā ekadeśakāryādayaḥ katham /
tatpālanāt kila proktā mṛdvāditvaṃ yathā manaḥ // 4.31 //
```

> If all are restrained, how can there be one-part practitioners and
> the rest? They are said to be so according to what they preserve, it
> is claimed. Restraint, like mind, may be weak and so forth.

The running unit extends from [215|14] through [216|12–13] in
`Vasubandhu/Sources/kosabhasya.txt`. The question about refuge at
[216|13–14] opens the next discussion and is reserved for 4.32.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“If all are restrained, how can there be one-part practitioners and
the rest?” If every upāsaka stands in lay restraint, why did the
Blessed One speak of a lay follower who practices one part, a part,
the greater part, or the whole? “They are said to be so according to
what they preserve, it is claimed.” For a person is called a
practitioner of whichever training rule that person guards; yet all
are equally established in restraint.

“This runs contrary to the sūtra.” What is contrary to it? The claim
that restraint is acquired solely through accepting upāsaka status,
on the supposed basis of a statement beginning “from killing ...”.
But the sūtra is not read in that way. The account defining an
upāsaka occurs in the Mahānāma Sūtra, and not elsewhere.

As for the passage read “for life, free from [or apart from] life,
gone for refuge, filled with confidence,” there those who had seen
the truth were showing a confidence born of understanding and an
adherence to the true Dharma even at the cost of their lives: “Even
for the sake of life, we are incapable of abandoning this Dharma.”
That passage does not define restraint. Moreover, “free from life”
is not read anywhere; who would read a phrase so obscure in meaning?

Nor does the question about one-part practitioners make sense if it
concerns simply people who have broken training rules. In that case,
what need is there for a special answer? Who, knowing lay restraint,
would not also know that someone who has not broken a particular
training rule practices that rule? The question does make sense when
addressed to people who do not know the extent of lay restraint and
are capable of training in just that much: “Venerable sir, by how
much does a lay follower become a one-part practitioner, and by how
much a complete practitioner?”

But if a person could be an upāsaka without restraint, or with an
incomplete restraint, could the same not be true of a monk or a
novice? How, then, are the particular limbs of lay restraint and the
other restraints fixed? By the Teacher's designation. Let the
determination of upāsaka status and the other statuses likewise be
accepted as dependent on the Teacher's designation. Thus, on this
proposal, someone can conventionally be an upāsaka even without
restraint, whereas a monk or novice cannot. The Kāśmīras, however,
do not accept this.

As for all restraints, they may be weak and so forth, as minds may
be. Their weakness, middling strength, or great strength depends on
the mind in the person's own continuum. Accordingly, even an
arhat's prātimokṣa restraint could be weak, while an ordinary
person's could be strong.

## 4. The Disputed Lay Classification

The first answer distinguishes acquiring `upāsakasaṃvara` from
guarding particular `śikṣāpada`s. On that answer, every upāsaka has
the restraint, while the names “one-part” through “complete” track
what each actually preserves. The Kārikā's `kila` marks this as a
reported explanation. The Bhāṣya immediately subjects it to a
sūtra-based objection, so it is not the uncontested result of 4.31.

The counterargument depends on the **question posed in the sūtra**.
If its audience already knew the full scope of lay restraint, an
answer based on whether they had broken particular rules would be
trivial. The question is intelligible when the measure of the
restraint itself is in question. This challenges the neat division
between full acquisition and partial preservation; it does not by
itself settle every case of lay status.

The Mahānāma-sūtra exchange is compressed and changes speakers
quickly. In particular, the cited `prāṇāpetaṃ` formula is challenged
within the text. The translation above makes its proposed sense
visible without treating that wording as a secure definition of
restraint or assigning every objection to a named school.

## 5. Designation and Intensity

The objection involving monks and novices asks whether permitting
an upāsaka without complete restraint would dissolve all status
boundaries. The reply invokes `śāstṛprajñapti`: the Teacher's
designation fixes the limbs and can, on the alternative proposal,
fix who counts as an upāsaka. The Kāśmīras expressly reject the
proposal of upāsaka status without restraint. **Status by
designation** therefore remains disputed here.

The closing determination has a different axis. `Mṛdu`, `madhya`,
and `adhimātra` qualify the **strength of restraint** according to
the mind of an individual's continuum. They are not alternative
names for the one-part through complete classifications. The arhat
and ordinary-person comparison shows that spiritual attainment does
not mechanically fix the strength of this particular restraint.

## 6. Organon Contact Point

For our Jñāna work, this passage asks us to classify four relations
separately: recognized status, acquisition of restraint, preservation
of particular rules, and intensity of restraint. A Pure Reason vow
would likewise need an account of how it is instituted, what is
actually sustained, and how strongly it operates. That is our
reconstruction; the Kośa's explicit dispute concerns upāsaka status
and its sūtra-based classifications.
