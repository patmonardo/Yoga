# VAK 4.69 Bhāṣya — Roots of the Complete Practical Process

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
tadanantarasaṃbhūterabhidhyādyāstrimūlajāḥ /
kuśalāḥ saprayogāntā alobhadveṣamohajāḥ // 4.69 //
```

> Because they arise immediately after them, covetousness and the other
> mental courses are born from the three roots. The wholesome courses,
> together with their preparation and aftermath, are born from non-greed,
> non-hatred, and non-delusion.

The first half completes the preceding inquiry into the three unwholesome
roots. The second applies the three-phase form of VAK 4.68 to wholesome
courses and explains their genesis from a wholesome mind.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

How are covetousness and the other mental courses born from greed?
“Because they arise immediately after them, covetousness and the other
mental courses are born from the three roots.” What arises immediately
after greed is called greed-born; what arises immediately after hatred is
called hatred-born; and what arises immediately after delusion is called
delusion-born.

The unwholesome courses of karma have been explained. How are the wholesome
ones constituted? The verse says: “The wholesome courses, together with
their preparation and aftermath, are born from non-greed, non-hatred, and
non-delusion.” Wholesome courses of karma, including preparation and
aftermath, arise from non-greed, non-hatred, and non-delusion because they
arise from a wholesome mind, and such a mind is necessarily associated
with non-greed and the other wholesome roots.

In this analysis, abstention from preparation for an unwholesome course is
preparation for the wholesome course. Abstention from the principal
unwholesome course is the principal wholesome course. Abstention from its
aftermath is the wholesome aftermath.

For example, when a novice is being admitted to full ordination, entering
the residence, venerating the monks, requesting a preceptor, and proceeding
through the first and second formal proclamations constitute preparation.
At the third formal proclamation, the manifest action and the non-manifest
action arising in that moment constitute the principal course. Afterward,
while the supports are announced, their observance is made known, and the
non-manifest restraint continues, this constitutes the aftermath. The
exact syntax of the final procedural details is compressed in the witness,
but the allocation of the three phases is clear.

## 4. Root, Mind, and Determinate Course

The first half distinguishes an unwholesome root from the determinate
mental course that follows it:

```text
lobha  → immediately subsequent mental course → greed-born
dveṣa  → immediately subsequent mental course → hatred-born
moha   → immediately subsequent mental course → delusion-born
```

`Abhidhyā`, `vyāpāda`, and `mithyādṛṣṭi` are therefore not simply synonyms
for greed, hatred, and delusion. They are determinate mental courses whose
genesis can be classified through the root immediately preceding them. The
next verse will distinguish this broad genesis from the root by which each
course reaches completion.

The wholesome side supplies a different but complementary relation:

```text
alobha + adveṣa + amoha
    necessarily associated with wholesome mind
        ↓
wholesome preparation → principal course → aftermath
```

Here the wholesome roots do not merely label an act retrospectively. They
belong to the consciousness from which the entire course arises. The
Bhāṣya thereby distinguishes behavioral non-performance from a positively
generated wholesome restraint.

## 5. Abstention as a Complete Course

`Virati`, abstention, is articulated across all three phases:

```text
abstention from unwholesome preparation
    = wholesome preparation

abstention from principal wrongdoing
    = principal wholesome course

abstention from its continuation
    = wholesome aftermath
```

This is stronger than the bare absence of an event. The ordination example
shows the positive practical form: the agent enters a procedure, requests
admission, passes through its formal acts, acquires restraint at the
decisive proclamation, and continues under that restraint afterward.

The example also joins VAK 4.69 to the modal distinction of 4.67. The
principal wholesome course possesses both `vijñapti` and `avijñapti`: a
formal act makes the undertaking manifest, while the acquired restraint
continues as a non-manifest determination.

## 6. Organon Contact Point

The governing Power of a wholesome course is now located in its complete
genesis:

```text
Wholesome Roots
    → Wholesome Mind
    → Preparation
    → Principal Restraint
    → Persistent Aftermath
```

For our Rational Moral Agent, this prevents a behavior-only judgment. The
same visible omission can arise from inability, fear, calculation, or an
acquired wholesome determination. The course is wholesome when its phases
are generated and governed by the appropriate roots through a wholesome
mind.

This also advances the movement from `karmapatha` toward `Mārga`. A single
abstention becomes path-like when its ground governs preparation, decisive
undertaking, and continued conduct as one intelligible course. This is the
Organon reconstruction; Vasubandhu's immediate subject is the Abhidharma
classification of wholesome courses and ordination restraint.
