# VAK 4.46 Bhāṣya — Immovable Karma and the Level of Maturation

## 1. Kārikā Anchor


## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

First, then: “Wholesome karma in the desire domain is meritorious;
what is produced above is immovable.” The word “wholesome” carries
forward. Wholesome karma belonging to the form and formless domains
is immovable.

But has the Blessed One not called the three meditative absorptions
“moved”? He says, for example, “Whatever here is thinking and
examining, the noble ones call that movement.” That was said with
reference to their being subject to disturbance in concentration.
Yet those absorptions have also been called “immovable” in the
Āniñja Sūtra, with reference to the path leading toward the
immovable. Why, then, is what is called moved in one place called
immovable elsewhere?

“Because karma does not shift from those levels with respect to
maturation.” Desire-domain karma does move with respect to
maturation. How? Because its placement is not fixed. Karma associated
with one destiny can ripen in another; karma associated with one
class of gods can ripen in another divine class. The same karma
capable of producing features such as measure, strength, complexion,
activity, happiness, and enjoyment, which ripens among gods, may
sometimes, through other conditions, ripen instead among humans,
animals, or pretas. The compact Sanskrit for this list and its
qualification by `anyabhūmika` are uncertain in the running
e-text.

Karma belonging to the form and formless domains, however, is never
able to ripen in a level other than its own. It is therefore called
immovable because its maturation has a fixed placement. As for
demeritorious karma, it is commonly known in the world to be
unwholesome karma. Why labour over a meaning already established by
ordinary usage? The exposition of meritorious karma and the other
classes is complete. The classes beginning with karma to be
experienced as pleasure remain to be explained. That explanation
now follows.

## 4. Where the Apparent Contradiction Dissolves

The objection is real: the cited teaching calls meditative states
`señjita`, while another calls them `āniñja`. The Bhāṣya answers by
specifying two different respects of predication:

| Predication | Respect specified by the Bhāṣya |
|---|---|
| The absorptions are moved | Disturbance or impurity within `samādhi` |
| Higher wholesome karma is immovable | Its `vipāka` does not shift to a different `bhūmi` |

The partial quotation about thinking and examining is introduced as
one example (`ity evamādi`). It does not say that all three
absorptions contain thinking and examining in the same way. Nor does
“immovable” mean unconditioned or changeless in every respect.

## 5. The Threefold Classification Completed

The `śubham` of the first half is explicitly carried into the
second: `rūpārūpyāvacaraṃ kuśalaṃ karmāneñjam`. The resulting
classification is:

| Class | Determination in this unit |
|---|---|
| `puṇya` | Wholesome karma of the desire domain |
| `apuṇya` | Unwholesome karma, treated as familiar usage |
| `āneñja` | Wholesome karma of the form and formless domains, fixed as to its maturation level |

`Avyavasthāna` explains the comparative mobility of desire-domain
results; `anyapratyayavaśāt` preserves the role of other conditions.
`Vyavasthitavipākatva` is the positive ground for calling higher
karma immovable. The contrast concerns the **level of maturation**,
not a claim that desire-domain karma produces arbitrary results or
that higher-domain karma has ceased to be conditioned.

The last two sentences make the section boundary explicit: the
`puṇya` triad is finished, and the three classes of felt result
announced in 4.45 are next.

## 6. Organon Contact Point

For our study of OM Artha as a platform of determination and
appearance, this passage supplies a disciplined question: which
features of a future result are fixed by an operative determination,
and which remain sensitive to other conditions? The Kośa's answer
here is specific to karma and `bhūmi`. Our broader account of
knowledge, scheduling, and appearing truth is a project comparison,
not a translation of `āneñja` into a general generator of reality.
