# VAK 4.28 Bhāṣya — Receiving the Eight-Limbed Upavāsa

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
kālyaṃ grāhyo 'nyato nīcaiḥ sthitenoktānuvāditā /
upavāsaḥ samagrāṅgo nirbhūṣeṇāniśākṣayāt // 4.28 //
```

> The upavāsa is to be received at the proper morning time, from
> another, by one standing lower and repeating what has been said.
> It is to be received with all its limbs, without exceptional
> adornment, until the following sunrise.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

How is an upavāsa received when it is undertaken for a day and a
night? It is to be received at the proper time, from another, by a
person positioned lower and repeating what has been spoken. It is
to be complete in its limbs, received without adornment, and to
last until the night has ended.

First, the proper time is sunrise, since the restraint extends over
a day and night. Someone who has already made the undertaking “I
will always observe the eight” may, however, receive it even after
eating. It is to be received from another, not simply by oneself
without regard to another, in order to guard against transgression
when occasions arise.

One should be positioned lower, crouch, or kneel, making the
prescribed joined-hands gesture before the other person. Restraint
does not arise in one who lacks respect. One should repeat the
giver's words, neither before nor simultaneously with them. In
that sequence the restraint is received from another; otherwise
giving and receiving would not be established. It must be received
with all eight limbs, not with some limbs missing.

One should receive it without adornment, apart from habitual
adornment, which does not strongly produce vanity. It should be
received until the night is completed, that is, until the next
sunrise. Otherwise one might have merely good conduct, not the
upavāsa restraint. This also allows fruitfulness to be attributed
to the night or day fasts of the sheep butcher and the adulterer
mentioned here, without making them the complete upavāsa restraint.

It is called `upavāsa` because through it one lives near the Arhats
by following their training. Others say that through it one lives
near lifelong restraint. It is also called `poṣadha` because it
nourishes the wholesome roots of those whose wholesome roots are
small. A cited verse says that it bears nourishment for the
wholesome mind and is therefore called `poṣadha` by the Blessed One.

## 4. The Conditions of Reception

The Kārikā compresses an enacted procedure. The Bhāṣya specifies
both an ordinary rule and a qualification:

| Condition | Bhāṣya determination |
|---|---|
| Time | Ordinarily at sunrise; a prior standing undertaking permits reception even after eating |
| Source | From another person, to support non-transgression |
| Posture | Lowered, crouched, or kneeling with joined hands; respect is required |
| Speech | Repeat **after** the giver, neither before nor simultaneously |
| Content | All eight limbs, not a partial observance |
| Adornment | Habitual adornment is excepted; adornment that strongly promotes vanity is restricted |
| Terminus | Through the night until the next sunrise |

The speech sequence establishes a real giving-and-receiving
relation. Mere duplication of words at the same time would not
constitute reception from another in the sense specified here.
`Agaurava`, lack of respect, is also explicitly said to prevent
restraint from arising; the bodily form matters because it enacts
that disposition.

## 5. Good Conduct and a Constituted Restraint

The consequence of deviating from the stated procedure is carefully
limited: one may have `sucaritamātra`, **mere good conduct**, while
not possessing this technically constituted upavāsa restraint.
The Bhāṣya does not say such conduct is worthless. Its example of
partial night and day fasts assigns them fruitfulness while keeping
them distinct from the complete observance.

The two explanations of `upavāsa` locate its aim: temporary
nearness to the Arhats' training, or to lifelong restraint. The
name `poṣadha` adds the nourishment of weak wholesome roots. The
procedure therefore has a formative end as well as exact
conditions of acquisition.

## 6. Organon Contact Point

For our Method, the passage distinguishes a good action from a
practical form that has a definite genesis, content, reception,
and duration. In our own vow work, we can ask what makes a
commitment actually received and operative, rather than treating
the declaration or outward conformity as sufficient. The
giver–recipient sequence is a concrete case of mediation, and the
etymologies make the formative end visible. This is an Organon
question raised through the Kośa; it does not import the monastic
procedure into our Pure Reason vows.
