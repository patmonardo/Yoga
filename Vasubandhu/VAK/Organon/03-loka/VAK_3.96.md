# VAK_3.96

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

> एकद्वित्रिचतुर्द्वीपा न च द्वौ सह बुद्धवत् ।
>
> प्रत्युद्यानस्वयंयानकलहास्त्रजितोऽवधाः ॥ ३.९६ ॥

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

> eka-dvi-tri-catur-dvīpā na ca dvau saha buddhavat /
>
> pratyudyāna-svayaṃyāna-kalahāstrajito 'vadhāḥ // 3.96 //

The final word is textually decisive. The Bhāṣya glosses `avadhāḥ` through
`vadho na pravartate`—killing does not occur—even for the ruler who conquers
by weapons. It must therefore be read as **without killing**, not as
“boundaries” or “territorial limits.”

## 3. Lexical Analysis

```text
eka-dvi-tri-catur-dvīpāḥ     → ekadvitricaturdvīpā
na                           → na
ca                           → ca
dvau                         → dvau
saha                         → saha
buddha-vat                   → buddhavat
pratyudyāna                  → pratyudyāna
svayam-yāna                  → svayaṃyāna
kalaha                       → kalaha
astra-jitaḥ                  → astrajito (before a-)
avadhāḥ                      → 'vadhāḥ (after avagraha)
```

| Form | Morphology | Lexical force here |
|---|---|---|
| eka-dvi-tri-catur-dvīpāḥ | nominative masculine plural distributive compound | ruling respectively one, two, three, and four continents |
| na ca dvau saha | negative clause with nominative dual | two do not occur simultaneously |
| buddha-vat | indeclinable comparative in `-vat` | as in the case of Buddhas |
| pratyudyāna | compound member | subjects or subordinate kings coming forth to receive the ruler |
| svayaṃ-yāna | compound member | the ruler's going personally to those to be subdued |
| kalaha | compound member | quarrel or conflict preceding submission |
| astra-jitaḥ | participial form closing the coordinated series | conquered through weapons; the transmitted inflection is syntactically compressed |
| avadha | nominative masculine plural adjective | without killing; non-homicidal |

The first compound distributes four territorial scopes across the four
cakravartins established in 3.95. The second series distributes four modes of
conquest across the same ordered class. The Bhāṣya, not the compressed surface
alone, fixes both mappings.

## 4. Grammar

The first statement supplies territorial scope:

```text
[te cakravartinaḥ]
    eka-dvi-tri-catur-dvīpāḥ [yathākramam]
```

> Those cakravartins [rule], respectively, one, two, three, and four
> continents.

The wheel-to-range mapping is:

```text
iron   → one continent
copper → two continents
silver → three continents
gold   → four continents
```

The singularity clause follows:

```text
na ca dvau [cakravartinau utpadyete] saha
    buddhavat
```

> Nor do two arise together, as [two do not arise together] in the case of
> Buddhas.

The omitted verb is recovered from the preceding discussion of arising. The
scope of `buddhavat` generates a Bhāṣya debate: whether non-co-presence means
within one world-system or throughout all world-systems.

The second line gives method and limit:

```text
[te yathākramam]
    pratyudyāna-
    svayaṃyāna-
    kalaha-
    astra-jitaḥ

[sarve]
    avadhāḥ
```

> Respectively, they conquer through reception, personal approach, conflict,
> and weapons; [yet] all are without killing.

`Avadhāḥ` predicates one ethical constraint of the entire plural subject; it
is not a fifth conquest mode.

## 5. Translation

### Close syntactic construe

> They [rule] one, two, three, and four continents respectively; and two do
> not [arise] together, as with Buddhas. They conquer respectively through
> reception, personal approach, conflict, and weapons; they are without
> killing.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> The iron-, copper-, silver-, and gold-wheel sovereigns rule respectively
> one, two, three, and four continents. Two cakravartins do not arise
> simultaneously in the same World-field, just as two Buddhas do not. The
> gold-wheel ruler is voluntarily received; the silver-wheel ruler goes to
> the subordinate kings, who then submit; the copper-wheel ruler meets them
> in conflict before their submission; and the iron-wheel ruler encounters
> armed resistance. Nevertheless, none of the four kills. After victory they
> establish beings in the ten wholesome courses of action.

The exact wheel mappings, same-world qualification, absence of killing, and
subsequent moral establishment are Bhāṣya determinations. The commentary also
records disagreement over whether Buddhas may arise simultaneously in
different world-systems.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> Sovereign power is determined through four coordinates: its rank, its
> spatial range, its mode of overcoming resistance, and its internal ethical
> limit. As rank descends, territorial scope contracts and coercion increases;
> yet even the armed form remains bounded by non-killing. The World-order does
> not define power merely by what it can overcome. It completes the
> determination by specifying what power may not do and the wholesome order
> it must institute after victory.

| svaka-karma | one's own karma | principle governing the arising of sentient royal associates |

The Bhāṣya cites seven royal treasures: wheel, elephant, horse, jewel, woman,
householder, and counselor. Its causal clarification is strict: sentient
treasures do not arise through the cakravartin's karma. Each arises through
its own karma capable of producing that association.

## 8. Logical Determination

### Complete sovereignty matrix

| Wheel | Rank | Territorial scope | Mode of conquest |
|---|---|---:|---|
| gold | highest | four continents | others come forth voluntarily |
| silver | next-highest | three continents | ruler approaches; others submit |
| copper | middling | two continents | conflict, then submission |
| iron | lowest | one continent | armed encounter, then submission |

The classification has the nested form:

```text
rank
    → territorial range
        → mode of establishing rule
            → universal ethical limit: no killing
                → positive result: ten wholesome karmapathas
```

The limit is internal to the determination of cakravartin power:

```text
WeaponConquest(x)
    ∧ Cakravartin(x)
        → NonKilling(x)
```

Armed force and killing are therefore not treated as synonymous operations.
The lowest mode permits the former while excluding the latter.

### Functional singularity

```text
one relevant world-system
    → at most one Buddha functioning as world-guide
    → at most one cakravartin functioning as world-sovereign
```

The Bhāṣya does not silently universalize this rule. It preserves a dispute:
one position excludes Buddhas in other world-systems, while the
`Nikāyāntarīya` position accepts simultaneous Buddhas elsewhere. Therefore:

```text
singularity within one World-system
    ≠ demonstrated uniqueness across every World-system
```

### Causal ownership within coordinated appearance

```text
association with cakravartin
    → conjunction of independently accumulated karmic continuities
    ≠ one agent producing another agent through its karma
```

The royal assemblage is coordinated without collapsing causal ownership.

## 9. Interpretive Note

The methodological abstraction of 3.96 is: **a power is incompletely
specified until its range, operation, and limit are jointly determined.**

```text
Power = <rank, range, method, limit, result>
```

This greatly sharpens the agential reading of the so-called Physical World.
The four continents are not merely pieces of mythic geography; in this verse
they become the typed spatial range of an operation. The modes of conquest
then state how that operation crosses resistance, while `avadha` places a
constraint over every branch of the system.

```text
spatial enclosure
    → where the operation has jurisdiction

temporal enclosure
    → when the operation can appear

ethical enclosure
    → which transitions the operation may perform
```

This is a further determination of the Mind of the Enclosure. A complete
World-system does not merely contain agents and events; it types their scope
and constrains their permissible transformations. Here the constraint is
especially strong because it survives the transition from peaceful reception
all the way to armed confrontation.

The comparison with the Buddha remains functional, not identificatory. Both
are singular World-operations within the relevant field, but one institutes
Dharma through teaching and the other institutes worldly order through
sovereignty.
