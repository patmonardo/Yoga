# VAK_3.58 Bhāṣya — Avīci and the Eight Great Hells

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
adhaḥ sahasrair viṃśatyā tanmātro 'vīcir asya hi /
tadūrdhvaṃ sapta narakāḥ sarve 'ṣṭau ṣoḍaśotsadāḥ // 3.58 //
```

> Twenty thousand yojanas beneath this Jambūdvīpa lies Avīci, itself twenty
> thousand yojanas in vertical extent and breadth. Above it are seven hells;
> all eight have sixteen subsidiary regions.

The Bhāṣya resolves both pronouns and measures, explains the name Avīci,
distinguishes two kinds of pleasant feeling while evaluating an alternative
etymology, preserves two topologies for the seven other hells, and cites the
Buddha on the common structure of the eight great hells.

## 2. Continuous Sanskrit

```iast
Narakāḥ kasminn avakāśe, kiyatpramāṇāś ca?

Adhaḥ sahasrair viṃśatyā tanmātro 'vīcir asya hi.

Asyaiva Jambūdvīpasyādho viṃśatyā yojanasahasrair Avīcir mahānarako,
viṃśatisahasrapramāṇa evodvedhavistārābhyām. Evam asyādharatalam itaś
catvāriṃśatā yojanasahasrair bhavati.

Duḥkhanirantaratvād Avīciḥ. Anyeṣu sāntaraṃ duḥkham. Tad yathā Saṃjīve
chinnāvabhinnasaṃpiṣṭaśarīrāṇāṃ śītalā vāyavo vānti, punar api tān
sattvān saṃjīvayanti. Ata eva Saṃjīvaḥ.

Nāsmin sukhavīcir astīty Avīcir ity apare.

Anyeṣv api sukhā vedanā vipāko nāsti. Naiḥṣyandikī na vāryate.

raudrakarmabhir ākīrṇāḥ pratyekaṃ ṣoḍaśotsadāḥ //

catuskandhāś caturdvārā vibhaktā bhāgaśo 'sitāḥ /
ayaḥprākāraparyantā ayasā prativarjitāḥ //
```

The running witness contains mechanical damage in compounds and in the cited
verses, especially around `catuskandhā`, `asitāḥ`, and the closing flame
description. The continuous text regularizes evident spacing and sandhi but
does not silently reconstruct a critical sūtra text.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Where are the hells located, and how large are they?

Twenty thousand yojanas beneath this Jambūdvīpa lies Avīci, itself of that
same measure. Directly beneath this Jambūdvīpa, at a distance of twenty
thousand yojanas, lies the great hell Avīci. It measures twenty thousand
yojanas in both vertical extent and breadth. Thus its lower floor lies forty
thousand yojanas beneath this surface.

It is called Avīci because suffering there is without interval. In the other
hells suffering has an interval. For example, in Saṃjīva, when beings' bodies
have been cut, split, and crushed, cool winds blow and cause them to revive
again. For precisely this reason it is called Saṃjīva, “Reviving.”

Others say that it is called Avīci because there is no interval of pleasure
there. But pleasant feeling as karmic maturation is absent in the other hells
as well; pleasant feeling belonging to homogeneous outflow is not thereby
ruled out.

Above Avīci are seven hells. On the principal account, the seven are arranged
successively one above another: Pratāpana, Tāpana, Mahāraurava, Raurava,
Saṃghāta, Kālasūtra, and Saṃjīva. Others say that they are situated at the
sides of Avīci. All eight have sixteen subsidiary regions each.

For the Blessed One has said: “These eight hells have been declared difficult
to cross, filled through cruel actions, each possessing sixteen subsidiary
regions. They have four enclosing divisions and four gates, are divided into
sections, and are bounded and covered by iron. Their ground too is heated
iron, blazing and filled with fire; extending for many hundreds of yojanas,
it stands filled with flame.”

The translation of the cited verse is necessarily provisional where the
working Sanskrit is damaged. Its secure force is the common four-gated,
iron-bounded, blazing structure and the sixteen subsidiary regions of each
great hell.

## 4. Natural Movement and Voices

The unit proceeds through a question and four determinations:

```text
where and how large?
    → Avīci's location, dimensions, and lower floor

why “Avīci”?
    → principal: suffering without interval
    → alternative: no interval of pleasure
    → reply: distinguish vipāka from naiḥṣyandika feeling

where are the seven other hells?
    → principal: successively above Avīci
    → alternative: at Avīci's sides

what structure do all eight share?
    → sixteen subsidiary regions each
    → scriptural description of gates, iron enclosure, and blazing ground
```

Vasubandhu gives the opening question, primary explanations, and technical
reply. Two separate `ity apare` statements preserve alternative voices: one
for the name Avīci, another for the spatial arrangement of the seven hells.
The closing stanzas are explicitly cited as the words of the Blessed One.

## 5. Avīci's Address and Dimensions

The Bhāṣya resolves the compressed reference structure:

```text
asya
    → this very Jambūdvīpa

tanmātra
    → Avīci has the same 20,000-yojana measure
      in vertical extent and breadth
```

The resulting lower-world address is:

```text
Jambūdvīpa surface reference       0
Avīci upper boundary         −20,000 yojanas
Avīci vertical extent         20,000 yojanas
Avīci lower floor            −40,000 yojanas
```

The forty-thousand depth of the lower floor is derived from location plus
internal extent. It is not an independent third measurement.

`Udvedha` and `vistāra` must remain distinct: the first is vertical extent,
the second breadth. `Tanmātra` transfers one numerical value to both axes; it
does not say that Avīci has only one dimension.

## 6. Avīci, Saṃjīva, and the Structure of Feeling

The principal explanation of `Avīci` is temporal-experiential:

```text
Avīci
    → duḥkha without interval

other hells
    → duḥkha can be interrupted
```

Saṃjīva supplies the counterexample. After bodies are cut, split, and crushed,
cool winds revive the beings. The interval does not release them from the
hell; it enables renewed suffering and explains the name “Reviving.”

The alternative etymology shifts from uninterrupted suffering to absence of
a pleasure-interval. Vasubandhu's reply tests whether that feature is
distinctive:

```text
pleasant feeling as vipāka
    → absent in the other hells too

pleasant feeling as naiḥṣyandika
    → not excluded
```

`Vipāka` and `naiḥṣyandika` are causal classifications, not simply greater
and lesser degrees of pleasure. The reply therefore shows why the alternative
definition needs technical qualification: absence of pleasant maturation
does not uniquely distinguish Avīci.

## 7. The Seven Other Hells and Two Topologies

The principal account places the seven successively above Avīci. From lowest
to highest, the resulting order is:

```text
Avīci
Pratāpana
Tāpana
Mahāraurava
Raurava
Saṃghāta
Kālasūtra
Saṃjīva
```

The source lists the seven from Pratāpana upward through Saṃjīva; reversing
that list would describe a descent from the uppermost hell and should be
labeled accordingly.

The alternative account places them at Avīci's sides. The disagreement is
topological, not an alternative inventory:

```text
shared entities
    Avīci + seven named great hells

principal relation
    seven successively above

alternative relation
    seven beside Avīci
```

A faithful representation must preserve the common inventory while attaching
each arrangement to its own authority.

## 8. Sixteen Subsidiary Regions and the Citation

Each of the eight great hells is `ṣoḍaśotsada`, furnished with sixteen
`utsadas` or subsidiary painful regions:

```text
8 principal hells
    × 16 subsidiary regions each
        = 128 attachment-relations
```

This arithmetic describes assignments, not necessarily 128 absolutely unique
types. VAK 3.59 will identify the recurring kinds and explain how four at each
of four gates yield sixteen for every great hell.

The cited verses already establish several common features:

- the eight are difficult to cross;
- cruel karmas fill them;
- each has sixteen subsidiary regions;
- the structure has four gates and iron enclosure;
- its heated iron ground burns across hundreds of yojanas.

Because the cited Sanskrit is damaged, uncertain architectural words should
not be over-normalized. The citation's broader structure is secure enough to
show that the hells are karmically populated, spatially bounded, and
systematically subdivided.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

VAK 3.58 makes the lower world simultaneously spatial, experiential, and
karmic:

```text
address and extent
    + temporal regime of feeling
    + causal class of feeling
    + regional topology
    + karmic population condition
        → determinate hell-domain
```

For the Organon Dataset, it is especially important not to encode “Avīci” as
only a place-name or only a moral symbol. Its doctrinal identity crosses
several axes: location, measure, suffering-continuity, relation to neighboring
hells, and subsidiary structure.

The etymological dispute demonstrates a classification test. A proposed
feature must not merely apply; it must discriminate the named domain from
relevant alternatives. When “no pleasant maturation” applies to other hells
as well, the account fails as a unique determination unless further causal
distinctions are introduced.

This is an Organon reconstruction of the source's method, not terminology
attributed to Vasubandhu. It also clarifies the relationship between Loka and
Karma: the present chapter specifies result-domains and cites cruel actions
as their filling condition, while the systematic analysis of karmic acts and
maturation belongs to the later Karma chapter.

The passage should not be converted into a modern physical map or used to
erase its ethical and experiential determination. Its own model is a
conditioned world in which place, embodiment, feeling, and karma are internally
connected.

## 10. Review Status

This Study restores the complete VAK 3.58 unit at 162.28–163.18: Avīci's
reference to Jambūdvīpa, its location and dimensions, the principal and
alternative explanations of its name, the `vipāka`/`naiḥṣyandika`
distinction, the seven named hells, competing vertical and lateral
arrangements, sixteen subsidiary regions for each, and the complete cited
scriptural passage.

The Sanskrit and translation remain provisional. The damaged words in the
scriptural citation, the best rendering of `vīci`, the precise causal scope
of `naiḥṣyandika` feeling, and the spatial force of the alternative lateral
arrangement require comparison with critical Sanskrit, Tibetan, Chinese, and
established translations.

The Study stops before `ṣoḍaśotsadāḥ katame?`, the question opening the
detailed VAK 3.59 discussion. It does not identify the four `utsada` types,
enter the hell-guard controversy, or begin the cold-hell list.
