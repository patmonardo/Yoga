# VAK_3.28 Bhāṣya — Causal Relation and Ignorance as an Operative Contrary

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> hetur atra samutpādaḥ samutpannaṃ phalaṃ matam /
> vidyāvipakṣo dharmo 'nyo 'vidyāmitrānṛtādivat // 3.28 //

> Here dependent arising is cause, while the dependently arisen is regarded
> as result. Ignorance is a distinct dharma opposed to knowledge, as an enemy
> is opposed to a friend, untruth to truth, and so forth.

The two halves of the verse emerge within a long controversy. The first
distinguishes cause-role from result-role; the second rejects both “anything
that is not knowledge” and “mere absence of knowledge” as definitions of
ignorance. Between them, the Bhāṣya debates the status, wording, scope, and
causal meaning of dependent arising itself.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Another sūtra says that ignorance has unwise attention as its cause, and that
unwise attention in turn has ignorance as its cause. Some say that unwise
attention is already stated here because it is included within appropriation.
But how is it included? If by association, it would equally be included in
craving and ignorance. Even if inclusion were granted, that would not show
here that unwise attention causes ignorance. And if inclusion alone disclosed
cause and result, craving and ignorance could likewise be denied separate
member-status wherever they were included together.

Another says that unwise attention, taught elsewhere as ignorance's cause, is
located at the time of contact: “Depending upon the eye and forms, a turbid
attention born of delusion arises.” Ignorance must also occur at the time of
feeling, since another sūtra says that craving arises depending upon feeling
born of contact with ignorance. Thus unwise attention at contact can condition
the ignorance accompanying feeling. Ignorance is not causeless, no additional
member is needed, and no regress follows, because that unwise attention is
itself said to be born of delusion.

An objection says this causal point, stated at the end, should have been
stated here. The reply says it can be inferred by reasoning: faultless feeling
does not condition craving in arhats, undistorted contact does not condition
afflicted feeling, and an arhat's faultless contact is not distorted. But the
opponent answers that allowing every unstated point licensed by possible
reasoning would overextend the text. This reply therefore fails.

The original endpoint objection is itself misplaced. The teaching is not
incomplete merely because it does not state another member beyond ignorance
or aging-and-death. Its intended subject for trainees confused about the
process is how this life connects with another and another with this life.
That purpose has already been stated: removal of confusion concerning the
prior limit, subsequent limit, and middle.

The Blessed One said, “Monks, I shall teach you dependent arising and the
dependently arisen dharmas.” What distinguishes them? From the standpoint of
the treatise, initially nothing: both expressions range over all conditioned
dharmas. A question follows: how can future dharmas, not yet arisen, be called
dependently arisen? The reply compares their being called conditioned before
actual production: they have been intended by formative volition. Even future
untainted dharmas have been intended with respect to acquisition by wholesome
volition. The threatened extension to nirvāṇa is blocked by designation
according to kind, as something not yet visibly formed may nevertheless be
called form because it belongs to that kind.

The sūtra's intention, however, is this: “Here dependent arising is cause,
while the dependently arisen is regarded as result.” A member functioning as
cause is called dependent arising because another arises from it; a member
functioning as result is called dependently arisen. Every member can be
established in both ways because it is result in one relation and cause in
another. No disorder follows, since the reference differs—just as one person
is son relative to a father and father relative to a son.

The elder Pūrṇāśa is reported to have offered a four-cornered classification:
future dharmas are dependent arising but not dependently arisen; an arhat's
final dharmas are dependently arisen but not dependent arising; other past
and present dharmas are both; unconditioned dharmas are neither.

The Sautrāntikas object that these are school theses rather than the meaning
of the sūtra. They reject the stage-based interpretation of twelve five-
aggregate states because the sūtra defines ignorance itself as non-knowledge
concerning the past and so forth. A text whose meaning is explicit should not
be reinterpreted.

The reply says not every explicit-looking statement is definitive and that
teachings may name a complex by what predominates. The elephant-footprint
sūtra, for example, explains earth-element through hair and body-hair even
though other material qualities are present there. The Sautrāntika rejoinder
is that this is a collection of examples of earth-element, not the naming of
hair as a five-aggregate stage; the definition is complete rather than
residual. Even other bodily earth-element is covered by the phrase concerning
whatever else is hard and solid. If an unmentioned remainder of ignorance can
be shown, let it be shown—but why insert dharmas of another kind into
ignorance?

Although all five aggregates occur in the proposed stages, the member should
be fixed by the factor whose presence and absence govern the relevant effect.
An arhat has five aggregates, yet formations productive of renewed existence
do not arise from the five aggregates as such; they arise from ignorance.
Likewise cognition destined toward meritorious, nonmeritorious, or
imperturbable results, and craving and the rest, depend upon their specifically
stated conditions. The sūtra means what its definitions say.

The Sautrāntikas also reject the simple cause/result gloss and the fourfold
classification as extra-scriptural. The sūtra defines dependent arising as
the lawfulness, stability, and non-deceptiveness expressed by “when this
exists, that exists.” This `dharmatā` is the regular manner of dharmas: only
when ignorance exists do formations occur, not otherwise. Dependent arising
is this necessity, not merely the cause considered alone. They also argue
that excluding future dharmas from the dependently arisen conflicts with the
sūtra's inclusion of all members through aging-and-death and disrupts the
three-time arrangement.

Another school holds dependent arising to be unconditioned, citing the claim
that this law remains whether Tathāgatas arise or do not arise. Vasubandhu
accepts and rejects this according to the intended sense. It is acceptable if
it means that formations invariably arise in dependence upon ignorance and
never without conditions or from some unrelated condition, whether Buddhas
appear or not. It must be rejected if it posits an eternal entity called
dependent arising. Arising is a characteristic of the conditioned; an eternal
entity cannot be the characteristic of what is impermanent, nor could such a
separate entity be properly related to ignorance and the other members.

The text then analyzes the expression. `Pratītya` is explained as “having
reached or depended upon,” and `samutpāda` as manifestation or arising:
arising upon reaching a condition. A grammatical objection says the absolutive
normally gives an earlier action by the same agent—“having bathed, he eats.”
But before arising no entity exists that could first depend and later arise,
while an action without an agent also seems impossible.

The reply turns the objection back upon the grammarian. Does a present or a
future dharma arise? If present, it would already have arisen, and repeated
arising produces regress. If future, the same objection about a nonexistent
agent returns. Whatever status the dharma has when it arises is the status in
which it depends: a future dharma oriented toward arising is said in that very
state to depend upon its condition. Moreover, the grammatical division into
agent and action is itself not finally established; in “it becomes,” no act
of becoming distinct from the becoming thing is found. Conventional usage
should not be manipulated into a trick.

The sentence means: when this exists, that exists; from the arising of this,
that arises. Verses add that an absolutive can also occur with simultaneous
relations, as darkness goes upon obtaining a lamp or someone sleeps with the
mouth opened. Others propose a different etymology involving distributive
conditions and unstable things arising through assemblages of causes, but the
proposal does not fit formulations such as “depending on eye and forms,
eye-consciousness arises.”

Why does the Buddha state both “when this exists, that exists” and “from the
arising of this, that arises”? Suggested answers include: emphasis; showing
the succession of members; showing succession across births; and displaying
both immediate and mediate conditionality, since formations may follow
ignorance immediately or through an interval. Others say the pair rejects
both causeless arising and production from an eternal cause such as primordial
nature or a person. An objection notes that the second formula alone would
already reject both.

The reply points to theorists who posit an ātman as continuing support while
formations and the other members exist, though those members arise from
ignorance and the rest. The double formula excludes this: whatever arises
from a given condition exists when that very condition exists, not when some
other supposed substrate exists.

Teachers offer further explanations. One says the pair shows non-abandonment
and arising: while ignorance is not abandoned, formations are not abandoned;
from its arising, they arise. Others say it displays continuity and arising:
the stream of effect lasts as long as the stream of cause, and the effect
arises from the cause's arising. This latter reading is challenged as poorly
ordered and as importing cessation into a passage explicitly explaining
arising; it is therefore rejected.

Vasubandhu then gives the association of the members. An ordinary person,
failing to know that this is merely dependently arisen formation, adheres to
self-view and the conceit “I am,” and undertakes bodily, verbal, and mental
karma for personal pleasure or avoidance of pain: meritorious karma for
future pleasure, imperturbable karma for refined neutral or pleasant result,
and nonmeritorious karma for pleasure in this life. These are formations
conditioned by ignorance. Through their projective power, the consciousness-
continuum proceeds to its destiny through intermediate existence, like the
movement of a flame.

Consciousness is conditioned by formations other than itself; this permits
the consciousness-member to be specified as the six classes of consciousness.
Conditioned by consciousness, five-aggregate name-and-form arises throughout
the life in the respective destiny. As name-and-form matures, the six sense
spheres arise in sequence. With objects available, consciousness occurs; the
conjunction of the three is contact capable of producing pleasure and the
other feelings; from it arise the three feelings.

From feeling comes craving: desire-realm craving for one oppressed by pain
and oriented toward pleasant feeling; form-realm craving in relation to
pleasant and neutral feeling; formless-realm craving in relation to neutral
feeling. From craving for agreeable feeling comes appropriation of sensuality,
views, rules and observances, and a doctrine of self. Sensuality is the five
sense-qualities; views are the sixty-two described in the *Brahmajāla*;
rules include abstention from misconduct; observances include dog- and
cow-practices and the disciplines of naked ascetics, Brahmins, Pāśupatas, and
wandering mendicants.

The self-doctrine is either the supposed personal existence, or a doctrine in
which “self” is asserted; others take it as self-view and the conceit “I am.”
Because no self exists, appropriation of a self-doctrine is appropriation of
a mere designation. Scripture says that the uninstructed ordinary person
follows the designation “self,” though no self or what belongs to self is
there. Appropriation is desire-and-passion directed toward these objects.

Conditioned by appropriation, karma productive of renewed existence is
accumulated; this is becoming. Conditioned by becoming, future five-aggregate
birth occurs through the descent of consciousness. With birth, aging-and-
death occurs as taught. Thus arises the whole great aggregate of suffering:
“whole” because devoid of anything belonging to self; “great” because the
mass of suffering is without beginning or end; “arising” means manifestation.
This is the Vaibhāṣika method stated earlier.

What, then, does ignorance mean? If it meant simply whatever is not knowledge,
the eye and other non-cognitive dharmas would also become ignorance. If it
meant the absence of knowledge, it would be nothing at all, which is
unsuitable. Therefore ignorance is “a distinct dharma opposed to knowledge,
as an enemy, untruth, and so forth.”

An enemy is not just anything other than a friend, nor the mere absence of a
friend; it is something standing opposed to a friend. Truth is called `ṛta`,
and a statement opposed to it is untruth. Likewise non-dharma, non-benefit,
and non-duty are adversarial counterparts of dharma, benefit, and duty.
Ignorance should similarly be understood as a distinct dharma standing
opposed to knowledge.

How is this known? Because ignorance is taught as a condition. Moreover, it
is called a fetter, bondage, latent tendency, flood, and yoke in the sūtras.
A mere absence could not function in these ways, nor could the eye and other
neutral non-knowledge factors. Ignorance is therefore a distinct dharma.

## 4. Argumentative Architecture and Voices

This long unit contains several nested disputes rather than one continuous
school monologue:

```text
cause of ignorance and the endpoint objection
    → cause/result distinction in the sūtra
    → Pūrṇāśa's four-cornered classification
    → Sautrāntika critique of the Vaibhāṣika stage-model
    → dispute over lawfulness as conditioned or unconditioned
    → grammatical analysis of pratītyasamutpāda
    → rival readings of the double causal formula
    → renewed exposition of the twelve-member association
    → definition of ignorance as an operative contrary
```

The Sautrāntikas' objections are substantial and are not resolved merely by
repeating the Vaibhāṣika position. `Nikāyāntarīyāḥ`, other-school proponents,
advance the unconditioned-law thesis. Several explanations are assigned to
unnamed “others” or teachers. The final causal exposition explicitly returns
to the Vaibhāṣika method.

## 5. Cause and Result as Relational Roles

The first half of the verse states the sūtra's intended distinction:

```text
a member relative to what arises from it
    → dependent arising / cause

a member relative to what produced it
    → dependently arisen / result
```

The same member may bear both descriptions without disorder because the
relation (`apekṣā`) differs. The father-and-son comparison makes the form
clear: one person can be son in one relation and father in another.

The Sautrāntika critique forces a qualification. Dependent arising is not
exhausted by labeling an entity “cause.” The sūtra also presents the stable
conditional rule: only with ignorance do formations occur, not otherwise.
The causal member and the lawfulness of its conditioning relation must not be
collapsed.

## 6. Stable Law Without an Eternal Entity

The claim that the conditional law remains whether Buddhas appear or not is
accepted in one sense:

```text
formations invariably depend upon ignorance
    and do not arise without conditions
    or from an unrelated condition
```

It is rejected if it means:

```text
an eternal separate entity
    called dependent arising
    stands behind impermanent events
```

The invariance belongs to the conditioned relation, not to an unconditioned
thing. This distinction is central: necessity of dependence does not require
reification of the law as another substance.

## 7. The Double Formula and the Exclusion of a Substrate

The Bhāṣya preserves several explanations of:

```text
when this exists, that exists
from the arising of this, that arises
```

The pair may emphasize determination, distinguish member- or birth-
succession, or include immediate and mediate conditioning. Its sharpest use
in this unit excludes a theory in which an ātman serves as a standing support
while the members arise from their own conditions:

```text
what arises from condition C
    exists when C exists
    not because some other substrate S exists
```

The long grammatical dispute reaches a restrained conclusion: conventional
agent-action grammar cannot dictate an impossible metaphysics of a thing that
first exists, then depends, then arises. The sentence expresses conditioned
occurrence, not the itinerary of an already completed agent.

## 8. Ignorance as an Operative Contrary

The second half of the verse distinguishes three proposals:

```text
avidyā = everything other than vidyā
    rejected as too broad

avidyā = mere absence of vidyā
    rejected as causally and functionally empty

avidyā = a distinct dharma opposed to vidyā
    accepted
```

`Vipakṣa` is determinate opposition. An enemy is not every non-friend and not
the empty absence of friendship; untruth is a statement opposed to truth.
Ignorance likewise functions as a condition and as fetter, bondage, latent
tendency, flood, and yoke. It must therefore be an operative afflictive
determination.

This conclusion does not yet decide whether ignorance is a kind of defective
`prajñā`; that is the distinct question of VAK 3.29.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

VAK 3.28 yields two complementary controls for the theory of projection.
First, cause and result are relational roles, not permanent types. The same
artifact can be an outflow relative to its source and a project relative to
what it subsequently conditions. Second, ignorance is not missing data. It is
an active contrary determination capable of bending attention, action, and
the ensuing causal field.

For an eventual Oculus this implies:

```text
artifact identity
    must be distinguished from causal role

stable rule
    must be distinguished from reified rule-entity

missing value
    must be distinguished from actively contrary classification
```

The Sautrāntika challenge is especially valuable. A useful analytic View must
remain answerable to the source's explicit definitions; “naming by
predominance” cannot become permission to insert heterogeneous content into a
category without demonstrating the governing dependence.

At the comparative level, this Buddhist account shows why avidyā cannot be
repaired through information addition alone. Yet it remains Vasubandhu's
theory of a distinct conditioned affliction opposed to knowledge. Its relation
to Yoga Vidyā and absolute Self-recognition must be argued later rather than
silently identified here.
