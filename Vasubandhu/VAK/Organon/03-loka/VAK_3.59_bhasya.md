# VAK_3.59 Bhāṣya — Subsidiary Hells, Hell Guards, and Karmic World-Production

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
kukūlaṃ kuṇapaṃ cātha kṣuramārgādikaṃ nadī /
teṣāṃ caturdiśaṃ śītā anye 'ṣṭāvarvudādayaḥ // 3.59 //
```

> Embers, putrid mire, then the razor-road group, and a river lie in their four
> directions. There are eight other cold hells, beginning with Arvuda.

The first half of the verse completes the account of the sixteen subsidiary
regions belonging to each of the eight great hot hells. The second half adds
the eight cold hells. The Bhāṣya then closes its account of the hell-containers
and briefly identifies the primary locations of animals and pretas before the
next kārikā turns to the sun and moon.

The running witness is mechanically damaged at several points. This reading
regularizes evident spacing, sandhi, agreement, and such forms as `tīvra`,
`ṣoḍaśāṅgula`, and `taṇḍula`, while retaining `Arvuda`, the form transmitted
by the local source. The readings `anukrāmatām`, `vivartanīvāyubījavat`, and
the second derivation of `utsada` remain provisional; no critical Sanskrit
text has been silently substituted for the witness.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

What are the sixteen subsidiary regions?

Embers, putrid mire, then the razor-road group, and a river lie in the four
directions of those hells.

At each of their gates are four subsidiary regions. The ember field is
knee-deep. When a being places a foot in it, the skin, flesh, and blood
disintegrate; when the foot is raised, the skin, flesh, and blood arise again.

The putrid mire is a mire of excrement. Creatures called *nyaṅkuṭas* live
there. They are entirely white, with black heads and needle-shaped mouths;
they pierce the beings there as far as the bones.

There is a great road covered with razor edges. When beings walk upon it and
set down a foot, their skin, flesh, and blood are cut apart; their bodies are
restored as before. In the sword-leaf forest, sharp swords fall upon the
beings, cutting off their major and minor limbs, while dark, mottled dogs
devour them. The iron śālmalī forest has sharp thorns sixteen fingers long.
As beings climb it, the thorns turn downward and pierce their bodies; as they
descend, the thorns turn upward. Crows with iron beaks repeatedly tear out and
devour their eyes.

These three beginning with the razor road are counted together as one because
they share the common character of an assault by weapons.

The fourth subsidiary region is the river Vaitaraṇī, filled with boiling,
caustic salt water. Men holding swords, lances, and spears prevent the beings
from reaching either bank. Whether they move upward, downward, or across,
they are heated and cooked, like sesame seeds, grains of rice, and the like in
a large water-filled pot set over a fire. The river has arisen around the
great hell like a moat.

These four subsidiary regions are called sixteen because they are repeated
according to the four directions.

They are called *utsadas* because they are places of additional torment: after
beings have fallen into the hells, they are tormented again in these regions.
Others explain the term by saying that, after confinement in hell, beings
sink down in them.

One question gives rise to another: are the hell-guards counted as sentient
beings, or not?

Some say that they are not. How, then, do they act? Their activity is produced
by the karma of sentient beings, like the wind and seeds at the evolution of a
world.

But the Venerable Dharmasubhūti has said:

> Those who are angry, cruel in action, and inclined toward evil,
> and who delight in the suffering of others, are born as Yama's rākṣasas.

This refers to those who, instructed by Yama, cast beings into the hells. They
are called Yama's rākṣasas; it does not refer to the agents that perform the
torments.

Others say that the hell-guards are counted as sentient beings. Where, then,
does the karmic result of their conduct mature? In those very hells. There is
room there for the maturation of the karma of those who commit acts of
immediate retribution; what could prevent there also being room for theirs?

Why are they not burned by the fire? Presumably because karma has set a limit
to the fire, or because the guards are formed from a special kind of element.

These, then, are called the eight great hot hells.

There are eight other cold hells, beginning with Arvuda: Arvuda, Nirarvuda,
Aṭata, Hahava, Huhuva, Utpala, Padma, and Mahāpadma. Their names correspond to
the alterations of body and voice undergone by beings struck by intense cold.

These too lie beneath this very Jambūdvīpa, laterally alongside the great hot
hells.

How can there be room beneath a Jambūdvīpa of this size for Avīci and the
others? The continents are wider below, like heaps of grain. For this same
reason the great ocean becomes deeper by degrees.

Thus all these sixteen hells are produced through the governing efficacy of
the karma of sentient beings. Individual hells, however, are specifically
produced by their respective karmas—by the karma of many beings, of two, or
of one. Their varieties are configured in innumerable ways, and their location
is not fixed: some occur in rivers, mountains, deserts, or other regions, as
well as below the continent.

This completes, for the present, the arrangement of the receptacles of the
hells.

Animals range over land, water, and sky. Their original location is said to be
the great ocean, from which they spread elsewhere.

Yama is king of the pretas. Five hundred yojanas beneath Jambūdvīpa is his
delimited capital, their original location, from which they spread elsewhere.
Some pretas possess great powers and bear splendor like that of the gods. The
rest are as described in the *Preta Avadāna*.

## 4. Natural Movement and Source Voices

The commentary moves through six connected determinations:

1. it derives sixteen subsidiary regions from four types repeated at four
   gates;
2. it classifies three visibly different regions as one type through their
   common function, weapon-assault;
3. it preserves two explanations of the name `utsada`;
4. it opens, but does not simply close, the controversy over whether the
   hell-guards are sentient;
5. it adds and locates the eight cold hells, then distinguishes common from
   particular karmic world-production;
6. it closes the lower-world geography with the root locations of animals and
   pretas.

The voices must remain distinct. `eke` introduces the non-sentient account of
the guards; Bhadanta Dharmasubhūti is cited against it; the reply restricts his
verse to Yama's rākṣasas who cast beings into hell; another `eke` introduces
the sentient account. The final replies concerning karmic maturation and
immunity from fire defend that second account, but the Bhāṣya transmits the
controversy rather than formally announcing a final school verdict.

## 5. Four Types at Four Gates

The arithmetic is structural:

```text
four gates × four subsidiary types = sixteen utsadas
```

The four types are:

| Type | Determination |
|---|---|
| `kukūla` | knee-deep ember field |
| `kuṇapa` | excrement-mire inhabited by piercing creatures |
| `kṣuramārgādika` | razor road, sword-leaf forest, and iron śālmalī forest |
| `Vaitaraṇī` | encircling river of boiling caustic water |

The apparent enumeration exceeds four only if surface names are counted
without attending to the Bhāṣya's classificatory act. Razor road, sword-leaf
forest, and iron śālmalī forest are unified by `śastrābhinipātasāmānya`, their
common character as weapon-assault. The sixteen therefore do not denote
sixteen unrelated kinds. They are four repeatable encounter-types distributed
across four directions.

This sixteen must also be kept distinct from the later sixteen principal
hells: eight hot and eight cold. Each of the eight great hot hells has sixteen
subsidiary regions; the eight cold hells form a second class of principal
hells.

## 6. Hell-Guards: Activity and Sentient Status

The question is exact: are the guards `sattvasaṃkhyāta`, counted among
sentient beings? Their observed activity does not decide the issue.

On the first account, the guards are not sentient. Their apparently purposive
motions are environmental effects brought forth through the sufferers' karma.
The comparison with wind and seeds during cosmic evolution makes karmic
production capable of generating organized activity without requiring a
corresponding sentient agent behind every action-form.

Dharmasubhūti's verse appears to supply a counterexample: cruel beings are
reborn as Yama's rākṣasas. The reply introduces a role distinction. Those
rākṣasas are sentient officers who, under Yama's instruction, throw beings
into hell; they are not thereby identical with the tormenting figures whose
status is disputed.

On the second account, the guards are sentient. Two objections then arise:
where their cruel karma matures, and why the fires do not burn them. The reply
locates their maturation within the hells themselves and offers two possible
explanations of their immunity: karmically bounded fire or constitution from
special elements. These are alternative explanatory possibilities, not a
single demonstrated mechanism.

The discussion thus separates three matters often collapsed into one:

```text
visible action
    ≠ automatically a sentient actor
assigned role
    ≠ identity with every tormenting appearance
presence in an environment
    ≠ identical susceptibility to that environment
```

## 7. The Eight Cold Hells and Their Location

The cold hells are Arvuda, Nirarvuda, Aṭata, Hahava, Huhuva, Utpala, Padma,
and Mahāpadma. The Bhāṣya says that their names correspond to transformations
of body and voice under extreme cold. The series therefore combines names
associated with bodily deformation and utterances elicited by cold; it is not
presented here as an arbitrary list.

They lie below Jambūdvīpa, laterally in relation to the great hot hells. The
objection about insufficient room assumes that the visible width of the
continent continues unchanged below. Vasubandhu rejects that geometrical
assumption: continents broaden downward like heaps of grain, a shape also
used to explain the gradual deepening of the ocean.

## 8. Shared World and Particular Hell

The causal distinction near the end of the unit is decisive:

```text
sattvakarmādhipatya
    → the common sixteen-hell world-order

svaiḥ svaiḥ karmabhiḥ
    → a particular hell-configuration
       for many beings, two beings, or one being
```

The first statement concerns the common receptacle or world-system. The
second concerns a particular result configured through particular karma. A
stable cosmographic classification therefore does not imply that every
experienced hell is spatially one of sixteen invariant public places. The
Bhāṣya expressly allows innumerable particular forms and unfixed locations,
including rivers, mountains, deserts, other regions, and subterranean places.

This is not a reduction of painful experience to private imagination. Both
the shared receptacle and the particular configuration are karmically
produced. The distinction is one of causal scope: collective world-order and
specific resultant domain.

The notices concerning animals and pretas finish this geographic movement.
Both classes have a stated root location and a wider dispersal. Yama is named
here specifically as king of the pretas, while Yama's rākṣasas appeared above
in the separate dispute over hell personnel. Those functions should not be
merged. The qualification that some pretas possess great power and godlike
splendor also prevents `preta` from functioning as a simple visual synonym
for uniform destitution.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The most important analytical gain is the separation of a produced appearance
from a claim about the kind of bearer appearing through it. A tormenting form
can perform an actor-role in the hell-world, yet the role alone does not
establish that it is a sentient being. Conversely, accepting sentient guards
requires further causal explanations of their karmic result and elemental
relation to the environment. The Bhāṣya tests ontology by consequences rather
than merely reading agency from the grammar of a scene.

The same discipline applies to world-production. `Adhipatya` names the
governing efficacy through which the shared world is produced; `sva-sva-
karman` determines particular configurations within that world. In Organon
terms, this offers a useful model for keeping a common domain, an instantiated
environment, and the status of the agents within it as separate analytical
dimensions. This is a project-level reconstruction of the distinction, not
terminology attributed to Vasubandhu.

The passage also gives a precise sense in which an appearance can be
“counterfeit” without being causally ineffective. If the non-sentient account
is accepted, guard-like behavior is a karmically efficacious projection that
does not possess the ontological bearer its appearance suggests. But the text
does not license turning every unpleasant actor, political power, or historical
formation into a literal Māra or hell-guard. Such comparisons belong to a
later comparative inquiry and must preserve the present distinctions between
appearance, agency, sentience, causal production, and experienced result.
