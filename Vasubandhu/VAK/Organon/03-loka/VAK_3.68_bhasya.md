# VAK_3.68 Bhāṣya — Pārijāta, Fragrance, and Sudharmā

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
viṃśatyantaritāny eṣāṃ subhūmīni caturdiśam /
pūrvottare pārijātaḥ sudharmā dakṣiṇāvare // 3.68 //
```

> In the four directions from these gardens are pleasant grounds separated by
> twenty [yojanas]. In the north-east is Pārijāta; in the south-west,
> Sudharmā.

The Bhāṣya identifies the pleasant grounds as divine recreation places, then
describes Pārijāta's dimensions and fragrance. Its account of fragrance
becomes a sustained causal discussion: how can scent travel against the wind,
what supports its continuity, and how does this report relate to a Buddha
verse about ordinary flower-fragrance? The unit closes by locating Sudharmā,
the gods' assembly hall, in the south-west.

The running source contains mechanical noise, including a prefixed `s` in
`sprativātam`, irregular word joins, and spelling variants; these are

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

In the four directions from these gardens are four pleasant grounds,
separated from them by twenty yojanas. They are themselves recreation places
of the gods, and through their mutual rivalry they spread beauty.

Outside, beside the city, Pārijāta is in the north-east and Sudharmā in the
south-west.

Pārijāta is a kovidāra tree and a support for the highest sensual enjoyment
of the Trāyastriṃśa gods. Its root-base extends five yojanas; it is one
hundred yojanas high. Its branches and foliage extend fifty yojanas.

When it is in full bloom, its fragrance travels one hundred yojanas
downwind and fifty yojanas against the wind.

Downwind is reasonable; but how can it travel against the wind?

Some say that this is stated with reference to its not going beyond the tree.
For wind does not literally blow against the wind.

Rather, one should ascribe such efficacy to the fragrance itself: even while
being checked by gentle divine breezes, it produces a further continuity of
fragrance. Because this continuation begins ever more faintly, it soon ceases;
therefore it does not travel such a great distance.

But does this continuity of flower-fragrance proceed supported by its own
material constituents, or does the air itself become perfumed? There is no
fixed rule here. The teachers accept both possibilities.

But then what the Blessed One said—“The fragrance of flowers does not travel
against the wind, neither jasmine, tagara, nor sandalwood; but the fragrance
of good people travels against the wind, and the good person blows in every
direction”—was said with reference to human flower-fragrance, which is
commonly known in the world. It does not have such power. The Mahīśāsakas,
however, recite: “Its fragrance travels one hundred yojanas downwind and
fifty yojanas against the wind.”

Sudharmā is a divine assembly hall in the south-western quarter. Seated there,
the gods deliberate what is to be done and what is not to be done.

## 4. Natural Movement and Voices

The commentary moves from the garden layout into an extended causal inquiry,
then returns to Sudharmā:

1. Four pleasant grounds lie twenty yojanas from the four gardens and add to
   the area's beauty through mutual rivalry.
2. Pārijāta is located in the north-east and characterized by its sensual
   function, dimensions, and fragrance.
3. The claim of fragrance traveling against the wind prompts an objection.
4. The Bhāṣya preserves a scope-restriction account and a causal-continuity
   account, then asks what bears the fragrance-series.
5. A Buddha verse appears to conflict with the report; Vasubandhu restricts
   that verse to ordinary human flower-fragrance and records a
   Mahīśāsaka-specific report.
6. Sudharmā is located in the south-west and identified as the assembly where
   gods deliberate about what is and is not to be done.

Voices and attributions matter here. `Ity eke` introduces one explanation;
the efficacy account follows as another response. The question whether the
fragrance-series depends on its own constituents or perfumed air is answered
without a single mandated model: the teachers accept both. The Mahīśāsaka
report is explicitly attributed and should not be made the anonymous wording
of the general account.

## 5. The Grounds and Pārijāta

The four `subhūmis` are located twenty yojanas from the four gardens. They
are themselves `krīḍāsthāna`, recreation places of the gods. The text says
their mutual rivalry spreads beauty; the grounds contribute to a shared
splendor through comparison with one another.

Pārijāta stands outside the city in the north-east. The Bhāṣya identifies it
as a kovidāra and calls it a support for the highest sensual enjoyment of the
Trāyastriṃśas. Its measures are:

| Feature | Measure |
|---|---:|
| Root-base | 5 yojanas |
| Height | 100 yojanas |
| Branch and foliage spread | 50 yojanas |
| Full-bloom fragrance downwind | 100 yojanas |
| Full-bloom fragrance against the wind | 50 yojanas |

The source supplies the branch-and-foliage extent but does not expressly name
its geometrical axis. The translation avoids converting that measure into a
more specific radius or diameter.

## 6. The Objection About Upwind Fragrance

The Bhāṣya treats the downwind claim as ordinary: wind carries fragrance in
its direction. It then asks how fragrance can travel against the wind. One
group answers that “against the wind” means only that fragrance extends to a
point not beyond the tree's own reach. The next objection notes that wind does
not itself blow against the wind, pressing the explanation to clarify what
actually moves.

The causal response assigns efficacy to the fragrance. Although gentle divine
breezes resist it, the fragrance produces a further fragrance-series. That
series starts with diminishing force and therefore ceases soon, which
accounts for its shorter upwind range.

The argument distinguishes:

```text
wind direction
fragrance's causal efficacy
resistance by divine breezes
range of the resulting fragrance-series
```

The effect is not explained by claiming that the wind reverses direction.

## 7. What Supports the Fragrance-Series?

Vasubandhu next asks whether the flower-fragrance continuum is supported by
its own material constituents or whether the air becomes perfumed. The
answer is deliberately non-exclusive: there is no fixed rule, and teachers
accept both accounts.

These are two causal-support models, not two observed fragrances:

```text
model A: fragrance-series supported by its own constituents
model B: air becomes perfumed and bears the continuing scent
```

The text preserves the explanatory choice without deciding it. Its account
of Pārijāta's unusual range thus includes a claim, a challenge, a proposed
mechanism, and an acknowledged plurality of supporting models.

## 8. Buddha's Verse and the Mahīśāsaka Report

The cited verse states that ordinary flower-fragrance does not go against the
wind, while the fragrance of good people does. Vasubandhu resolves the
apparent conflict by specifying the verse's scope: it speaks of human flower-
fragrance, familiar in the world, which lacks Pārijāta's extraordinary
efficacy.

The Mahīśāsakas, however, recite the explicit numerical report: one hundred
yojanas downwind and fifty against the wind. That attribution belongs to
their transmitted account. The citation is not used to erase the cosmological
claim; rather, the Bhāṣya differentiates ordinary human flowers from the
special tree and notes a school-specific recitation.

## 9. Sudharmā and Organon Study

Sudharmā is placed outside the city in the south-west. It is a `devasabhā`, a
divine assembly hall, where the gods sit and deliberate `kṛtya` and `akṛtya`:
what is to be done and what is not to be done.

The unit therefore joins two distinct determinations within one divine
environment:

```text
Pārijāta → refined sensual enjoyment and causal analysis of fragrance
Sudharmā → assembly and deliberation concerning conduct
```

For the Organon, the latter phrase is a concrete instance of practical
discrimination inside produced Loka. It describes the gods' deliberative
activity; it is not itself a claim that their assembly constitutes Pure
Practical Reason. This reconstruction keeps the source's institutional
description intact while noting the practical form of the activity.
