# VAK_3.04 Bhāṣya — Domains, Destinies, and the Designation “Being”

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> narakādisvanāmoktā gatayaḥ pañca teṣu tāḥ /
> akliṣṭāvyākṛtā eva sattvākhyā nāntarābhavaḥ // 3.04 //

> Within those domains are five destinies, called by their own names beginning
> with hell. According to the Vaibhāṣika determination, they are exclusively
> unafflicted and indeterminate, designated as beings; intermediate existence
> is not a destiny.

The qualification “according to the Vaibhāṣika determination” is required by
the complete commentary. The Bhāṣya first argues that the destinies are
`anivṛtāvyākṛta`, non-obscured indeterminate, but then records another view
according to which they may also be wholesome or afflicted. The exclusion of
intermediate existence is maintained, though its justification is debated.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Why are these called the desire-, form-, and formless-domains? A domain is so
called because it bears its own characteristic. The domain connected with
desire is the desire-domain. The domain connected with form is the
form-domain, with the middle member elided, as in compounds such as
“diamond-child” and “pepper-drink.” Because form does not exist there, it is
formless. The state of being formless is formlessness. Alternatively, what is
capable of being formed is “formable”; what is not formable is “formless,” and
its state is formlessness. The domain connected with that is the
formless-domain. Or the desire-domain is the domain of desires, that which
bears desires. The form- and formless-domains should be understood similarly.

What is this thing called desire? In brief, it is craving associated with
morsel-food and sexual intercourse. For a verse says: “The attractive things
in the world are not desires; a person's desire is craving born of
conception. The attractive things remain just as they are in the world, while
the resolute discipline desire concerning them.”

An Ājīvaka answered the venerable Śāriputra: “If the attractive things in the
world are not desires, and you call conceptual craving desire, then a monk who
conceives unwholesome thoughts will still be an enjoyer of desires.”
Śāriputra replied: “If the attractive things in the world are desires, and
conceptual craving is not desire for you, then your own teacher will be an
enjoyer of desires merely upon seeing delightful forms.”

Are all dharmas that occur in the three domains connected with their
respective domains? No. Those in which craving for desire, form, or
formlessness lies latent are connected with that domain. What, then, are those
three cravings? Those which lie latent in the three respective domains. Is
this not the circular explanation of the horse and its tether—“Whose tether
is this? The horse's. Whose horse is this? The tether's”—so that neither is
known?

It is not circular in this way, because the locations within the desire-domain
have already been specified. The craving of one not free from attachment to
those locations is desire-craving, and the dharma in which it lies latent is
connected with the desire-domain. Craving for form and formlessness should be
understood correspondingly in one who is free from attachment to the lower
domain. Alternatively, craving belonging to a non-concentrated level is
desire-craving; craving in the dhyānas and formless attainments is craving for
form and formlessness.

How can desire-craving occur in a mind of magical creation? Because one takes
delight in that creation after hearing of it or after falling away from it;
or the creator's mind may itself have craving through the force of the
creation, or because odors and tastes are created. This craving belongs to the
desire-domain, since a form-domain mind does not create those two.

Is there only one system of three domains? Systems of three domains have no
end: there are as many domains as the extent of space. For this very reason
there is no appearance of completely new beings. Although innumerable beings
attain complete nirvāṇa at the appearance of each Buddha, beings are never
exhausted, just as space is not exhausted.

How are the world-systems arranged? The Tiryaksūtra says that, just as when the
rain god Īṣādhāra rains there is no break or interval between the
streams of water falling from the sky, so toward the east there is no break
or interval among world-systems contracting and expanding; and likewise in
the south, west, and north. It does not say “above and below.” Others say that
there are systems above and below too, according to a reading of another
school. Above Akaniṣṭha there is again a desire-domain, and below a
desire-domain there are again Akaniṣṭhas. One who is free from attachment to
one desire-domain is free from attachment to all of them, and likewise for
the form- and formless-domains. Someone born in a given world-system who
develops a supernormal power based on the first dhyāna reaches only that
world-system's Brahmā-world, not another.

Within the three domains just stated are “five destinies called by their own
names beginning with hell”: hell-beings, animals, pretas, devas, and humans.
The desire-domain contains four complete destinies and a portion of the fifth;
the form- and formless-domains contain only portions of the deva destiny.

Are there then domain-dharmas outside the destinies, since the destinies are
said to be within the domains? Yes. Wholesome dharmas, afflicted dharmas, the
receptacle-world, and intermediate existence also belong to the domains. But
the five destinies “are exclusively unafflicted and indeterminate, designated
as beings; intermediate existence is not” one of them. The destinies are
exclusively non-obscured indeterminate; otherwise the destinies would become
intermixed. They are designated as beings and are not intermediate existence.

The Prajñapti says that the five destinies are included within the four modes
of birth, but that the four modes of birth are not exhausted by the five
destinies: intermediate existence is left unincluded. The Dharmaskandha also
lists the eye-element derived from the four great elements as hellish, animal,
preta, divine, human, cultivation-produced, and belonging to intermediate
existence.

A sūtra likewise excludes intermediate existence from the destinies. Which
sūtra? The one that gives seven existences: hell-existence, animal-existence,
preta-existence, deva-existence, human-existence, karma-existence, and
intermediate existence. Here the five destinies are taught together with
their cause and with what accompanies the going toward them. Their being
non-obscured indeterminate follows from the exclusion of their cause,
karma-existence, from them.

The Kāśmīras also recite a discourse attributed to the elder Śāriputra. When
one produces and accumulates karma to be experienced in hell through the
manifestation of the relevant influxes and through the crookedness, faults,
and impurities of body, speech, and mind, the five aggregates mature in the
hells as result. Once the result has arisen, one receives the designation
“hell-being”; yet no hell-being is apprehended apart from those dharmas. This
too is taken to prove that the destinies are exclusively non-obscured
indeterminate.

An objection appeals to the Prakaraṇa statement that all latent tendencies lie
latent in the destinies. The reply is that there are five kinds of
relinking-consciousness corresponding to the destinies. The statement is
therefore unobjectionable because “destiny” includes the entrance into a
destiny, just as mention of a village includes its outskirts.

Others hold that destinies may also be wholesome or afflicted. Separate
mention does not necessarily imply exclusion: within the five corruptions,
the corruption of afflictions and the corruption of views are stated
separately, although views are afflictions. In the same way, karma-existence
could itself also be a destiny; it may have been stated separately in order to
show the cause of destiny.

Would the same reasoning make intermediate existence a destiny? No, because
it does not fit. A destiny is that to which beings go; intermediate existence
is not a destination, since it arises in the very place of death. But then the
formless destinies would not be destinies either, since they too arise in the
place of death. In that case, the answer is that intermediate existence is not
a destiny precisely because it is intermediate existence—because it occupies
the interval between destinies. If it were a destiny, it would not be called
intermediate existence.

What, then, of Śāriputra's statement that once the result has arisen one
receives the designation “hell-being”? It says “when the result has arisen,”
not “the result alone.” And what of the claim that no hell-being is apprehended
apart from those dharmas? It denies a substantial person who travels toward a
destiny: no hell-being is apprehended apart from the aggregates. It does not
deny the inclusion of other aggregates.

The Vaibhāṣikas, however, maintain that the destinies are exclusively
indeterminate. Some say that they consist only in maturation-result; others
say that they also include what is produced through accumulation.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

This is a compound unit with three movements:

```text
112.24–114.04
    what makes a dhātu and what connects a dharma with it?
    how many three-domain world-systems are there?

114.04–114.27
    what are the five destinies?
    why are intermediate existence and karmic cause excluded?

114.27–115.16
    objections to their exclusively indeterminate status
    alternate view and Vaibhāṣika restatement
```

The expository voice conducts the domain inquiry. Scriptural verses, an
Ājīvaka, and Śāriputra appear in the discussion of desire. The destiny
argument invokes the Prajñapti, Dharmaskandha, another sūtra, and a Kāśmīra
recension of a Śāriputra discourse. An unnamed alternate position argues that
destinies may also be wholesome or afflicted. The concluding sentence
explicitly attributes the exclusively indeterminate account to the
Vaibhāṣikas, while noting two internal explanations of its constitution.

The research witness contains only the middle movement. Treating it as the
whole commentary would conceal both the determination of the field in which
destinies occur and the objections limiting the apparent finality of the
verse's ethical classification.

## 5. Domain, Connection, and World-System

The initial definition, `svalakṣaṇadhāraṇād dhātuḥ`, makes a domain that which
bears its own characteristic. Yet a dharma's occurrence in a domain is not
enough to make it “connected with” that domain. Connection is determined by
the craving capable of lying latent in it. The apparent circle—domain-craving
defined by domain, domain-connection defined by craving—is checked by the
already established locations and by the alternative distinction between
non-concentrated, dhyāna, and formless levels.

This preserves three different relations:

```text
occurs in a domain
    ≠ necessarily connected with that domain

craving lies latent in a dharma
    → that dharma is connected with the corresponding domain

domain
    bears its characteristic field of connected dharmas
```

The discussion then pluralizes the entire three-domain structure. There are
indefinitely many world-systems, arranged without gaps horizontally and,
according to another school's reading, also above and below. Yet freedom from
attachment to one domain applies to all corresponding domains. Cosmological
plurality does not multiply the kind of attachment or the liberation from it.

## 6. Destiny Within Domain

The locative `teṣu`, “within those,” marks a strict containment rather than an
identity:

```text
dhātu
    includes gati
    but also includes
        wholesome and afflicted dharmas
        receptacle-world
        intermediate existence
```

The five destinies are hell, animal, preta, deva, and human. Their distribution
across domains is uneven: the desire-domain contains four entire destinies and
part of the divine destiny, whereas the two higher domains contain only parts
of the divine destiny. Domain and destiny are therefore different axes of
classification.

`Sattvākhyā` does not establish a substance called a being behind the relevant
dharmas. In the cited formula, the designation “hell-being” applies when the
corresponding aggregates have arisen as karmic result, and no such being is
found apart from the dharmas. The text determines a rule of designation upon
a resultant complex, not an independent bearer separable from it.

## 7. Result, Cause, Transition, and Dispute

The Vaibhāṣika argument separates four functions:

| Function | Term | Determination |
|---|---|---|
| field | `dhātu` | broader domain containing destinies and non-destiny dharmas |
| result-position | `gati` | settled course of sentient rebirth |
| generating cause | `karmabhava` | karma-existence producing the destiny |
| transition | `antarābhava` | interval between destinies, not itself a destination |

On this model, a painful destiny is not thereby an afflicted act. Its karmic
cause may be afflicted or unwholesome, while the matured result-position is
non-obscured indeterminate. The separate enumeration of `karmabhava` supports
this cause-result distinction.

The alternate view denies that separate enumeration proves exclusion and
allows wholesome or afflicted dharmas within a destiny. It also rereads the
Śāriputra citation: “when the result has arisen” does not say “the result
alone,” and denial of a hell-being apart from the aggregates rules out a
substantial transmigrating person, not necessarily every non-resultant member
of the aggregate series.

The attempted definition of `gati` as a destination also encounters the
formless case, where rebirth arises at the place of death. The more successful
exclusion of intermediate existence is functional: it is the interval between
destinies and therefore cannot itself be one of the termini it connects.

The Bhāṣya closes without erasing the disagreement. The Vaibhāṣikas retain the
exclusively indeterminate classification, but even they differ over whether a
destiny is exclusively maturation-result or also includes accumulation-produced
dharmas.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

The preparatory prose prevents `dhātu` from being translated as a mere place.
It is a characteristic-bearing domain of connected dharmas, and innumerable
instances of the three-domain world-system may occur throughout space.

`Gati` is best retained as “destiny,” provided its verbal and functional force
remains visible: it is a course that terminates in a determinate rebirth
configuration. It is neither coextensive with its domain nor simply identical
with the karma that produces it.

The verse's `akliṣṭāvyākṛtā eva` receives the more exact Vaibhāṣika expression
`anivṛtāvyākṛta`, “non-obscured indeterminate.” But the official translation
must expose its school status because the complete commentary records a
contrary view. The exclusivity expressed by `eva` belongs to the proposition
under dispute.

`Nāntarābhavaḥ` excludes intermediate existence from the five destinies; it
does not deny intermediate existence. The sources cited in the Bhāṣya and the
functional distinction between transition and destination support this
reading.

Finally, `sattvākhyā` is a designation claim. The citation denies an
independent hell-being apart from the relevant dharmas, while the opposing
interpretation cautions that this alone does not determine the complete range
of dharmas included in a destiny.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

VAK 3.04 changes the cosmology from a map into a differentiated process:

```text
domain
    field of possible occurrence and attachment

karma-existence
    generating cause

intermediate existence
    transition

destiny
    resultant placement

“being”
    designation upon the determinate aggregate-complex
```

The critical achievement is not the multiplication of worlds but the
non-collapse of these functions. A cause is not its result; a passage is not
its destination; a domain is not one class of occupants; and the designation
of a bearer is not another substance added behind the configured dharmas.

For the Organon, this is the first strong transit-logic of Loka. A Rational
Moral Agent may be discussed only if agency, morally determining activity,
transition, resultant placement, and the empirical designation of the bearer
remain distinguishable. The text itself supplies the narrower architecture;
“Rational Moral Agent” is the project's reconstruction, not a translation of
`sattva`.

The discussion of innumerable world-systems adds an important universality.
Freedom from attachment is not local escape from one cosmic territory: one
who is free from a given domain's attachment is free from all corresponding
domains. Thus the cosmological many and the functional determination of
craving do not compete. Repetition of the field does not multiply its
principle.

This also disciplines the project's language of Outflow and Projection.
Karmic activity generates a resultant placement, but the produced destiny is
not identical with that activity. Any later theory of Projection must retain
the distinction between projecting cause, transition, projected
configuration, and the designation of the agent within it.
