# VAK_3.72

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

> स्थानात् स्थानादधो यावत्तावदूर्ध्वं ततस्ततः ।
>
> नोर्ध्वं दर्शनमस्त्येषामन्यत्रर्द्धिपराश्रयात् ॥ ३.७२ ॥

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

> sthānāt sthānād adho yāvat tāvad ūrdhvaṃ tatas tataḥ /
>
> nordhvaṃ darśanam asty eṣām anyatrarddhiparāśrayāt // 3.72 //

| Form | Morphology | Lexical force here |
|---|---|---|
| sthānāt sthānāt | repeated ablative singular | from each successive station |
| adhaḥ | adverb | downward / below |
| yāvat ... tāvat | correlative pair | as far as ... so far |
| ūrdhvam | adverbial accusative | upward / above |
| tataḥ tataḥ | distributive repetition | from each station in succession |
| na ... darśanam asti | negative existential construction | there is no seeing |
| eṣām | genitive plural pronoun | for these lower-station beings |
| anyatra | indeclinable | except / apart from |
| ṛddhi | feminine noun | supernormal efficacy or power |
| parāśraya | masculine compound | reliance upon another |

The Bhāṣya separates the final expression into two alternatives: access by
one's own `ṛddhi`, or access through reliance upon another. It should not be
flattened into one compound power.

## 4. Grammar

The first line establishes a rule of vertical spacing:

```text
sthānāt sthānāt
    adhaḥ [jambūdvīpaḥ] yāvat
    tataḥ tataḥ
    ūrdhvam [sthānāntaram] tāvat
```

> From each station, as far as Jambūdvīpa lies below, so far above that station
> lies the next station.

Jambūdvīpa is not stated in the Kārikā but is supplied by the Bhāṣya as the
fixed lower reference-point. The line therefore concerns proportional
distance, not primarily the range of downward vision.

The second line answers whether lower-station beings can go upward and see:

```text
eṣām
    ūrdhvam darśanam
    na asti
    anyatra
        ṛddheḥ
        para-āśrayāt [vā]
```

> These beings have no upward seeing, except through supernormal power or
> reliance upon another.

The disjunction and the practical examples of each access mode are Bhāṣya
determinations.

## 5. Translation

### Close syntactic construe

> From each station, as far as [the reference point lies] below it, so far
> above it lies the next station. These beings do not see upward, except through
> supernormal power or reliance upon another.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> Beginning from Jambūdvīpa, the distance from any heavenly station down to
> Jambūdvīpa equals the distance from that station up to the next station. Thus
> the principal station of the Four Great Kings stands 40,000 yojanas above
> here, the station of the Thirty-Three another 40,000 above that, and each
> subsequent interval equals the total height already reached. The sequence
> ends at Akaniṣṭha, above which there is no further station. Beings of a lower
> station do not ordinarily ascend to and see a higher residence. They may do
> so through their own ṛddhi or by being led by a more powerful deva or one
> belonging to that higher station. Yet a genuinely higher dhātu or bhūmi does
> not thereby become an object of lower-level faculties. According to the
> Nikāyāntarīyas, a higher being appears below through a constructed body
> belonging to the lower bhūmi.

The Jambūdvīpa reference, examples and terminal station, two access modes,
object-domain restriction, and constructed-body account are Bhāṣya
determinations.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> The hierarchy is generated through a proportional spacing rule, but distance
> alone does not determine access. Every station also configures a proper
> object-domain for its inhabitants. Supernormal movement or assistance may
> cross a spatial interval without automatically making the native form of a
> higher bhūmi perceptible to lower faculties. Communication across levels may
> therefore require transformation into a form adequate to the recipient's
> domain. Position, locomotion, perceptual capacity, and presentable form are
> coordinated but irreducible determinations.

## 8. Logical Determination

### Proportional spacing rule

Let Jambūdvīpa be height `0`, and let `Hₙ` be the height of station `Sₙ`:

```text
distance(Sₙ, Jambūdvīpa) = Hₙ
distance(Sₙ₊₁, Sₙ)       = Hₙ

therefore:
    Hₙ₊₁ = Hₙ + Hₙ = 2Hₙ
```

Beginning with the fourth terrace as the principal station of the Four Great
Kings:

| Station | Height above Jambūdvīpa | Interval from preceding station |
|---|---:|---:|
| Four Great Kings' principal station | 40,000 | 40,000 from the reference |
| Trāyastriṃśa station | 80,000 | 40,000 |
| Yāma station | 160,000 | 80,000 |
| Tuṣita station | 320,000 | 160,000 |

The distance is not a fixed increment. The absolute height doubles with each
successor.

### Terminal condition

```text
station sequence → ... → Akaniṣṭha
successor(Akaniṣṭha) = none
```

The recurrence operates over a bounded ordered set; it does not imply an
infinite series of stations.

### Distinct access predicates

```text
travelsTo(A, higherStation)
isPresentAt(A, higherStation)
sees(A, higherResidence)
hasAsObject(A, higherNativeBody)
belongsTo(A, higherBhūmi)
```

No two of these predicates are automatically identical. The Bhāṣya's examples
require them to be represented separately.

### Exceptional ascent

```text
own-power route:
    lower Agent + sufficient ṛddhi → ascent

mediated route:
    lower Agent
        + powerful guide or resident higher deva
        → ascent through parāśraya
```

These are alternative means of access: endogenous capacity and relationally
mediated capacity.

### Object-domain restriction

```text
native higher-level body
    + lower-level faculty
        → aviṣayatva
        → not perceptible as an object of that faculty
```

The Bhāṣya compares this with a tangible object outside the operative range of
touch. Non-perception here is not mere absence or distance; it is a mismatch
between object-level and faculty-domain.

### Cross-level presentation model

According to the Nikāyāntarīyas:

```text
higher being's native body
    → not presented directly below

higher being's intention
    → constructs lower-bhūmi body
        → appears as if native to the lower world
        → becomes available to lower perception
```

The transformation changes the presentation interface, not the originating
station of the higher being.

## 9. Interpretive Note

VAK 3.72 supplies two architectures at once:

```text
metric architecture:
    station → proportional successor distance

semantic architecture:
    station → faculty-range → possible object-domain
```

The first tells us where levels stand. The second tells us what can appear to
an Agent stationed at a level. A cosmological coordinate does not by itself
grant epistemic access.

This is a remarkably precise account of Flying. Ṛddhi or a higher guide may
carry an Agent across the spatial relation, but locomotion does not abolish
bhūmi-difference. Arrival, vision, and comprehension cannot be treated as one
operation. The middle world is navigable, yet navigation remains constrained
by the faculties and object-domain of the traveler.

The Nikāyāntarīya proposal then gives a strong presentation principle:

```text
when source-level form exceeds recipient-level object capacity,
construct a recipient-compatible form.
```

This resembles a semantic interface rather than a mere disguise. The higher
being's native determination cannot simply be serialized unchanged into the
lower field; a lower-bhūmi body must be produced so that a real relation of
appearance can occur there.

```ts
interface WorldStation {
  height: number;
  facultyDomain: string;
  successor?: WorldStation;
}

const successorHeight = (station: WorldStation) => 2 * station.height;

interface CrossLevelPresentation {
  sourceBhumi: string;
  recipientBhumi: string;
  nativeBodyPerceptible: false;
  presentedBody: "ConstructedAtRecipientBhumi";
}
```

This is explanatory project notation, not Vasubandhu's vocabulary. The
semantic–syntactic discipline matters here because a sentence about “seeing
upward” could otherwise conceal four different operations: spatial ascent,
encounter, perceptual objecthood, and level-adequate manifestation.

The `dhyānāntara` difficulty of 3.71 and the `aviṣayatva` rule here point in the
same methodological direction: the Kośa does not let ordinal placement erase
the predicates that actually define a domain. Being inserted into a sequence
is never sufficient by itself.
