# VAK_3.93 Bhāṣya — The Great Kalpa and the Bodhisattva’s Resolve

## Kārikā Anchor

```iast
vivartate 'tha saṃvṛtta āste saṃvartate samam /
te hy aśītir mahākalpas tad asaṃkhyatrayodbhavam // 3.93 //
```

> It expands; then, contracted, it remains; it contracts for an equal span.
> Those eighty constitute a great kalpa; from three `asaṃkhyeyas` of these
> arises [buddhahood].

The first line completes the four-phase cycle discussed in 3.92. The second
turns from cosmic time to the great-kalpa measure and the bodhisattva’s
attainment of buddhahood.

## Continuous Sanskrit

The Bhāṣya's natural unit runs from the definition of the great kalpa through
its account of the bodhisattva’s resolve, compassion, and the period in which
buddhas arise. Its sixty-place numerical catalogue is very long; the
catalogue is represented below by an explicit editorial marker, while its
opening, conclusion, and argumentative use are retained.

```iast
Te hy aśītir mahākalpaḥ.

Etan mahākalpasya pramāṇam. Kalpaḥ kiṃsvabhāvaḥ? Pañcaskandhasvabhāvaḥ.

Yad ucyate: “Tribhir asaṃkhyeyaiḥ kalpānāṃ budhatvaṃ prāpyate,” iti, tat
katameṣāṃ kalpānām? Ya eva mahākalpo nirdiṣṭaḥ.

Tad asaṃkhyatrayodbhavam // 3.93 //

Buddhatvam. Saṃkhyeyānāṃ kalpānāṃ trayeṇa.

Katham asaṃkhyeyasyāsati saṃkhyeyāvasāne punas tritvam ucyate? Naivam etad
veditavyam. Kiṃ tarhi? “Ṣaṣṭiḥ sthānāntarāṇy asaṃkhyeyam” iti muktakasūtraṃ
paṭhyate.

Katamāni ṣaṣṭiḥ? Eko hy advitīyaḥ prathamaṃ sthānāntaram; ekakānāṃ daśako
dvitīyam; daśa daśakāni śataṃ tṛtīyam; daśa śatāni sahasram; daśa sahasrāṇi
prabhedaḥ; daśa prabhedā lakṣam; daśa lakṣā atilakṣaḥ; daśātilakṣāḥ kauṭiḥ;
... [the source continues the successive named tenfold places] ...
daśa mahābalākṣāṇy asaṃkhyam. Aṣṭakaṃ madhyād vistṛtam.

Ity eteṣāṃ ṣaṣṭisthānāntaragatāṃ saṃkhyām anuprāptāḥ kalpā asaṃkhyeyān ity
ucyante. Tato vyāvṛtya punar gaṇyante. Evaṃ trīṇi asaṃkhyeyānīti ucyante; na
tu naiva parisaṃkhyātuṃ śakyanta iti.

Kiṃ tu khalu kālaprakarṣeṇaiva kṛtapraṇidhānā bodhisattvā bodhim abhisaṃbudhyante.
Kim etad eva bhaviṣyati? Mahatā hi puṇyajñānasaṃbhāreṇa ṣaḍbhiḥ pāramitābhiḥ
bahubhir duṣkaraśatasahasrais tribhiḥ kalpāsaṃkhyeyair anuttarāṃ samyaksaṃbodhim
abhisaṃbudhyante bodhisattvāḥ.

Yady apy anyathāpy asti mokṣāvakāśaḥ, kimarthaṃ ta iyantaṃ yatnam ārabhante?
Parārthaṃ ta iyantaṃ yatnam ārabhante: kathaṃ parān api mahato duḥkhaughāt
paritrātuṃ śaknuyām iti.

Ka eṣāṃ parārthena svārthaḥ? Eṣa eva teṣāṃ svārtho yaḥ parārthas tasyābhimatatvāt.

Idānīm etac chrāddhāsyate. Satyaṃ duḥśraddhānam etad ātmaṃbharibhir niṣkaruṇaiḥ;
kāruṇikais tu śraddhitam evaitat. Yathā ceha kecid abhyastanair dhṛṇyā asaty api
svārthe paravyasanābhiratā upalabhyante, tathā punar abhyastakāruṇyā asaty api
svārthe parahitakriyābhirāmāḥ santīti saṃbhāvyam.

Yathaiva cābhyāsavaśād anātmabhūteṣu saṃskāreṣu saṃskṛtalakṣaṇānabhijñā
ātmasnehaṃ niveśya tad-dhetor duḥkhāny udvahanti, evaṃ punar abhyāsavaśād
ātmasnehaṃ tebhyo nirvartya pareṣv apekṣāṃ vardhayitvā tad-dhetor duḥkhāny
udvahantīti saṃbhāvyam.

Gotrāntaram eva hi tat tathājātīyaṃ nirvartate yat pareṣāṃ duḥkhena duḥkhāyate,
sukhena sukhāyate, nātmana iti. Na te punaḥ svārtham anyaṃ paśyanti. Āha cātra:

“Hīnaḥ prārthayate svasaṃtatigataṃ yaistair upāyaiḥ sukhaṃ;
madhyo duḥkhanivṛttim eva, na sukhaṃ duḥkhāspadaṃ tad yataḥ;
śreṣṭhaḥ prārthayate svasaṃtatigatair duḥkhaiḥ pareṣāṃ sukhaṃ;
duḥkhātyantanivṛttim eva ca yataḥ, tad duḥkhaduḥkhyeva saḥ.”

```

**Textual and translation notes.** The witness has conspicuous corruption and
variant spellings in places; sandhi and punctuation are normalized, not
critically reconstructed. The long sequence of named numerical magnitudes is
abridged as marked; the translation states its rule and the Bhāṣya’s
conclusion.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

These eighty intermediate kalpas are a great kalpa. This is the measure of a
great kalpa. What is a kalpa by nature? It has the nature of the five
aggregates.

It is said that buddhahood is attained through three `asaṃkhyeya` kalpas. Of
which kalpas? Of the very great kalpa just described. “From the arising of
three `asaṃkhyeyas” [is the phrase in the verse]. The referent is buddhahood:
it is attained through three countable kalpas [of the named `asaṃkhyeya`
magnitude].

An objection asks: if `asaṃkhyeya` has no endpoint of counting, how can one
speak of three? It should not be understood that way. A *Muktaka Sūtra* is
recited: “Sixty place-values are an `asaṃkhyeya`.” The text begins the
sequence: one is the first place; ten units make the second; ten tens make a
hundred, the third; ten hundreds make a thousand; ten thousands make the
next named magnitude; ten of those make a hundred-thousand, and so on through
successive named powers of ten. It concludes with the sixtieth place,
`asaṃkhya`, and a further expression describing its relation to the middle.
When kalpas reach the number at these sixty places, they are called
`asaṃkhyeya`. Counting then turns back and begins again; thus one speaks of
three `asaṃkhyeyas`. It is not that they cannot be enumerated.

Bodhisattvas who have made their resolve over a very long span awaken to
bodhi. Is that all that is meant? Bodhisattvas awaken to unsurpassed, complete
awakening through three `kalpāsaṃkhyeyas`, with a great accumulation of merit
and knowledge, the six perfections, and many hundreds of thousands of
difficult deeds.

If there is also an opportunity for liberation by another way, why do they
undertake such effort? They undertake it for the sake of others: “How may I
also rescue others from the great flood of suffering?” What is their own
interest in benefiting others? Precisely this is their own interest: the
benefit of others is what they wish for.

“Now this will be hard to believe.” Indeed, self-serving people without
compassion find it hard to believe. But compassionate people do believe it.
Here some people, through long habit, delight in the suffering of others even
when they gain no benefit themselves. So too it is possible that people who
have long cultivated compassion delight in acting for others’ benefit even
without personal gain.

Just as, through habit, people place self-love in conditioned formations that
are not self, not understanding the marks of the conditioned, and undergo
suffering on their account, so too, through habit, they may withdraw
self-love from these, increase their regard for others, and undergo
suffering on their account. A distinct disposition of this kind develops:
one who grieves at others’ suffering and rejoices at their happiness, not at
one’s own. Such people see no other self-interest.

The verse says: the inferior person seeks happiness for their own continuum
by whatever means; the middling person seeks only the cessation of suffering,
not happiness, since happiness is a basis for suffering; the superior person
seeks others’ happiness even through suffering in their own continuum, and
seeks the complete cessation of suffering—because suffering itself is
suffering to them.

## Natural Movement

The commentary turns from the scale of the world-cycle to the meaning of
`asaṃkhyeya` and the bodhisattva path. It does not leave the term as
absolute uncountability: in the cited numerical convention it is a named
magnitude at the sixtieth place, after which counting can begin again. Three
such spans of great kalpas give the measure of the bodhisattva’s arduous
course to buddhahood.

The extended defense of that course is specifically ethical: compassion can
be cultivated until another’s suffering and happiness become one’s concern.
The text contrasts this with self-directed happiness and the more limited
aim of ending one’s own suffering.

## Organon Contact Point

Light contact: this passage links an immense temporal measure to a
deliberately cultivated orientation toward others. The relation is a
comparative reading only; Vasubandhu’s claims are given in the translation
above.
