# VAK_3.12 Bhāṣya — Scriptural Proof and Contested Transmission

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> sahaikatra dvayābhāvād asantānād dvayodayāt /
> kaṇṭhokteś cāsti gandharvāt pañcokter gatisūtrataḥ // 3.12 //

> Reflection is not a valid example because the two cannot coexist in one
> place, because it is not of the same continuity, and because it arises from
> two causes. Intermediate existence exists, moreover, because it is expressly
> stated, because of the gandharva, because five non-returners are stated, and
> because of the discourse on the courses of good persons.

The first half recapitulates the completed rational refutation of reflection.
The second opens the proof from `āgama`, authoritative teaching. The Bhāṣya
does not appeal to one uncontested quotation: it works through variant
recitations, rival interpretations, and apparent countertexts.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Intermediate existence exists because it is expressly stated. A sūtra says:
“There are seven existences: hell-existence, animal-existence, preta-
existence, deva-existence, human-existence, karma-existence, and intermediate
existence.”

The opponents reply that this sūtra is not recited in their transmission.
Then there is the proof “because of the gandharva.” A teaching says: “When
three conditions are present, the embryo descends into the mother's womb: the
mother is capable of conception and in season; the mother and father are
impassioned and have come together; and a gandharva is present.” Apart from
intermediate existence, what other gandharva could this be?

They do not recite this passage either. What do they read? “The dissolution
of the aggregates is present.” If so, how is the Āśvalāyana Sūtra to be
understood? It asks whether the gandharva who has become present is a
kṣatriya, brāhmaṇa, vaiśya, or śūdra, and whether he has come from the east,
south, west, or north. Coming from a direction cannot reasonably be predicated
of a dissolution of aggregates.

If that too is not read, there is the proof “because five are stated.” The
Blessed One taught five non-returners: one who attains parinirvāṇa in the
intermediate state, one who attains it upon rebirth, one who attains it without
effort, one who attains it with effort, and one who goes upstream. If there
were no intermediate existence, how could there be one called an
`antarāparinirvāyin`?

Some say that there are devas named “Antarā.” But then the expressions
beginning with “upon rebirth” would likewise have to be names of devas. This
construction is therefore not sound.

There is also proof “because of the discourse on courses.” In the sūtra on the
seven courses of good persons, three intermediate attainers of parinirvāṇa are
taught, differentiated by time, place, and degree. The first resembles a
small spark that goes out just as it is produced. The second resembles a
piece of heated iron that goes out while flying upward. The third resembles a
piece of heated iron that, having flown upward, goes out before falling to the
earth. It is mere invention to posit three kinds of devas named “Antarā” that
differ in time, place, and degree in these ways.

Others say that one who abandons the afflictions at an interval in lifespan or
near the devas is an intermediate attainer of parinirvāṇa. Such a person may
attain parinirvāṇa after reaching the domain, after reaching a perception, or
after reaching a thought; hence three kinds are obtained.

Alternatively, the first attains parinirvāṇa after acquiring the common class-
character in the form-domain; the second after also experiencing divine
prosperity; and the third after entering the devas' recitation of Dharma. One
who attains parinirvāṇa upon rebirth enters a still more excellent recitation,
or, in most cases, attains it immediately upon rebirth after shortening the
lifespan.

None of these explanations corresponds to the similes of the spark and heated
iron, because they lack the relevant distinctions of place and course. On
these accounts an intermediate attainer should also be taught in the
formless-domain, since one could attain parinirvāṇa at an interval in the
lifespan there; but none is taught. The cited verse enumerating lifespan
classes does not do so. Therefore these explanations too are mere invention.

What if the opponents do not recite any of these sūtras? What can now be done,
since the Teacher has attained parinirvāṇa and this leaderless teaching has
split in many ways and continues even today to be divided at will in both text
and meaning? For those who accept this transmission as authoritative,
intermediate existence is nevertheless established by authoritative teaching.

An objection asks about the sūtra statement: “Then Dūṣī Māra fell with his own
body into the great Avīci hell.” How can that be reconciled with an
intermediate phase?

While still alive, he was enveloped by hellish flames. Having died, he reached
Avīci through intermediate existence: that is the intention of the passage.
For exceedingly powerful and complete karmas do not wait for the body to be
laid aside. Thus his karma to be experienced in the present life ripened
first, and the karma to be experienced upon rebirth ripened afterward.

How, then, is the statement to be understood that one who performs and
accumulates the five deeds of immediate retribution is reborn in hell
immediately afterward? Its intention is “without going to another destiny,”
and it indicates that the karma is to be experienced upon rebirth. If the
words are forced literally, they would entail both that one must perform all
five rather than one and that rebirth follows immediately upon the act rather
than after living for an intervening period. Moreover, who denies that an
intermediate being is in the process of being reborn? Through intermediate
existence that being proceeds toward hell immediately after death. We say
that it is facing rebirth, not that it has already arrived there.

What, then, of the verse addressed to the aging brahmin: “Driven onward by old
age, you have come into my presence; there is no lodging for you in between,
and you have no provisions for the road”?

Its intention may be that there is no intermediate lodging among humans.
Alternatively, even in intermediate existence there is no settled lodging,
because it is founded upon movement toward attainment of the rebirth-place.
It is difficult to determine with certainty that one of these alone is the
intended meaning; the same challenge applies to the opponent's reading.
Therefore, since the cited verse does not conflict with either position, it is
not an indicator proving the absence of intermediate existence. A genuine
indicator must leave no alternative interpretation.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The scriptural proof is cumulative and adversarial:

```text
explicit enumeration of seven existences
    opponent rejects the recension

gandharva present at conception
    opponent substitutes “dissolution of aggregates”

Āśvalāyana passage
    tests the substituted reading through class and direction

five non-returners and seven courses
    opponent invents alternate meanings of “intermediate”

alternate meanings tested against similes and the formless-domain

apparent countertexts
    Dūṣī Māra and Avīci
    immediate-retribution karma
    no lodging in between
```

The opponents are not consistently named in this unit; they continue the
school dispute over intermediate existence. Several alternate explanations
are introduced with `eke` or `anye punar āhuḥ`. Vasubandhu's expository voice
tests them for linguistic, classificatory, and contextual coherence.

The remarkable statement about a leaderless and divided teaching is not a
rejection of scripture. It acknowledges that appeal to `āgama` is conditioned
by what a school receives as its authoritative corpus.

## 5. Four Converging Scriptural Grounds

The Kārikā compresses four grounds:

| Ground | Textual force |
|---|---|
| `kaṇṭhokti` | intermediate existence is explicitly enumerated among seven existences |
| `gandharva` | a third being is present for descent into the womb |
| `pañcokti` | one of five non-returners attains parinirvāṇa in the intermediate state |
| `gatisūtra` | three intermediate attainers are distinguished by time, place, and degree |

The argument does not merely count quotations. The Āśvalāyana passage controls
the meaning of `gandharva`: social class and arrival from a direction fit a
being approaching conception, not an abstract dissolution of aggregates.

Likewise, the three fire similes control `antarāparinirvāyin`. The differences
concern progress before arrival. Reinterpreting “Antarā” as the proper name of
devas or as an interval during a deva lifespan fails to account for the stated
differences of course and place.

## 6. Transmission Is Itself Contested

The opponent repeatedly answers, “We do not recite that.” The Bhāṣya reaches
a sober limit:

```text
Teacher no longer present
    ↓
teaching without a single leader
    ↓
school transmissions divided
    ↓
both wording and meaning contested
```

Accordingly, the conclusion is conditional but exact: for those who accept
this `āgama` as authoritative, intermediate existence is established through
authoritative teaching as well as reasoning.

This is not relativism about the causal argument. VAK 3.11 supplied an
independent rational case. It is a recognition that scriptural proof cannot
compel someone who denies every relevant witness belongs to their recension.
The Bhāṣya therefore distinguishes the reach of `yukti` from the reach of
`āgama`.

## 7. Māra, Avīci, and “Immediate” Rebirth

The Dūṣī Māra passage is especially important because its surface wording
says that he fell “with his own body” into Avīci. The Bhāṣya separates two
results:

```text
while alive
    present-life karma ripens
    hellish flames envelop him

after death
    rebirth-result ripens
    intermediate existence reaches Avīci
```

`Avīci` is the hell destiny reached when the corresponding result matures;
the phrase “with his own body” is explained through present-life hellish
affliction before the subsequent rebirth.

The five deeds of immediate retribution are handled by the same semantic
discipline. “Immediately” means without entering another destiny and marks
the karma as ripening upon rebirth. It does not eliminate the destination-
directed intermediate phase. An intermediate being can be “being reborn”
without already being `upapanna`, arrived in rebirth.

The verse denying lodging in between also fails as decisive counterevidence.
`Vāsa`, settled residence, may be denied precisely because intermediate
existence is a directed passage rather than an abode. The Bhāṣya demands that
a genuine negative indicator exclude alternative readings; this one does not.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

The first three compounds complete VAK 3.11's reflection argument. They
should not be translated as new scriptural proofs for intermediate existence:
co-location, continuity, and two-cause structure explain why reflection is an
invalid example.

`Kaṇṭhokteś cāsti` supplies the new predicate: intermediate existence exists
because it is explicitly stated. `Gandharvāt`, `pañcokteḥ`, and
`gatisūtrataḥ` add independent textual grounds.

`Gandharva` is interpreted locally as the intermediate being present at the
conception-complex. The Bhāṣya's argument does not require importing the full
mythological range of Gandharvas into this occurrence.

`Antarāparinirvāyin` must retain the spatial-temporal force of attaining
parinirvāṇa in the intermediate phase. The rival proper-name and lifespan-
interval readings are reported and rejected, not silently omitted.

Finally, the apparent countertexts establish an interpretive rule: a surface
expression such as “immediately” or “no lodging in between” must be tested
against context, doctrinal function, and available alternate readings before
it can negate a systematically established category.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The immediate philosophical result is methodological:

```text
yukti
    tests causal possibility and necessity

āgama
    supplies converging textual determination

textual discrimination
    tests variant readings and rival interpretations
```

Authority is therefore not a brute external command. It becomes effective
through a tradition of reading whose witnesses can be compared, challenged,
and assessed for consequence. Yet the Bhāṣya also recognizes the historical
fracture of that tradition: no textual argument can operate independently of
what has actually been transmitted.

The Dūṣī Māra discussion supplies a restrained lesson for the later learning
pass. Māra is not treated as an omnipotent evil sovereign. He remains a being
within karmic order: powerful karma can afflict his present body and determine
his subsequent Avīci rebirth. The apparently demonic power does not stand
outside the law of result.

That is enough for the translation pass. The Organon may later examine how
counterfeit appearance, karmic immediacy, and transition are related. Here the
Bhāṣya's achievement is exact: neither vivid wording nor sectarian recitation
settles the issue without causal and interpretive discrimination.
