# VAK_3.49 Bhāṣya — Mountains, Continents, and the Outer Boundary

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
aśvakarṇo vinitako nimindharagiris tataḥ /
dvīpā bahiś cakravāḍaḥ sapta haimāḥ sa āyasaḥ // 3.49 //
```

> Then come Aśvakarṇa, Vinitaka, and Mount Nimindhara. Beyond Nimindhara are
> the continents, and beyond them is Cakravāḍa. The seven surrounding
> mountains are golden; that outer boundary is iron.

The Bhāṣya completes the mountain list begun in VAK 3.48, places Sumeru in
the middle, identifies Nimindhara as the outermost surrounding mountain,
locates the four continents beyond it, and describes Cakravāḍa as the outer
enclosure of the four-continent world.

## 2. Continuous Sanskrit

```iast
Aśvakarṇo vinitako nimindharagiriḥ.

Itīme kāñcanamaṇḍalapratiṣṭhā aṣṭau mahāparvatāḥ. Madhye Sumeruḥ. Śeṣāḥ
Sumeruparicāyāvasthitāḥ. Tasyānyasaptaparvataprākāraparikṣiptasya yo
bāhyaḥ parvato Nimindharagiriḥ,

tataḥ

dvīpāḥ.

Tato bahiś catvāro dvīpāḥ. Tebhyaḥ punar

bahiś Cakravāḍaḥ.

Tena cāturdvīpakaś cakrīkṛtaḥ. Teṣāṃ tu

sapta haimāḥ sa āyasaḥ.

Yugandharādayaḥ sapta parvatāḥ sauvarṇāś; Cakravāḍaḥ śāstrakaḥ.
```

The continuous text regularizes punctuation and evident sandhi. The final
word appears as `śastrakaḥ` in the running witness; its intended force is
uncertain. Because the Kārikā's `āyasaḥ` securely identifies Cakravāḍa as
iron, the conventional translation follows that explicit reading while
leaving the damaged prose form visible for later review.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Aśvakarṇa, Vinitaka, and Mount Nimindhara complete the list. These are the
eight great mountains established upon the golden maṇḍala. Sumeru is in the
middle, while the others are positioned in surrounding arrangements around
Sumeru. Of the seven mountain ramparts surrounding it, the outermost is Mount
Nimindhara.

Beyond it are the continents. Outside Nimindhara are the four continents,
and beyond them again is Cakravāḍa. By Cakravāḍa the four-continent world is
encircled.

Of these mountains, the seven beginning with Yugandhara are golden, while
Cakravāḍa is iron.

The next words in the running source, `catūratnamayo meruḥ`, begin VAK 3.50.
The later question concerning the genesis of these materials and structures,
including the Sāṃkhya debate, follows that opening pāda and is therefore not
silently imported into the present translation.

## 4. Natural Movement and Voice

The unit completes the topology in four steps:

```text
three remaining mountain names
    → complete set of eight great mountains

Sumeru in the middle
    → seven surrounding mountain ramparts

outside Nimindhara
    → four continents

outside the continents
    → iron Cakravāḍa enclosing the whole field
```

Vasubandhu speaks directly in descriptive exposition. No separate opponent
or school appears in the exact 3.49 unit.

The material statement closes the description: the seven mountains beginning
with Yugandhara are golden, while the enclosing Cakravāḍa is iron. Sumeru's
distinct four-jewel composition belongs to VAK 3.50.

## 5. Completion of the Eight Great Mountains

The list begun in VAK 3.48 is now complete:

| Position in enumeration | Mountain |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Sumeru |
| 2 | Yugandhara |
| 3 | Īśādhāra in the local witness |
| 4 | Khadiraka |
| 5 | Sudarśana |
| 6 | Aśvakarṇa |
| 7 | Vinitaka |
| 8 | Nimindhara |

The Bhāṣya immediately distinguishes their spatial roles:

```text
Sumeru
    → in the middle

the remaining seven
    → positioned around Sumeru
    → function as successive mountain ramparts

Nimindhara
    → outermost of those seven
```

Thus the count of eight includes the center plus seven surrounding ranges.
It would be incorrect to describe all eight as concentric rings or to include
Cakravāḍa among these eight great mountains.

## 6. Continents and Cakravāḍa

The word `tataḥ`, “from there” or “beyond that,” is resolved by the prose as
an outward movement from Nimindhara:

```text
Sumeru
    → seven surrounding ranges
        → outermost: Nimindhara
            → beyond it: four continents
                → beyond them: Cakravāḍa
```

The Bhāṣya supplies `catvāro dvīpāḥ`, four continents. The Kārikā itself says
only `dvīpāḥ`; the number and exact spatial relation are commentarial
determinations.

`Cakravāḍa` is not merely the next mountain in the same inner series. Its
function is terminal and enclosing: `tena cāturdvīpakaś cakrīkṛtaḥ`, by it
the four-continent world is made circular or encircled. It constitutes the
outer mountain-wall of the local world-system.

The topology is therefore both centered and bounded. “Middle” and “outside”
are not free-standing labels but relations established by the ordered whole.

## 7. Golden Ranges and the Iron Boundary

The final clause distributes two material predicates:

```text
sapta haimāḥ
    the seven [beginning with Yugandhara] are golden

sa āyasaḥ
    that one [Cakravāḍa] is iron
```

The singular pronoun `saḥ` refers to Cakravāḍa. The number seven excludes
central Sumeru and designates the surrounding mountain system. Sumeru's
material character is not left simply “golden”; the following verse gives it
a separate four-jewel composition.

The material contrast also corresponds to different topological functions:

| Structure | Material | Function |
|---|---|---|
| seven ranges from Yugandhara through Nimindhara | gold | concentric inner ramparts |
| Cakravāḍa | iron | outer enclosure of the four-continent world |

The text does not explain the genesis of these materials within the exact
3.49 boundary. That genetic question occurs after the opening of 3.50.

## 8. Translation and Boundary Consequences

The Bhāṣya requires the translation to make three implicit relations
explicit:

- `tataḥ` proceeds outward from Nimindhara to the continents;
- `bahiḥ` then places Cakravāḍa outside the continents;
- “the seven” refers to the mountains surrounding Sumeru, not to the complete
  set of eight and not to the continents.

The local readings `vinitaka` and `nimindhara` are retained. Standardized
forms found in other cosmological witnesses should not replace them before
critical comparison.

The natural commentary boundary is also doctrinally consequential. The
first-pass Bhāṣya Study attached the later Sāṃkhya `pariṇāma` debate to VAK
3.49. In the running source, however, `catūratnamayo meruḥ`, the opening of
VAK 3.50, intervenes before that debate. The polemic must therefore be
studied with 3.50 rather than back-projected into this geographical verse.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

VAK 3.49 completes a relational topology:

```text
center
    → successive surrounding ramparts
        → inhabited regional field
            → terminal outer boundary
```

Each structure receives its identity through several axes at once: name,
material, position, membership in a system, and spatial function. Nimindhara,
for example, is not adequately represented merely as a mountain; it is the
outermost member of the seven golden ranges and the inner boundary relative
to the continents.

For the Organon Dataset, the passage recommends relations rather than a flat
inventory:

```text
Sumeru          role: center
Yugandhara–
Nimindhara      role: surrounding golden ramparts
continents      role: regions outside Nimindhara
Cakravāḍa       role: iron outer enclosure
```

This is an Organon reconstruction, not Vasubandhu's formal vocabulary. Its
purpose is to preserve the configuration by which “inner,” “outer,”
“middle,” and “surrounding” mutually determine one another.

The verse gives a stable spatial result, not yet its genesis. Topology and
production must remain distinct dimensions of analysis. The next Study can
ask how the differentiated materials and structures arise without pretending
that their completed arrangement itself supplies that causal explanation.

## 10. Review Status

This Study restores the exact VAK 3.49 unit at 159.01–159.11: completion of
the eight-mountain list, Sumeru's centrality, the seven surrounding ramparts,
Nimindhara's outermost position, the four continents, the enclosing
Cakravāḍa, and the contrast between golden ranges and an iron boundary.

The Sanskrit and translation remain provisional. The forms `vinitaka`,
`nimindhara`, `paricāyāvasthita`, `cakrīkṛta`, and the damaged final prose
word `śastrakaḥ` require comparison with critical Sanskrit, Tibetan, Chinese,
and established translations.

The Study stops before `catūratnamayo meruḥ`, the opening of VAK 3.50. It
does not include Sumeru's four materials, the coloration of the sky, the
genesis of mountains and continents, the Sāṃkhya transformation debate, or
the mountains' vertical measures.
