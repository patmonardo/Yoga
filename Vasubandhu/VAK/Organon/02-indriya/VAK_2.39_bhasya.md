## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Does non-acquisition also have varieties like acquisition? No,
he says. What, then? “Non-acquisition is unafflicted-indeterminate.”
All non-acquisition is exclusively unobscured-indeterminate. With
respect to time, however, “for past and future dharmas it is
threefold.” There is no present non-acquisition of a present
dharma. For past and future dharmas, non-acquisition belongs to
all three times.

“And for those belonging to the desire realm and so forth, and
for the stainless.” “Threefold” carries forward. Non-acquisition
of desire-realm dharmas belongs to the desire, form, or formless
realm; likewise for form-realm, formless-realm, and uncontaminated
dharmas. There is no uncontaminated non-acquisition. Thus,
“non-acquisition of the path is held to be ordinary-person status.”
The treatise reads: “What is ordinary-person status? Non-obtaining
of noble dharmas.” Non-obtaining means non-acquisition; ordinary-
person status cannot be uncontaminated.

Non-obtaining of which noble dharmas? All of them, because no
distinction is stated. But this means non-obtaining without obtaining.
Otherwise, even a Buddha would be non-noble because of not
possessing the dharmas belonging to the disciple and solitary-buddha
lineages. Then the word “only” should be recited. It need not
be: even single expressions can convey restriction, as in “one
subsisting on water” or “one subsisting on air.”

Others say it is non-obtaining of receptivity to dharma-knowledge
concerning suffering and of what arises together with it. Nor
does association with that non-obtaining entail non-noble status,
since that non-obtaining has been utterly destroyed. But those
dharmas belong to three lineages: non-obtaining of which is meant?
All of them. Then the same fault follows. The same reply applies
again. Then the effort is pointless. The Sautrāntika account
is better. What is their account? “Ordinary-person status is
a continuum in which noble dharmas have not arisen.”

The sentence denying present non-acquisition contains the defective
*nāratyaprāptiḥ*. The translation reads it contextually as
*nāsty aprāptiḥ*, “there is no non-acquisition.” This preserves
the negative and the contrast with the following past/future
case; the research translation instead gives a positive present-
non-acquisition rule, which is not followed here.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The first question distinguishes non-acquisition from acquisition
in ethical classification, followed by temporal and realm rules.
Ordinary-person status then supplies a doctrinal case for the
exclusion of uncontaminated non-acquisition and becomes the focus
of a longer dispute about the exact scope of non-obtaining.

The initial definition is followed by a narrower proposal expressly
attributed to others. Its scope problem brings back the earlier
objection and reply. The passage then explicitly commends the
Sautrāntika account. Unlike several earlier anonymously reported
alternatives, this position has a named attribution and a locally
expressed preference.

## 5. Ethical, Temporal, and Realm Classifications

Non-acquisition is always unobscured-indeterminate in the account
being expounded. It does not take on the ethical character of
the dharma not acquired. Non-acquisition of a wholesome dharma
is therefore not classified as wholesome merely by that relation.

The temporal rule states three-time non-acquisition for past and
future dharmas, while denying present non-acquisition of a present
dharma. It does not say that non-acquisition has no temporal
classification. Nor should its denial be expanded into a claim
that everyone possesses every presently existing dharma: the
continuum restrictions established earlier remain operative.

Realm classification is likewise distinguished from the status
of the dharma concerned. Non-acquisition of dharmas in any of
the three realms, or of uncontaminated dharmas, can belong to
any of the three realms. No non-acquisition is itself uncontaminated.
“Threefold” in this clause concerns realm affiliation, not the
three times of the preceding clause.

## 6. The Scope of Non-Obtaining Noble Dharmas

The definition of ordinary-person status is tested through a
quantificational difficulty. Lacking some noble dharmas cannot
alone establish ordinary-person status: the Buddha does not
possess the distinct lineage-specific dharmas of disciples and
solitary buddhas. The proposed restriction is non-obtaining
without obtaining, not failure to possess every noble dharma
of every lineage.

The examples of subsisting on water or air defend the possibility
of an implicit restrictive sense without an explicit *eva*,
“only.” They concern how the expression is understood; they do
not provide a new account of what non-acquisition is as a dharma.

The Sanskrit's defective *cuddho* is read as *buddho* in this
argument. The contrast among the three lineages makes the
contextual resolution clear, but the transcription remains
visible for later collation.

## 7. The Narrower Proposal and the Sautrāntika Account

The alternative specifies *duḥkhe dharmajñānakṣānti* and its
co-arising dharmas. The compound includes both receptivity and
its companions; the proposal is not limited to a loosely described
“knowledge of suffering.” The subsequent clause denies that
the destroyed non-obtaining returns to make a noble person
non-noble.

The three-lineage question concerns those specified noble dharmas.
It should not be translated as a person whose disposition somehow
admits all three lineages. Since the response again says “all,”
the earlier scope objection and its restriction must be repeated.
The criticism is that the narrower formulation has not avoided
the original interpretive work.

The Sautrāntika formulation then identifies ordinary-person status
through a continuum in which noble dharmas have not arisen.
The local text commends this account rather than merely listing
it neutrally. Nevertheless, the formula is about ordinary-person
status; it should not be expanded into an unrestricted claim
that every technical non-acquisition in every context is simply
non-arising.

The question begins at 65.30. The continuous unit includes the
opening of VAK 2.40 through its ordinary-person-status clause
and the ensuing debate, ending at 66.20. The next question asks
how non-acquisition is relinquished and belongs with the remainder

The source also has defective spacing in the receptivity clause

translation preserves the argument's negation and treats the

The two completed material-acquisition clauses at the beginning

starts with the new question. This preserves sequence without

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The dispute distinguishes lacking a particular determination
from lacking the entire kind of attainment that establishes
noble status. A classification fails if it treats non-possession
of another lineage's dharmas as absence of noble attainment
altogether. The restrictive reply repairs that scope before
the alternative account changes the basis of the definition.

For Organon interpretation, status must therefore preserve both
the relevant range and the continuum's history. The Sautrāntika
formula refers to noble dharmas not having arisen, not simply
to their not being manifest in the present moment. Otherwise
the earlier problem of noble persons with worldly consciousness
would be lost rather than addressed.

The source's preference can be stated without turning the whole
preceding classification into an uncontested Sautrāntika theory.
Classification of a real non-acquisition and definition through
the continuum's condition remain different explanatory accounts.
Their distinction is part of the result of this unit.
