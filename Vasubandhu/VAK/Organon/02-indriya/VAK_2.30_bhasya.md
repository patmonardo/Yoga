# VAK_2.30 Bhāṣya — Indeterminate Consciousness and the Addition of Torpor

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> nivṛte 'ṣṭādaśānyatra dvādaśāvyākṛte matāḥ /
> middhaṃ sarvāvirodhitvād yatra syād adhikaṃ hi tat // 2.30 //

> In obscured-indeterminate consciousness eighteen are accepted;
> in the other indeterminate case, twelve. Because torpor is
> incompatible with none, wherever it occurs it is additional.

The desire-realm scope continues from VAK 2.28–2.29. The unit
ends after the numerical examples of adding torpor, before the
transition to the higher levels.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“In the obscured, eighteen.” In the desire realm, consciousness
associated with the view of a self in the personal aggregate or
with a view grasping an extreme is obscured-indeterminate. There
are eighteen mental factors in it: ten universal great-ground
factors, six affliction great-ground factors, and initial and
sustained examination. As before, the view does not constitute
an addition. “In the other indeterminate case, twelve are accepted.”
The indeterminate other than the obscured is the unobscured-indeterminate.
Twelve mental factors are accepted in it: ten universal great-ground
factors and initial and sustained examination. Teachers of the
outer regions also accept indeterminate regret. According to
them, consciousness associated with it has thirteen factors.

“Because torpor is incompatible with none, wherever it occurs
it is additional.” Torpor is not incompatible with any of the
mental factors described above, since it can be wholesome,
unwholesome, or indeterminate. Hence, wherever it occurs, it
should be understood as additional: where there are twenty-two,
there are twenty-three; where there are twenty-three, there
are twenty-four; and so forth.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The passage completes the desire-realm classification introduced
in VAK 2.28. The unwholesome cases of VAK 2.29 are followed by
the two indeterminate cases. A regional alternative concerning
regret interrupts the main count before the commentary gives
a general rule for adding torpor.

The thirteen-factor regret case is expressly attributed to
*bahirdeśakāḥ*, teachers of the outer regions. The local text
does not name a specific school or identify the region more
precisely. Its count should remain attributed, rather than be
silently merged into the principal twelve-factor account.

## 5. Eighteen and Twelve

| Desire-realm case | Factors counted | Total |
|---|---|---:|
| Obscured-indeterminate | Ten universal, six afflicted, initial and sustained examination | 18 |
| Unobscured-indeterminate | Ten universal, initial and sustained examination | 12 |
| Unobscured-indeterminate with indeterminate regret, according to the outer-region teachers | The preceding twelve plus regret | 13 |

The eighteen retain the six afflicted factors but omit the two
specifically unwholesome factors counted in VAK 2.29. This makes
the distinction between afflicted and unwholesome operational
within the enumeration. Indeterminate consciousness need not be
unafflicted: the obscured case is precisely the counterexample.

The twelve omit the six afflicted factors as well. They do not
thereby acquire the ten wholesome factors of VAK 2.28. Unobscured-
indeterminate and wholesome remain different classifications.
Neither count includes consciousness itself as an additional
mental factor.

## 6. The Two Views and the Alternative about Regret

The two views are *satkāyadṛṣṭi* and *antagrāhadṛṣṭi*. The local
passage names them as the relevant desire-realm obscured-indeterminate
cases without providing their full definitions. The translation
therefore uses descriptive glosses without importing a detailed
later account of their objects and varieties.

“As before” carries forward the explanation that a view is a
particular form of prajñā, already counted among the universal
factors. The view is present, but it does not add a nineteenth
factor. This preserves the distinction between a factor's
particular determination and an additional associated factor.

The outer-region teachers' claim extends regret to the indeterminate
category. In VAK 2.28 the account distinguished wholesome and
unwholesome regret; here an explicitly attributed position allows
a further ethical determination. The numerical consequence,
thirteen, is stated under that position. The passage gives no
argument deciding between the accounts.

## 7. Torpor as a Conditional Addition

The rule for *middha*, torpor, applies across the foregoing
ethical cases. The commentary grounds its compatibility in its
capacity to be wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate. It is
therefore not assigned exclusively to the afflicted class or
the unwholesome class.

Compatibility is not universal occurrence. *Yatra syāt*, wherever
it occurs, qualifies the addition: the count increases by one
when torpor is present. Nor does compatibility with the previously
stated factors mean that all the mutually alternative cases
occur together in one consciousness.

The explicit examples, twenty-two becoming twenty-three and
twenty-three becoming twenty-four, reach back to wholesome
consciousness without and with regret. By the same stated rule,
the eighteen-factor case becomes nineteen and the twelve-factor
case thirteen when torpor occurs. These last two are applications
of the rule, not extra examples supplied verbatim by the prose.

The thirteen obtained by adding torpor to twelve must remain
distinct from the thirteen attributed to the outer-region teachers
when regret is added. Equal numbers do not identify the added
factor or erase the doctrinal qualification on one of the cases.

## 8. Translation and Source Decisions

*Anyatra avyākṛte* is resolved by the prose as the indeterminate
case other than the obscured, hence unobscured-indeterminate.
“Elsewhere” without this restriction would give the numeral twelve
an unjustifiably broad range. “Indeterminate” is the ethical term
*avyākṛta*, not the classification *aniyata*, unfixed, introduced
for further mental factors in VAK 2.27.

“Torpor” continues the rendering of *middha* used in the preceding
studies; “sleepiness” is another possible conventional gloss.
The present passage gives its compatibility and ethical range,
not a full definition or an identification with *styāna*,
sluggishness. The two have distinct places in the enumeration.

The transcription's *aynatra* is read as *anyatra*. Its
*yathoktai ścaitair* is contextually understood as referring to
the mental factors already stated, and *ato tatra tatsyāt* as
the conditional relation made explicit by the kārikā's *yatra syāt*.
The Sanskrit excerpt retains those forms for review; the translation
does not claim an independently established restoration.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage brings together three different grounds for a count:
ethical classification, the identity of an already counted factor,
and the conditional presence of an additional factor. Obscured
and unobscured distinguish the principal sets; view is counted
through prajñā; regret and torpor can supply additions under
the stated conditions.

For Organon interpretation, the rule must retain both its scope
and its attribution. “Thirteen” without its derivation cannot
tell us whether torpor was added under the general rule or regret
under the regional alternative. A faithful reconstruction records
which members are counted and on what account they are admitted.

The compatibility rule also distinguishes possibility from
occurrence. Torpor's availability across ethical classes does
not require it in every instance of those classes. The count
changes upon its occurrence, not merely because its occurrence
is permitted. This completes the desire-realm inquiry while
preparing the next passage's restrictions for higher meditative
levels.
