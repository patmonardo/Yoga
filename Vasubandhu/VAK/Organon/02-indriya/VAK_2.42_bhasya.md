# VAK_2.42 Bhāṣya — The Non-Percipient Attainment

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> tathāsaṃjñisamāpattir dhyāne 'ntye niḥsṛtīcchayā /
> śubhā upapadyavedyaiva nāryasyaikādhvikāpyate // 2.42 //

> Likewise, the non-percipient attainment is in the final dhyāna,
> entered through desire for release. It is wholesome, with its
> result experienced exclusively in the next rebirth; it is not
> for a noble person and is acquired in one time.

The Bhāṣya explains the restricted force of “likewise” and
specifies what acquisition “in one time” means.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

What are the two attainments previously mentioned? The non-percipient
attainment and the attainment of cessation. What is the non-percipient
attainment? Just as the non-percipient condition was described
as “cessation of consciousness and mental factors,” “so too is
the non-percipient attainment.” It is the attainment of the
non-percipient, or a non-percipient attainment. It too is cessation
of consciousness and mental factors. This much is carried forward
by “likewise.” But this attainment is “in the final dhyāna.”
The final dhyāna is the fourth; the attainment belongs to that
level, not another. Why do people enter it? “Through desire
for release.” They consider it an escape and therefore enter
it wishing for liberation.

The non-percipient result, being maturation, is established as
indeterminate. This attainment, however, is “wholesome.” It is
exclusively wholesome, and its maturation among non-percipient
beings comprises five aggregates. Being wholesome, it is
“exclusively experienced in the next rebirth”: not in the present
life or a subsequent later life, nor with unfixed timing. Even
someone who has produced it and then falls away will, it is
said, necessarily produce it again and be born among non-percipient
beings. For this reason one who has obtained it does not enter
the certainty of the noble path. It is accepted only for an
ordinary person, “not for a noble person.” Noble persons do
not enter it, seeing it as a place of downfall. Those who enter
it regard it as escape.

But do noble persons acquire its past and future instances through
obtaining the fourth dhyāna, as with the dhyāna itself? Even
others do not acquire it in that way. Why? Even when previously
practiced, it is accomplished through great formative effort,
and it is without consciousness. Hence “it is acquired in one
time.” This means one temporal mode: only its present instance
is obtained, as with the restraint of individual liberation.
Once it has been obtained, however, from the second moment onward
one also possesses its past instance until one relinquishes it.
Because it is without consciousness, no future instance is cultivated.

The source's *na dṛṣṭadharmaparyāyavedanīyā* is construed as
excluding both present-life and later-life fruition, in contrast
to the explicitly exclusive next-rebirth category. The translation
retains the reportive *kila* in the claim about regaining a lost
attainment and its necessary result.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The opening identifies the two attainments from VAK 2.35 and
takes up the first. The word “likewise” imports a specific feature
from the preceding result: cessation of consciousness and mental
factors. The commentary then differentiates level, motive, ethical
character, maturation, eligibility, and temporal acquisition.

The acquisition question tests whether obtaining the fourth
dhyāna automatically brings this attainment with it. The answer
excludes that inference even for ordinary persons. No school
is named locally; the reportive qualification in the maturation
claim remains part of the exposition rather than being omitted.

## 5. Attainment and Result

The non-percipient attainment is wholesome; the non-percipient
condition described in VAK 2.41 is indeterminate maturation.
“Likewise” does not transfer every determination of the result
to its cause. The author explicitly limits what it carries
forward to cessation of consciousness and mental factors.

The five-aggregate maturation statement must be retained alongside
that cessation. It concerns the resultant existence, whose birth
and death moments were expressly described as percipient in
VAK 2.41. It does not require five manifest aggregates throughout
every moment of the intervening suspension.

Wholesomeness alone does not imply the next-rebirth timing for
every wholesome dharma. Here the prose classifies this particular
attainment and specifies its result. Its ethical character and
its maturation schedule remain separate determinations.

## 6. The Wish for Release and Noble Status

The motive is the wish for liberation because practitioners
regard the attainment as escape. That is a description of their
construal, not the author's endorsement of the attainment as
liberation. Noble persons instead regard it as a place of downfall
and do not enter it.

The claim about one who loses the attainment is stronger than
mere possibility: such a person necessarily regains it and
undergoes the stated rebirth, according to the reported account.
The following exclusion of entry into the certainty of the noble
path is tied to that claim. The passage does not present a
practical recommendation to cultivate the state.

## 7. Acquisition in One Time

The question distinguishes entering an attainment from acquiring
past and future instances through acquisition of its level.
A noble person's non-entry does not by itself answer that second
question. The reply accordingly states that even others do not
acquire it automatically with the fourth dhyāna.

“One time” refers to present acquisition of the attainment,
not a claim that it can occur only once. After it has occurred,
its past instance is also possessed from the second moment onward,
until relinquishment. The statement of present acquisition and
the subsequent possession of the past are therefore compatible.

The final denial concerns cultivation of a future instance under
the technical account of acquisition. It does not mean that
nobody could ever enter the attainment again; the preceding
paragraph explicitly discusses regaining it. The absence of
consciousness and the requirement of great formative effort
explain the restriction in the present account.

## 8. Textual and Translation Decisions

The running transcription includes *niarodha*, *nāryāṃ*,
*atāvanna*, and *dvitaīyādiṣu*. The translation contextually reads
cessation, noble persons, the negative reply concerning others,
and the second and subsequent moments. The excerpt retains
the forms available for review; no independent collation is claimed.

The name-explanation *asaṃjñināṃ samāpattir asaṃjñā vā* offers
two construals. The translation preserves both without expanding
the first into an assertion that rebirth among non-percipient
gods must precede cultivation of the attainment. The causal
sequence stated here is attainment followed by maturation.

“Without consciousness” qualifies the attainment's state, not
the absence of preparatory cognition. The text attributes a
purpose and great formative effort to entering it. Rendering
it as an entirely unintentional lapse would lose those clauses.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage separates cessation's descriptive character from
its ethical, causal, and path determinations. Cessation of mental
activity does not alone establish liberation, and a wholesome
attainment is not thereby an attainment of a noble person.
The distinction between what practitioners seek and what the
account says results is central to the exposition.

For Organon interpretation, the temporal distinction is equally
important: obtaining a present instance, possessing a past instance,
and cultivating a future instance are different relations. A
single statement that the attainment is “available” would erase
precisely what the final question and reply distinguish.

These are the source's determinate contrasts. They do not yet
justify a broader claim that every suspension of mental activity
has the same function or result. The second attainment remains
to be examined on its own terms in the next unit.
