# VAK_2.31 Bhāṣya — Mental-Factor Exclusions at Higher Levels

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> kaukṛtyamiddhākuśalāny ādye dhyāne na santy ataḥ /
> dhyānāntare vitarkaś ca vicāraś cāpy ataḥ param // 2.31 //

> Of these, regret, torpor, and the unwholesome are absent in
> the first dhyāna; in the intermediate dhyāna, initial examination
> is absent as well; beyond this, sustained examination also.

The Bhāṣya specifies the inherited exclusions and further interprets
“also” to include deception and crookedness at the higher levels.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

Taking the rule for mental factors already stated for the desire
realm: “Of these, regret, torpor, and the unwholesome are absent
in the first dhyāna.” Of what has been described, regret and
torpor are entirely absent in the first dhyāna, as is whatever
is unwholesome: hostility; the factors beginning with anger,
except crookedness, intoxication, and deception; and shamelessness
and lack of moral caution. Everything else remains as before.
Those absent in the first dhyāna are also absent “in the intermediate
dhyāna, together with initial examination.” The remainder is
as before. “Beyond this, sustained examination also.” Beyond
the intermediate dhyāna, in the second and subsequent dhyānas
and in the formless levels, what was previously excluded is
absent, as is sustained examination. The word “also” includes
deception and crookedness. The remainder is as before.

For crookedness is recounted only as far as Brahmā, not above,
because it is connected with an assembly. When the monk Aśvajit
asked him in his own assembly, “Where, Brahmā, do these four
great elements cease without remainder?”, he did not know and
made an evasive declaration: “I am Brahmā, the Lord, the Maker,
the Fashioner, the Creator, the Producer, the Father of beings.”
Thus it has been stated how many mental factors occur in which
consciousness at which level.

The series of titles in Brahmā's declaration is transmitted
imperfectly, particularly *sraṣṭā sṛjaḥ*. “Creator, Producer”
is a provisional rendering of that sequence, not an independently
collated restoration.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The passage takes the desire-realm distribution as its point
of departure and specifies successive exclusions. First-dhyāna
exclusions carry into the intermediate level; the subsequent
levels inherit those exclusions and add others. Repeated “the
remainder is as before” preserves the previous classification
where no alteration is stated.

The Brahmā episode supports the upper limit assigned to crookedness.
Within the quoted narrative, the question belongs to Aśvajit and
the titles to Brahmā; the commentary characterizes the response
as an evasion made without knowing the answer. The declaration
is not an assertion of Brahmā's creative authority by the author.
The final sentence closes the mental-factor distribution inquiry.

## 5. The First-Dhyāna Exclusions

Regret and torpor are wholly excluded at this level, along with
whatever is unwholesome. The prose specifies hostility, the
anger-group with three exceptions, and the pair of shamelessness
and lack of moral caution.

The exceptions are crookedness, intoxication, and deception:
*śāṭhya*, *mada*, and *māyā*. They are exceptions to the exclusion
of the anger-group, not exceptions permitting unwholesome dharmas
in a level where the passage has just excluded the unwholesome.
The distinction between afflicted and unwholesome, made explicit
in VAK 2.30, remains necessary here.

The scope is the distribution of factors by level. The presence
of crookedness in the discussion, illustrated through Brahmā,
prevents treating the passage simply as an inventory of factors
simultaneously manifest during a wholesome meditative absorption.
A level's possible consciousnesses and one particular wholesome
attainment at that level are different subjects.

## 6. Intermediate and Higher Levels

| Level | Further exclusions from the preceding distribution |
|---|---|
| First dhyāna, relative to desire realm | Regret, torpor, and the unwholesome factors specified in the prose |
| Intermediate dhyāna | Initial examination, *vitarka* |
| Second and higher dhyānas, and formless levels | Sustained examination, *vicāra*, plus deception and crookedness |

The intermediate dhyāna is the level between the first and second,
not merely an unspecified other dhyāna. The separate removal of
*vitarka* and *vicāra* establishes their different distributions;
the unit does not yet give their detailed definitions.

The prose interprets *api*, “also,” as including deception and
crookedness beyond the intermediate level. It does not include
*mada* in this further exclusion. Consequently, the three
exceptions named at the first level must not all be declared
absent above it. The earlier research study overextends the
exclusion at that point; this study follows the two names actually
specified in the running commentary.

## 7. The Brahmā Example

Crookedness is connected with an assembly, and the story supplies
an instance. Asked a question before his own assembly, Brahmā
does not answer it but recites titles of authority. The commentary
explicitly supplies his lack of knowledge and the evasive character
of the response.

The question concerns where the four great elements cease without
remainder. This excerpt does not give its answer or narrate the
whole encounter. Completing the story from another source would
exceed the present continuous unit.

The direct statement about assembly-dependence names crookedness.
The preceding sentence jointly excludes deception and crookedness
at higher levels, but the local explanation should not be expanded
into a complete theory of the social causes of both factors.
The evidence here has a specific role in the distribution argument.

## 8. Translation and Textual Decisions

*Ataḥ* refers back to the distribution already stated; it is not
an unexplained causal claim that the higher levels follow from
the mere existence of regret or torpor. *Yathāpratiṣiddham* carries
forward what has already been excluded, while “the remainder
is as before” governs the unchanged cases.

The transcription has *catavāri* for the four elements and
*ahamasmin* in the declaration. The translation contextually
reads “four” and “I am,” without claiming an independently
established Sanskrit emendation. The less secure title sequence
is marked after the translation rather than silently presented
as a certain reconstruction.

“Intoxication” continues the provisional rendering of *mada*;
it is not replaced with *māna*, conceit, a different named factor.
Likewise, torpor is *middha*, not the sluggishness *styāna* counted
among the afflicted factors. These distinctions matter to the
specific exclusions.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage determines a distribution through exclusions rather
than through a simple increase of mental powers. Its procedure
requires both the inherited classification and the exact changes
at each level. Removing an entire class or an individually named
factor has a different effect from qualifying a factor already
counted.

For Organon interpretation, the level, ethical character of the
consciousness, and identity of the factor remain separate axes.
First-dhyāna exclusion of the unwholesome does not eliminate every
afflicted possibility, and absence of a factor at a higher level
does not by itself describe every aspect of attainment there.

The Brahmā episode adds a local distinction between status and
knowledge. A claim to authority supplies no answer to the posed
question; the commentary makes the evasion evidence of crookedness.
That argumentative use should remain attached to the source's
particular example rather than become an independent theological
thesis in the translation.
