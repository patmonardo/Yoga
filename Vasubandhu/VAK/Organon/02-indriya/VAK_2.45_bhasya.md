## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

What is life? “Life is lifespan.” For the Abhidharma says,
“What is the life faculty? Lifespan belonging to the three realms.”
But this still does not explain what dharma lifespan is. “It
is the support of warmth and cognition.” The Blessed One said:
“When lifespan, warmth, and cognition leave the body, it lies
cast aside like an insentient piece of wood.” The dharma supporting
warmth and cognition, the cause of their persistence, is lifespan.
What, then, supports lifespan? If these operate in mutual dependence,
which will cease first, so that the other two cease through it?
There would follow perpetual non-cessation.

Then karma supports lifespan, since it continues as far as karma
has projected it. Why not accept karma itself as supporting
warmth and cognition too? Lest all cognition until death be
maturation. Then karma will support warmth, and warmth cognition.
Even so, cognition in the formless realm would lack support,
since warmth is absent. There karma will support it. Such freedom
is not available: one cannot simply make warmth support cognition
in one case and karma in another. The objection has already
been stated. What objection? “Lest all cognition until death
be maturation.” Therefore lifespan exists as the support of both.

We do not say that lifespan does not exist, but that it is not
a distinct entity. What, then? It is the projection, by karma
of the three realms, of the duration of group-commonality. For
as long as karma has projected that it should persist, it persists;
this is called lifespan. It is like the projected ripening-time
of crops or the duration of the flight of a released arrow.
Someone might hold that a particular quality called an impetus
arises in the arrow, through which it moves until it falls.
But on that account, its being one and [not diminishing—the
reading is damaged] would fail to explain differences in the
times of reaching different places at greater speeds, or its
fall. If wind obstructs it, it should fall sooner or never fall,
since no difference in the wind has been specified. Nevertheless,
they maintain that lifespan is a distinct real entity.

Does death occur only through exhaustion of lifespan, or otherwise
too? The Prajñapti states, “Death through exhaustion of lifespan,
not through exhaustion of merit,” in a fourfold analysis. The
first case is exhaustion of karma maturing as lifespan; the
second, of karma maturing as enjoyments; the third, of both;
the fourth, through failure to avoid harmful conditions. Should
relinquishment of lifespan also be stated? It need not be: that
death is through exhaustion of lifespan and falls within the
first case. When lifespan is exhausted, exhaustion of merit
has no further power to bring about death. Thus when both are
exhausted, death is said to be through exhaustion of lifespan.

The Jñānaprasthāna also asks whether lifespan should be said
to continue bound to a continuum, or to persist once arisen.
It answers that the lifespan of desire-realm beings who have
entered the non-percipient attainment or cessation-attainment
should be said to continue bound to a continuum; for those
in attainment and for form- and formless-realm beings, it should
be said to persist once arisen [the transmitted division is
uncertain]. What does this statement mean? The first concerns
what is impaired when its support is impaired, because it depends
on that continuum. The second concerns what has no such impairment
of support and persists as it arose. The Kāśmīra teachers call
the first subject to interruption and the second free of interruption.
Thus untimely death exists.

A sūtra speaks of four acquisitions of embodied existence:
“There is an acquisition of embodied existence in which one's
own intention is effective, not another's.” There are four
alternatives. One's own intention alone is effective for desire-
realm gods lost in play and those corrupted in mind: through
exhilaration and mental hostility they pass away from that state,
not otherwise. Buddhas should also be included, because their
death is self-determined. Another's intention alone is effective
for those in a womb or egg. Both are generally effective for
desire-realm beings. Neither is effective for all intermediate-
existence beings, form- and formless-realm beings, and many kinds
of desire-realm beings: hell-beings, inhabitants of Uttarakuru,
those on the path of seeing or in attainments of loving-kindness,
cessation, or non-percipience; royal seers, messengers of the
Conqueror, and the persons in the following imperfectly transmitted
list, including Dharmila, Uttara, Gaṅgila, the merchant's son
Yaśas, and Kumārajīvaka; all those in their final existence;
a bodhisattva's mother while carrying him; a wheel-turning king;
and his mother while carrying him.

Why, then, does the sūtra answer the question, “Which beings
are subject neither to their own intention nor another's?” with
“Those who have reached the sphere of neither-recognition-nor-
non-recognition, Śāriputra”? It is said that in the other dhyānas
and formless levels one's own intention is the noble path of
one's own level, and another's intention the access to a higher
level; neither is present there. But then a noble path belonging
to another level would count there as another's intention too.
Then, by mentioning the endpoint, the preceding cases are understood.
Sometimes an endpoint is understood through a beginning, as in
“the Brahmakāyika gods: this is the first happy rebirth”; sometimes
the beginning through an endpoint, as in “the Ābhāsvara gods:
this is the second happy rebirth.”

In those passages the expression “for example” reasonably signifies
an example, from which the remaining cases are understood. It
is characteristic of an example that even one instance of that
kind is shown. But that expression is absent here, so the inference
does not carry over. If “for example” were strictly an example-
marker, it would not fit here: “There are beings different in
body and different in recognition, namely humans and certain
gods.” Thus it should be understood as an expression of indication.
Enough of this extended discussion. Life has been explained.

The lifespan quotation has a problematic repetition of “in
attainment”; the translation retains its difficulty rather than
inserting an unmarked negation. The list of beings contains
uncertain names and syntactic attachments. It is preserved in
full in the Sanskrit, and the English names above are provisional.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The definition of life through lifespan prompts a demand for
its explanatory function. Proposed relations among lifespan,
warmth, cognition, and karma are tested before an alternative
defines lifespan as projected duration rather than a distinct
entity. The defender retains the latter position.

The next movement differentiates causes of death, interprets
a treatise's two lifespan descriptions, and records the Kāśmīra
conclusion that untimely death exists. The final fourfold account
of intention's efficacy meets a scriptural scope objection and
ends in a debate about how examples indicate a wider range.
These are connected but distinct arguments, not one uniform
proof of a vital substance.

## 5. Support and Projected Duration

The objection to direct karmic support of cognition is specific:
it would make all cognition until death maturation. The alternative
that interposes warmth then faces the formless case. The text
demands a consistent explanation across the relevant cases,
not arbitrary reassignment of the support.

The critical account denies a separate lifespan-entity, not
lifespan altogether. It grounds duration in karma's projection
of group-commonality. The crop and arrow comparisons illustrate
determined persistence; the arrow objection also tests whether
introducing a distinct impetus merely repeats the explanatory
problem. The damaged clause limits certainty about its wording.

## 6. Exhaustion, Interruption, and Death

The fourfold distinction separates lifespan-maturing karma from
enjoyment-maturing karma, their joint exhaustion, and adverse
conditions. Relinquishment of lifespan is included under the
first case rather than added as a fifth. The comment about both
being exhausted identifies lifespan's exhaustion as decisive;
it does not erase the fourfold comparison.

The later distinction concerns susceptibility to interruption
through injury to the support. “Persists once arisen” must be
read in that context, not as an assertion of permanence. The
Kāśmīra conclusion of untimely death belongs to this doctrinal
account and is not a clinical explanation or practical rule
for predicting an individual's death.

## 7. Intention and the Range of a Scriptural Answer

The next four alternatives concern whether one's own intention,
another's, both, or neither is effective in terminating the
specified embodied existence. They are not four degrees of
moral responsibility. The examples specify exceptional cases
within the account, rather than an unrestricted claim that all
life is under intentional control.

The summit-of-existence quotation appears narrower than the
preceding “neither” list. The proposed interpretation in terms
of paths at one's own or another level meets an objection;
the alternative appeal to an endpoint then meets a linguistic
objection about the absence of “for example.” The concluding
reply gives the expression an indicative force. Preserving those
turns matters: the wider range is argued for, not simply assumed.

## 8. Textual and Boundary Decisions

The source has defects in the warmth/cognition compound, the
mutual-support objection, and the arrow argument. The English
uses *ūṣmavijñānayoḥ*, warmth and cognition, as secured by the
prose. The canonical anchor retains its defective *ūṣmavijñāyoḥ*
with this note. The raw excerpt also preserves *pratipannābhāvāt*,
whose sense in the arrow objection remains uncertain.

The Jñānaprasthāna quotation cannot be treated as a cleanly
established distribution while its repeated attainment wording
remains unresolved. Its ensuing explanation of impairment and
non-impairment is clearer and is translated separately. Likewise,
uncertain proper names in the intention list are not supplied
from imagined parallels.

The explicit *uktaṃ jīvitam* at 75.18 closes the life discussion.
The verse's second half introduces conditioned marks at 75.19;
that full debate is reserved for VAK 2.46. This follows the natural
commentary boundary rather than truncating the life argument
or starting another lengthy inquiry here.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage distinguishes persistence, its causal explanation,
and the postulation of a separate supporting entity. Both sides
must account for lifespan; they disagree about what that account
requires. The critical formula offers projected duration, while
the defender retains a real lifespan-dharma.

For Organon interpretation, the death classifications add conditions
that a bare duration cannot express by itself. Karmic projection,
availability of enjoyments, integrity of support, and intention's
efficacy enter different questions. Their relations must be
specified rather than collapsed into one general principle
called life.

The final debate further shows that a scriptural example's scope
requires argument. Naming an endpoint may indicate a broader
class, but the commentary tests how that inference is licensed.
This interpretive work belongs alongside the causal analysis
in a continuous study of the passage.
