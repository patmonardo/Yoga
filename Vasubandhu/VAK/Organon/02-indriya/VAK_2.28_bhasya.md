# VAK_2.28 Bhāṣya — Wholesome Consciousness and Regret

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> savitarkavicāratvāt kuśale kāmacetasi /
> dvāviṃśatiś caitasikāḥ kaukṛtyam adhikaṃ kvacit // 2.28 //

> Because initial and sustained examination are present, wholesome
> desire-realm consciousness has twenty-two mental factors;
> in some cases regret is additional.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

It must now be stated how many mental factors necessarily occur
in which consciousness. First, desire-realm consciousness is
fivefold: wholesome and unwholesome—the unwholesome being twofold,
isolated or associated with another affliction—and indeterminate,
which is twofold, obscured-indeterminate and unobscured-indeterminate.
Desire-realm consciousness is necessarily accompanied by initial
examination and sustained examination. Hence, “Because initial
and sustained examination are present, in wholesome desire-realm
consciousness there are twenty-two mental factors.” They necessarily
occur: ten universal great-ground factors, ten wholesome great-ground
factors, initial examination, and sustained examination. “In some
cases regret is additional.” Regret is not present in every
wholesome consciousness. Where it is present, adding that factor
makes twenty-three mental factors.

What is this called *kaukṛtya*? *Kaukṛtya* is the condition of
something badly done. Here, however, the dharma that has this
as its object-support is called *kaukṛtya*: regret in consciousness.
Similarly, the gateway to liberation that has emptiness as its
object-support is called “emptiness,” and non-greed that has the
unattractive as its object-support is called “the unattractive.”
In ordinary usage too, designation of the occupants by their place
is observed: “The whole village has arrived,” “The whole country
has arrived.” What is badly done serves as the basis for regret.
Alternatively, this is a figurative application of the cause's
name to the effect, as in the statement, “These six bases of
contact should be understood as former action.”

But how is that which has something not done as its object-support
called *kaukṛtya*? Even what was not done can receive the designation
“done”: “I did not do well in not doing it.” Which regret is
wholesome? That in which one is troubled after failing to do
what is wholesome, or after doing what is unwholesome. By the
reverse, regret is unwholesome. Both kinds thus have a twofold
basis.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The opening question introduces the counts for particular kinds
of consciousness. The fivefold desire-realm division prepares
several cases, but this verse treats only wholesome consciousness.
The count of twenty-two is followed by regret as a conditional
addition, which prompts a longer explanation of the term.

The questions about the name and about omission test how the
technical mental factor relates to what it concerns. Two accounts
of transferred designation are offered, the second marked by
“alternatively.” The unit ends by distinguishing wholesome and
unwholesome regret. No named school or explicit rival doctrine
is introduced here.

## 5. The Fivefold Setting and the Count of Twenty-Two

The opening five are one wholesome case, two unwholesome cases,
and two indeterminate cases. “Isolated” translates *āveṇika*,
whose detailed explanation follows in VAK 2.29; it does not here
mean consciousness without mental factors. The two indeterminate
cases preserve the distinction between obscured and unobscured.

The count in the present verse is ten universal factors plus
ten wholesome factors plus *vitarka* and *vicāra*. Consciousness
itself is not a twenty-third mental factor. Nor are these numbers
counts of the twenty-two faculties examined earlier: the objects
of enumeration have changed to *caitasika* dharmas.

The statement that desire-realm consciousness has initial and
sustained examination supplies the two additions to the already
established classes. Their detailed definitions are not given
in this unit. “Initial examination” and “sustained examination”
remain provisional technical renderings, not a claim that the
two occur as separate temporal stages here.

## 6. The Conditional Addition of Regret

*Kvacit*, in some cases, makes regret additional without placing
it among the factors present in every wholesome consciousness.
The prose explicitly gives twenty-three when it is added to
the twenty-two. This is the local counting rule; further conditions
introduced later must be considered before treating it as an
exhaustive account of every possible addition.

The factor is *cetaso vipratisāraḥ*, regret in consciousness.
It is distinguished from the badly done act or condition serving
as its object-support. Confusing those roles would turn a mental
factor into the external deed about which the factor arises.

The treatment also gives substance to the previous description
of regret as *aniyata*, unfixed. Its occurrence is conditional,
and its ethical character is not fixed as exclusively wholesome.
“Unfixed” did not mean that its occurrence was without conditions.

## 7. Naming by Object, Basis, or Cause

The first explanation names a factor through what it takes as
object-support. The examples of emptiness and the unattractive
preserve the difference between an object and the dharma directed
toward it. The village and country examples then illustrate
naming occupants through their place; the badly done serves as
the basis of regret.

The alternative is explicit: *phale … hetūpacāraḥ*, application
of the cause to the effect in designation. The six contact-bases
receive the name of prior action. Likewise, regret receives a
name referring to what occasions it. The direction is from the
cause's name to the effect, not from the effect's name to the cause.

These explanations clarify technical usage without identifying
the named mental factor with its object or cause. They should
remain alternatives in the translation rather than become one
unqualified theory of how all naming works.

## 8. Omission and the Ethical Direction of Regret

The omission objection asks how “badly done” can name regret
about what was not done. The answer appeals to ordinary evaluative
speech: not doing something can itself be spoken of as doing
badly. The text thus includes omissions without pretending that
the omitted deed actually occurred.

| Regret | Concerning something done | Concerning something not done |
|---|---|---|
| Wholesome | Regret for doing the unwholesome | Regret for failing to do the wholesome |
| Unwholesome | Regret for doing the wholesome | Regret for failing to do the unwholesome |

The final *ubhayādhiṣṭhānam*, twofold basis, follows this paired
analysis of deeds and omissions. The enumeration also shows that
both wholesome and unwholesome conduct can enter the account.
Neither the mere presence of regret nor the ethical character
of an act considered alone determines the classification: whether
it was done or omitted and what is regretted must remain explicit.

The source has mechanical defects in the naming examples, including
*śūnyatālalmvanaṃ*, *vimokṣamukhṃ*, and *aśubhālambanaścālobho*.
The translation reads these contextually as having emptiness as
object-support, a gateway to liberation, and non-greed having the
unattractive as object-support. These are interpretive resolutions;
the continuous Sanskrit retains the transcription for review.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage moves from classification by ground to counting in
a specified consciousness. The prior classes supply necessary
members, while regret supplies a conditional addition. That
addition then requires distinctions among the factor, its object,
its basis, and its ethical determination.

For Organon interpretation, the semantic analysis is inseparable
from the count. One must know what *kaukṛtya* names before adding
it as a mental factor. Naming it through a deed does not turn
the deed into a factor of consciousness; naming an effect through
its cause does not collapse the causal relation.

The ethical account likewise preserves relational determination.
Regret is not made wholesome by self-reproach alone. The stated
classification depends on what was done or omitted and the
direction of the regret concerning it. This is the local result;
a further theory of moral self-knowledge would require additional
argument beyond the present definitions.
