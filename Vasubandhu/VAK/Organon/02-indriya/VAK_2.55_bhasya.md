# VAK_2.55 Bhāṣya — Temporal Causes, Disconnection, and the Unconditioned

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> sarvatragaḥ sabhāgaś ca dvayadhvagau tryadhvagās trayaḥ /
> saṃskṛtaṃ savisaṃyogaṃ phalaṃ nāsaṃskṛtasya te // 2.55 //

> The pervasive and homogeneous causes belong to two times;
> three belong to three times. The conditioned, together with
> disconnection, is result. The unconditioned has neither cause
> nor result.

The continuous unit consolidates the causes' temporal classification
and follows the full debate over the unconditioned through its explicit
close. The assignment of particular results to causes belongs to 2.56.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

What is the temporal restriction of these causes? Their temporal restriction
has been explained in substance, but not formulated in verse; therefore
it is now formulated. “The pervasive and homogeneous causes belong to
two times.” They are past and present only, not future; the reason has
been stated. “Three belong to three times.” The co-arisen, associated,
and maturation causes belong to all three times. Since no temporal
restriction is specified for the conditioning cause, it should be
understood as belonging to all times and also as dissociated from time.
The causes have been explained.

What is the result of which these are causes? “The conditioned, together
with disconnection, is result.” Which dharmas are results? The treatise
says, “Conditioned dharmas and cessation through discrimination.” Then,
since the unconditioned is a result, it should have a cause of which
it is the result; and since it is a cause, it should have a result of
which it is the cause. Only a conditioned dharma has a cause and a
result: “The unconditioned does not have those two.” Why? Because none
of the six causes and none of the five results can apply [as its cause
and its result].

Why is the path not accepted as the conditioning cause of disconnection?
Because that cause is established through non-obstruction of arising,
and the unconditioned does not arise. Whose result, then, is disconnection,
or how is it a result of the path? Because it is acquired through the
path's power. Then only the acquisition should be the path's result,
not disconnection, since its efficacy lies in that acquisition. The
path has one kind of efficacy regarding acquisition and another regarding
disconnection. How regarding acquisition? By producing it. How regarding
disconnection? By bringing one to it. Thus the path is not in any way
a cause of disconnection, yet disconnection is its result.

If it has no predominant result, how is the unconditioned a conditioning
cause? Through not obstructing arising. But it has no result, because,
being outside time, it is incapable of taking and giving a result.

The Sautrāntikas say: the Blessed One nowhere called the unconditioned
a cause; rather, he spoke of causes in an indirect formulation. How?
“The causes and conditions for the arising of material form are themselves
impermanent. How could material form arisen in dependence on impermanent
causes and conditions be permanent?” The statement extends through
consciousness.

Then the unconditioned could not even be an object-condition of
consciousness. It can, because of the qualification “for arising.”
The text says, “The causes and conditions for the arising of consciousness
are themselves impermanent”; it does not say that all conditions of
consciousness are impermanent. But then, since it says only that causes
for arising are impermanent, it does not exclude the unconditioned as
a conditioning cause through mere non-obstruction. The object-condition
is stated in scripture, but a non-obstruction cause is not; thus scripture
does not establish the unconditioned's causal status. Even if it is not
stated, it is not prohibited. And many scriptures have disappeared:
how can one determine that it was never stated?

What is this disconnection? Was it not already called cessation through
discrimination? Earlier, when asked what cessation through discrimination
is, you said “disconnection”; now, when asked what disconnection is,
you say “cessation through discrimination.” This mutually dependent
explanation cannot disclose its nature. Its nature must therefore be
stated otherwise. Its nature is individually known only by noble ones.
This much can be said: there exists a distinct, permanent, wholesome
entity, called both disconnection and cessation through discrimination.

The Sautrāntikas say that all the unconditioned is without substantial
existence. It is not a distinct entity like form or feeling. What is
it, then? Space is merely the absence of tangible obstruction: as when,
finding no obstruction in darkness, people say “space.” Cessation through
discrimination is the cessation of latent afflictions and birth already
arisen, and the non-arising of further ones through the power of
discrimination [provisional construal]. Non-arising through deficiency
of conditions, without discrimination, is cessation without discrimination;
for example, the remainder of a commonality of existence when death
occurs prematurely.

Members of another school say that wisdom has efficacy in preventing
the arising of latent afflictions, and hence this is cessation through
discrimination. But the non-arising of suffering is simply due to the
absence of the latent afflictions that cause its arising; wisdom has
no efficacy concerning that, so it is cessation without discrimination.
Yet that too is not established without discrimination, and is therefore
cessation through discrimination. Others say that the later absence of
something arisen is cessation without discrimination, because it ceases
of itself. On that interpretation, cessation without discrimination
would be impermanent, since it is absent before the thing has perished.

But cessation through discrimination would also be impermanent, since
discrimination precedes it. Discrimination does not precede it in that
way: discrimination does not first occur and then the non-arising of
unarisen dharmas. Their non-arising is already there. Rather, dharmas
that would arise without discrimination do not arise once discrimination
has arisen. Its efficacy here is to establish prevention of arising
for dharmas whose arising had not been prevented.

If nirvāṇa is simply non-arising, how is this scriptural statement
interpreted: “These five faculties, practiced, cultivated, and developed,
lead to the abandonment of past, future, and present suffering”? Abandonment
is nirvāṇa, but only future suffering can fail to arise, not past or
present suffering. That is so; but suffering is said to be abandoned
through abandoning afflictions that take it as their object. The Blessed
One said: “Abandon desire and passion for form. When desire and passion
are abandoned, that form will be abandoned by you and fully known,”
and so on through consciousness. Thus abandonment of suffering in all
three times is appropriate. Even if abandonment of past, future, and
present affliction were stated, the same reasoning would apply.

Alternatively, “past affliction” may mean affliction belonging to a
previous birth, and “present affliction” that belonging to this birth,
as in the exposition of craving's courses, where eighteen courses
“with reference to the past” concern a past birth, and likewise for
the present. These two kinds of affliction have deposited a seed-condition
in this continuum for future arising. Through abandoning that seed,
those afflictions too are abandoned, just as karma is exhausted through
the exhaustion of its maturation. Abandonment of future suffering or
affliction is its absolute non-arising because the seed is absent.
Otherwise, what is to be abandoned in the past and present? Effort
concerning what has ceased or is about to cease would have no purpose.

If the unconditioned does not exist at all, how is the Blessed One's
statement understood: “Of all dharmas, conditioned or unconditioned,
dispassion is declared foremost”? How can something nonexistent be
foremost among nonexistent things? We do not say that the unconditioned
does not exist at all. It is of the kind we have explained. We say,
“There is the prior absence of a sound; there is its subsequent absence,”
without thereby establishing absence as a positive entity. The unconditioned
should be understood likewise. An absence can also be supremely praiseworthy:
the absolute absence of every trouble deserves to be praised as foremost,
to incline those to be trained toward it.

If the unconditioned were merely absence, cessation could not be a noble
truth, since there would be nothing there. What does “truth” mean? Does
it not mean being without error? Noble ones see both without error:
suffering as suffering and the absence of suffering as absence. What
conflict is there with its being a noble truth? How can absence be the
third noble truth? Its being a noble truth has been explained; it is
third because it is seen and taught immediately after the second.

If the unconditioned were merely absence, consciousness taking space
or nirvāṇa as object would have a nonexistent object. We shall examine
this in the inquiry into the existence of past and future things.

What if the unconditioned were accepted as a substance? What would follow?
The Vaibhāṣika position would be preserved. The deities will preserve
it if they consider it worth preserving; but something unreal would
have been imagined. Why? Its nature is not apprehended as that of form
or feeling is, nor its function as that of the eye and the other faculties
is. How could the genitive relation “the cessation of this thing” be
established? It has no relation to that thing through cause and result
or the like. Mere negation is appropriate: “the absence of this.”

Even if it is a distinct entity, the cessation acquired through severing
the acquisition of a particular affliction is designated as that
'affliction's cessation.' What, then, determines the restriction of
its acquisition? Scripture speaks of “a monk who has attained nirvāṇa
in this very life.” How could absence be attained? Through obtaining
the counteragent, one obtains a support absolutely opposed to renewed
arising of affliction and rebirth; hence nirvāṇa is said to be attained.

Scripture too indicates mere absence. It speaks of the complete abandonment,
relinquishment, disappearance, exhaustion, dispassion, cessation, calming,
and vanishing of suffering, and the non-reconnection, non-arising, and
non-appearance of further suffering. “This is peaceful, this is excellent:
the relinquishment of all bases of appropriation, the exhaustion of craving,
dispassion, cessation, nirvāṇa” [the quotation's transcription is damaged].

Why not understand “non-appearance” as “nothing appears in it”? We find
that locative ineffective. What does “does not appear in it” mean? If
it means “when it exists,” non-appearance would follow permanently,
since nirvāṇa is permanent. If it means “when it is attained,” then
let suffering's non-appearance be admitted when that very thing through
which its acquisition is posited becomes present or is attained.
The illustration is then well applied: “The liberation of his mind
is like the extinguishing of a lamp.” As a lamp's extinguishing is
absence, so too is the Blessed One's liberation of mind.

The Abhidharma also says: “Which dharmas are without a basis? Unconditioned
dharmas.” That means without an entity, without a nature. That is not
its meaning. What is it, then? “Basis” has five senses: intrinsic nature,
as in “One possesses that entity which has been acquired”; object,
as in “All dharmas are to be known by knowledge in accordance with
the object”; the basis of bondage, as in “Where one is bound by the
fetter of attachment, is one also bound there by the fetter of aversion?”;
causal basis, as in “Which dharmas have a basis? Conditioned dharmas”;
and property, as in “field-property” and “house-property.” Here “basis”
means cause. Therefore the unconditioned does exist substantially,
say the Vaibhāṣikas; it simply has neither cause nor result. Enough
of this discussion.

## 4. Time, Causal Status, and Result

The temporal opening consolidates the previous discussions. Homogeneous
and pervasive causes must have arisen; co-arisen, associated, and
maturation causes admit future status. The conditioning cause includes
both temporal dharmas and the unconditioned under its broad non-obstruction
account. This does not place the unconditioned in a fourth temporal period.

The kārikā's *te*, “those two,” refers to cause and result, as the prose
expressly establishes. It does not mean merely “those results are not
unconditioned”: disconnection has just been included among results.
The research translation's initial formulation obscures this distinction.

## 5. Producing Acquisition and Attaining Disconnection

The initial defense separates the production of acquisition from bringing
one to disconnection. Calling disconnection a result of the path therefore
does not make it a produced dharma. The path has efficacy in both
relations, but the efficacy is differently determined.

This defense belongs to the account that treats cessation as a distinct
unconditioned entity. The later Sautrāntika account instead explains
attainment through a support rendered opposed to renewed affliction
and rebirth. These accounts should not be merged into a single theory
of producing acquisition and revealing an independently existent absence.

## 6. Cessation, Absence, and Noble Truth

The critique of circular definition demands an account beyond two
interchangeable names. The response appeals to noble realization and
a distinct permanent wholesome entity; the Sautrāntika alternative
explains the three unconditioned dharmas through absence or non-arising.
The permanence-and-wholesomeness claim here concerns cessation through
discrimination, not every unconditioned dharma indiscriminately.

The absence account expressly denies that it means sheer nonexistence
of the unconditioned. It distinguishes the truth of an absence from
the existence of an additional positive entity. Its defense of the
third noble truth turns on non-erroneous knowledge, while its account
of abandonment distinguishes ending a seed-condition from trying to
undo what has already happened.

The alternative school accounts of the two cessations remain separate.
Neither the identification of suffering's non-arising with cessation
without discrimination nor the identification of that cessation with
ordinary destruction is allowed to pass without objection.

## 7. Scriptural Scope and the Final Defense

The causality dispute distinguishes scriptural evidence for productive
conditions, evidence for object-conditions, and the claimed absence
of evidence for non-obstruction as a cause. The reply concerning lost
scriptures is part of the debate and does not establish a recovered
scriptural statement.

The final exchange turns on the senses of *vastu*. The Vaibhāṣikas
interpret *avastuka* as lacking a causal basis, rather than lacking
substantial existence. All five senses and their examples are retained
because the reply depends on that lexical distinction. The discussion
ends with this defense, after the sustained Sautrāntika critique and
its irony; it does not end in an explicitly agreed resolution.

## 8. Textual and Translation Decisions

The unit covers 90.23–94.17 and ends with *gataṃ tāvad etat*. The
cause-to-result assignments beginning at 94.18 introduce VAK 2.56
and are reserved, though the research 2.55 file includes them.
The running Sanskrit is preserved apart from source labels and spacing.

At 91.11, the witness's *prapaṇāt* is rendered contextually as “by
bringing one to it,” corresponding to attainment rather than production.
The research rendering “reveals or brings about” can obscure that
contrast; no separate theory of disclosure is inferred from this word.
At 91.14–15, the scope of *paryāyeṇa* is read with the following scriptural
argument, not as a Sautrāntika concession that the unconditioned itself
is an indirect cause.

At 92.06–07, *utpannānuśayajanmanirodhaḥ* is compressed; the translation
marks the construal involving arisen latent afflictions and birth as
provisional. At 92.09–10, the witness reads *anuśayānām utpattau*,
“in the arising of latent afflictions,” where the argument requires
preventing their arising. The English explicitly follows that contextual
sense; the negative must be restored by collation, not silently in
the Sanskrit. The damaged */pratisaṃkhyānirodhaḥ* at 92.07–08 is read
as cessation without discrimination from its explicit definition.

The long scripture quotation at 93.27–94.02 contains *svalpasya*,
*srvopādhipraniniḥ sarga*, and other defects. The English preserves
its enumerated actions but does not treat its reconstructed opening
or exact wording as secure. *Kṣatravastu* in the property example is
provisionally construed as field-property. The anomalous source label
near 94.11 is omitted with the other labels and does not reorder the
fivefold explanation. No manuscript collation is claimed.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

As Organon interpretation, the passage makes the demand for causal
intelligibility confront its own scope. A produced acquisition, a
liberative attainment, an object of knowledge, and a non-obstructing
condition cannot all be understood through one undifferentiated
production relation.

The pedagogical force is especially clear in the objection to circular
names: repeating “disconnection” and “cessation through discrimination”
does not yet disclose what is understood. The subsequent alternatives
must explain what changes through the path, what is attained, and what
is truthfully known. This supports our inquiry into learning without
settling the ontology in advance. The text preserves opposed accounts
of precisely those questions.
