# VAK_2.18 Bhāṣya — Further Relations of Necessary Possession

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> caturbhiḥ sukhakāyābhyāṃ pañcabhiś cakṣurādimān /
> saumanasyī ca duḥkhī tu saptabhiḥ strīndriyādimān // 2.18 //

> One possessing pleasure or body necessarily possesses four faculties;
> one possessing the eye and so forth, five, as does one possessing
> gladness. One possessing pain, however, possesses seven; one
> possessing the female faculty and so forth, eight.

“Eight” is supplied by *aṣṭābhiḥ*, the opening word of VAK 2.19.
The reading and translation include its explanation through the
completion of the eight-faculty cases. The next study begins with
the eleven- and thirteen-faculty realization cases.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

“In the cases of pleasure and body, four.” One possessing the
pleasure-faculty necessarily possesses four faculties: the three
already mentioned, beginning with neutral feeling, and the
pleasure-faculty. One possessing the body-faculty likewise possesses
four: those three and the body-faculty. “One possessing the eye
and so forth, five.” One possessing the eye-faculty necessarily
possesses five: neutral feeling, life, mind, body, and that faculty
itself. The same is to be understood for ear, nose, and tongue.

“And one possessing gladness.” One possessing the gladness-faculty
also necessarily possesses five: neutral feeling, life, mind,
pleasure, and gladness. With which pleasure-faculty is someone born
in the second dhyāna, who has not attained the third dhyāna, endowed?
With an afflicted one belonging to the level of the third dhyāna.
“One possessing pain, however, seven.” One possessing the pain-faculty
necessarily possesses seven: body, life, and mind, with four
feeling-faculties.

“One possessing the female faculty and so forth, eight.” One
possessing the female faculty necessarily possesses eight: those
seven together with the female faculty. “And so forth” includes
the male faculty, distress, and those beginning with faith. A
possessor of any of these likewise possesses eight in each case:
those seven with the male faculty as the eighth, and similarly
with distress. One possessing a faculty beginning with faith,
however, possesses those five together with neutral feeling,
life, and mind.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The passage continues the possession question from VAK 2.17.
It distributes four, five, seven, and eight across different
possessed faculties and states the members required in each case.
The question about someone born in the second dhyāna tests the
inclusion of pleasure in the gladness-holder's set.

No school or opponent is named. The question receives a short
technical answer rather than a developed explanation of the
mechanism of possession. That difference in argumentative scale
should be retained.

## 5. The Four-, Five-, and Seven-Faculty Cases

The common triad is neutral feeling, life, and mind. The counts
extend it differently:

| Given possession | Necessarily possessed set | Count |
|---|---|---:|
| Pleasure | Triad plus pleasure | 4 |
| Body | Triad plus body | 4 |
| Eye, ear, nose, or tongue, considered separately | Triad, body, and the named faculty | 5 |
| Gladness | Triad, pleasure, and gladness | 5 |
| Pain | Body, life, mind, pain, pleasure, gladness, and neutral feeling | 7 |

The first clause distributes four over two separate cases. It
does not require possession of both pleasure and body in a single
four-member set. Similarly, “eye and so forth” gives a separate
five-member set for each of the four faculties named in the prose.

The pain-holder's four feelings are pain, pleasure, gladness,
and neutral feeling. Distress is not a necessary addition from
possession of pain alone. The continuation confirms this by
adding distress separately as an eighth faculty.

## 6. The Second-Dhyāna Question

The gladness count entails possession of pleasure. The questioner
therefore specifies a person born in the second dhyāna who has
not attained the third: what pleasure-faculty can be possessed?
The answer is an afflicted pleasure-faculty belonging to the third
level.

The reply keeps three determinations distinct: realm or level
of birth, meditative attainment, and possession of a faculty
qualified by its level and afflicted status. It does not say
that the person has attained the third dhyāna after all, or that
pleasure and gladness are simultaneously manifest.

The text gives no further explanation of how this possession
is established. Calling it simply a latent faculty could suggest
a theory of latency not stated in the answer. The controlled
claim is the one made: possession of the specified afflicted
third-level pleasure despite non-attainment of that dhyāna.

## 7. Equal Counts with Different Members

For the female and male faculties and distress, the eight consist
of the previously specified seven plus the named faculty. This
seven-member basis is body, life, mind, and the four feelings;
it is not a set of seven sensory organs.

The faith-group has a different composition. Possession of any
one of faith, vigor, recollection, concentration, or prajñā entails
possession of all five and the common triad. It therefore also
counts eight without adding the seven-member pain-holder's set.
The contrastive *tu* in the prose makes that difference visible.

These counts state necessary accompanying possessions. They do
not assert that no other faculties can be possessed, and equal
counts do not make the sets interchangeable. The relation is
established for the specified possessor in each case.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for Translation

The predicate “necessarily possesses” carries forward the inquiry
and is repeated by *avaśyam* in the prose. Rendering it as “currently
experiences” would change the question, especially in the gladness
and pain cases where several feeling-faculties are counted together.

*Ādi* has locally specified ranges. After eye, the prose includes
ear, nose, and tongue as parallel cases. After the female faculty,
it includes male, distress, and the five beginning with faith.
The same abbreviation must not be expanded by one fixed list.

The cross-verse *aṣṭābhiḥ* completes the final subject of VAK 2.18.
Including its prose here avoids leaving “and so forth” undefined.
The eleven- and thirteen-faculty cases remain for VAK 2.19.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage makes possession relational: stating that one faculty
is possessed can entail a determinate set of others. Those sets
are sensitive to the faculty named. The same numeral can express
different necessary relations, while possession can extend beyond
what is presently manifest or meditatively attained.

For Organon reconstruction, the count should therefore be accompanied
by its members, its initiating condition, and the relation under
which they are counted. Four for pleasure differs from four for
body; eight for distress differs from eight for faith. A numerical
summary alone would lose the determination.

This does not yet establish a causal account in which each member
produces every other. Necessary co-possession and causal dependence
are different claims. The Bhāṣya establishes the former here,
with the second-dhyāna question clarifying its scope.
