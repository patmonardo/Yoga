# VAK_2.41 Bhāṣya — Commonality and the Non-Percipient Result

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> sabhāgatā sattvasāmyam āsaṃjñikam asaṃjñiṣu /
> nirodhaś cittacaittānāṃ vipākaḥ te bṛhatphalāḥ // 2.41 //

> Commonality is similarity among beings. The non-percipient
> condition among non-percipient beings is cessation of consciousness
> and mental factors, a maturation-result. They are Bṛhatphala.

The commentary treats both formations, including the full debate
over commonality and the account of birth and death among the
non-percipient beings.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

What is commonality? “Commonality is similarity among beings.”
Commonality is an entity: the resemblance among beings, called
“group-commonality” in the treatise. It is undifferentiated and
differentiated. Undifferentiated commonality is the commonality
of all beings as beings, since it occurs in each and every being.
Differentiated commonality is restricted according to differences
of realm, level, destiny, mode of birth, kind, female or male,
lay follower or monk, trainee or beyond training, and so forth.
There is also commonality of dharmas according to aggregates,
sense-bases, and dhātus. If commonality of beings did not exist
as an undifferentiated entity, there would be no undifferentiated
cognition and designation “being” among beings distinguished
by their individual differences. The same should be applied
to cognitions and designations of aggregates and so forth.

Can someone die and be reborn without relinquishing or acquiring
commonality of beings? There are four alternatives. The first
is someone reborn where they died. The second is someone entering
the certainty of the noble path: that person relinquishes
ordinary-person commonality and acquires noble commonality.
The third is a transition from one destiny to another. The
fourth excludes these cases.

If ordinary-person commonality exists as an entity, what need
is there for ordinary-person status as well? Humanity is not
posited as something other than human commonality. Moreover,
people do not see commonality, since it is immaterial, nor do
they distinguish it by prajñā; yet they recognize sameness of
kind among beings. Even if it exists, what activity does it
perform in that recognition? Why is commonality of non-sentient
things not also accepted? Rice, barley, mung beans, black gram,
mango, jackfruit, iron, gold, and so forth resemble others of
their own kind. And how are those commonalities, distinct from
one another, designated without distinction as “commonality”?

This also lends support to the Vaiśeṣikas. Their doctrine too
is that there exists a category called the universal, through
which a common cognition arises even concerning things of different
kinds. Their distinctive claim is that one such universal occurs
in many things. Whether they are supported or not, the Vaibhāṣikas
say, this commonality exists because it is stated in a sūtra.
For the Blessed One said, “If he comes to this state, to commonality
with human beings.” That was said, but it was not said to be
a distinct entity. What, then, is it? Those very formations
in such a condition that the designation “human” and so forth
applies to them, as with commonality among rice and so forth.
But they do not accept this account.

What is the non-percipient condition? “Among non-percipient beings,
it is cessation of consciousness and mental factors.” The cessation
of consciousness and mental factors in those born among the
gods called non-percipient beings is the entity termed the
non-percipient condition. Through it, future consciousness and
mental factors are held back for an interval and cannot arise,
as river water is dammed. It is exclusively “maturation.” Maturation
of what? Of the non-percipient attainment. Which beings are those
among whom non-percipient beings occur? “They are Bṛhatphala.”
Among the gods called Bṛhatphala, some in a particular region
are non-percipient beings, as with those of the intermediate
dhyāna.

Do they ever become percipient? They do at birth and at death.
The sūtra reads: “After remaining for a very long time, with
the arising of recognition those beings pass away from that
place.” Passing away from there, like people waking from a long
sleep, they are reborn in the desire realm, nowhere else. Because
the formative force of their earlier attainment is exhausted
and no new one has accumulated, they fall like arrows whose
impetus is spent. Whoever is to be born there necessarily has
desire-realm karma to be experienced in a later life, just as
inhabitants of Uttarakuru have karma to be experienced as rebirth
among gods.

The transcription runs the differentiated-being and dharma-commonality
clauses together. Their division in the translation is contextual,
following the subsequent separate references to beings and to
aggregates and so forth. The scriptural phrase introducing human
commonality is also imperfectly transmitted; no independently
collated restoration is claimed.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

Two formations are treated. Commonality receives a definition,
subdivisions, an argument from common cognition, a fourfold
comparison, and a sustained ontological dispute. The non-percipient
condition receives an explanation of its operation, maturation
cause, location, and termination.

The Vaibhāṣikas are explicitly named in the commonality defense.
Their sūtra appeal is followed by a critical distinction between
using the term and asserting a separate entity. The source then
records that they reject the alternative account. This closing
disagreement is absent from the immediate research translation
but retained here with the preceding Vaiśeṣika comparison.

## 5. Commonality and Common Cognition

The initial account distinguishes commonality shared by all
beings from commonalities restricted by particular classifications.
It also invokes commonality of dharmas through aggregates,
sense-bases, and dhātus. Its argument is that common cognition
and designation among different individuals require the relevant
real commonality.

The critic asks what work that entity performs when ordinary
recognition occurs without its being separately apprehended.
The challenge concerning non-sentient kinds tests the restriction
to beings; the question about designating distinct commonalities
as commonality tests the explanatory account at another level.
Neither question simply denies that things can resemble one
another.

The Vaiśeṣika comparison preserves a difference: the universal
is one while occurring in many. The passage therefore does not
identify the two systems without qualification. The critical
alternative grounds common designation in the formations as
thus constituted, while the Vaibhāṣikas retain a distinct entity.

## 6. The Four Alternatives

The comparison has two predicates: death and rebirth, and change
of the relevant commonality. Rebirth in the same class supplies
the first without the second. Entry into the noble path supplies
the second without the first. Transition between destinies supplies
both; the residual case supplies neither.

The “same place” wording is thus read in the classificatory
context, not as a geographical address. The noble-path case
also distinguishes relinquishing ordinary-person commonality
from dying. That distinction motivates the next objection about
its possible duplication of ordinary-person status, discussed
in VAK 2.39–2.40.

## 7. The Non-Percipient Condition

The condition is described as a cessation of both consciousness
and mental factors, not merely the absence of the factor recognition
while other mental activity continues. The dam analogy explains
the prevention of future arising for an interval within the
account being expounded.

The condition is maturation of the non-percipient attainment.
Cause and result remain distinct: the attainment will be examined
in VAK 2.42. Bṛhatphala names the divine class within which a
particular region contains the non-percipient beings; the source
does not identify every Bṛhatphala being as non-percipient.

The stated birth and death exceptions mean that the suspension
is not an unqualified absence of consciousness throughout every
moment of that existence. Nor is its long duration liberation:
the commentary describes its ending and a subsequent desire-realm
rebirth.

## 8. Translation and Source Decisions

The source's *sākṛśyaṃ*, *skndha*, and *satvasttva* are read
contextually as resemblance, aggregates, and the repeated designation
“being.” The damaged clause naming the non-percipient gods and
the later recognition clause are rendered according to their
clear question-and-answer structure. The excerpt retains the
transcription for review.

“Non-percipient” renders the *asaṃjñin/āsaṃjñika* terminology;
“recognition” retains the rendering of *saṃjñā* established in
VAK 2.24. The prose itself specifies the wider cessation of
consciousness and mental factors, so the conventional class-name
must not narrow that account.

The arrow comparison concerns exhaustion of the earlier attainment's
formative force and lack of new accumulation. The following karma
statement specifies desire-realm karma awaiting a later life;
it does not say that the non-percipient maturation itself becomes
a new act producing that rebirth.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

Commonality poses a question about the grounds of classification:
does recognition of sameness require a separate commonality-entity,
or can the formations in their determinate condition ground
common designation? The dispute preserves both the fact of
classification and disagreement about its explanation.

For Organon interpretation, shared naming, resemblance, and a
postulated real universal must remain distinct claims. The source's
reply to the sūtra citation is precise: a term's scriptural use
does not alone establish a separate entity answering to it.
The rejected alternative must nevertheless remain attributed,
not become an unqualified consensus.

The non-percipient case asks a different explanatory question:
how a conditioned interval of suspended mental arising is grounded
in an earlier attainment and ends when its force is exhausted.
Its inclusion in the same list of dissociated formations does
not make its function identical with commonality. Each formation
requires its own argument and determination.
