# VAK_2.17 Bhāṣya — Necessary Possession of Neutral Feeling, Life, and Mind

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> ekādaśabhir arhattvam uktaṃ tv ekasya saṃbhavāt /
> upekṣājīvitamanoyukto 'vaśyaṃ trayānvitaḥ // 2.17 //

> Arhatship is nevertheless said to be attained through eleven,
> because that is possible for one person. One possessing any one
> of neutral feeling, life, and mind necessarily possesses all three.

The first half and its discussion were translated with the complete
fruit-attainment unit in VAK 2.16. This study begins with the new
inquiry into possession. The anchor reads *upekṣā* with the running
witness rather than the research kārikā's shortened *upekṣa*.
“Any one” is explicit in the commentary's *anyatamena*.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

This is now examined: one possessing which faculty necessarily
possesses how many faculties? “One possessing neutral feeling,
life, or mind necessarily possesses the three.” Whoever possesses
any one of these, beginning with neutral feeling, necessarily
possesses three faculties—these very three. For none of them is
possessed without the others. With the remaining faculties there
is no fixed rule: one may possess them or may not.

First, one born in the formless realm does not possess the eye,
ear, nose, or tongue faculties. In the desire realm, one also
lacks them when they have not been acquired or have been lost.
One born in the formless realm does not possess the body-faculty.
One born in the form or formless realm does not possess the female
faculty; in the desire realm, one lacks it when it has not been
acquired or has been lost. The same applies to the male faculty.

An ordinary person born in the fourth dhyāna or the formless realm
does not possess the pleasure-faculty. Likewise, it is an ordinary
person born in the third or fourth dhyāna or the formless realm
who does not possess the gladness-faculty. One born in the form
or formless realm does not possess the pain-faculty. One free from
desire for the desire realm does not possess distress. One whose
wholesome roots have been severed does not possess the faculties
beginning with faith.

Ordinary persons and those established in the fruits do not possess
the faculty “I shall know.” Ordinary persons and those established
on the paths of seeing and no-more-training do not possess the
knowledge-faculty. Ordinary persons and trainees do not possess
the faculty of one who has known. In conditions not excluded,
one should understand possession of the respective faculty as stated.

## 4. Movement and Voice of the Commentary

The passage changes the predicate from attainment through faculties
to possession of faculties. It first establishes a necessary relation
among three, then explains why possession of the remaining faculties
is not fixed merely by possession of one of those three.

The explanation proceeds through conditions of non-possession:
realm of rebirth, non-acquisition or loss, ordinary-person status,
dispassion, severed wholesome roots, and path-position. The final
sentence directs the reader to the complementary cases. There is
no named school or opponent in this unit.

## 5. What the Necessary Triad Establishes

The statement is reciprocal: possession of neutral feeling entails
possession of life and mind; possession of either of the latter
entails possession of the other two. The three are not merely
frequently associated. *Avaśyam*, necessarily, and the denial of
possession of one without another give the claim its force.

The count is a necessary accompaniment, not an assertion that
exactly three faculties and no others are possessed. The prose
immediately allows the remaining faculties to be present or absent
under their stated conditions. “No fixed rule” is relative to this
initial inference, not a denial of every further rule governing
those faculties.

Nor does possession of the neutral-feeling faculty assert an
actually occurring neutral feeling at every moment. The question
is *samanvāgama*, possession or endowment, rather than simultaneous
manifestation. Reading it as an event-count would wrongly erase
the distinction between neutral feeling and other feelings already
established in VAK 2.07–2.08.

## 6. The Conditions of Non-Possession

The material-faculty cases distinguish absence through realm from
absence through failure to acquire or subsequent loss. Those are
different histories leading to the same negative answer concerning
possession. The text does not identify either with a faculty that
is possessed but not currently operating.

The pleasure and gladness sentences additionally specify an ordinary
person. That qualification must remain: the text does not extend
these exclusions to every person born at those levels. It also
distinguishes the thresholds: fourth dhyāna for pleasure, third
and fourth for gladness, with the formless realm included in both.

Distress is excluded through freedom from desire for the desire
realm. The five beginning with faith are excluded through severance
of wholesome roots. These conditions should not be replaced by
realm alone or by a judgment about a person's current mood.

## 7. Possession of the Realization Faculties

The final three exclusions concern ordinary-person, fruit, trainee,
and path statuses. In the provisional reading, ordinary persons
and those established in fruits lack the first faculty; ordinary
persons and those on seeing or no-more-training lack the second;
ordinary persons and trainees lack the third.

The phrase “established in fruits” must retain its scope. It does
not automatically say that anyone who has ever attained a fruit
can never again stand in another path-relation. Similarly, being
excluded from the knowledge-faculty here does not mean lacking
all knowledge: *ājñendriya* is a specific realization designation.

The damaged compounds are material to these statements. The research
translation supplies a coherent reading, but coherence does not
substitute for a verified Sanskrit restoration. The source difficulty
therefore remains recorded alongside the translated exclusions.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for Translation

*Samanvāgata* is rendered “possessing” throughout to distinguish
it from *prāpta*, attained, and from actual arising or operation.
The verse's *yukta* and *anvita* are resolved through the same
possession-language in the prose. The present passage does not
supply a full theory of the relation that establishes possession.

*Apratilabdha* and *vihīna* distinguish not acquired from lost.
*Aniyama* is “no fixed rule” for the remainder given the initial
triad, while the concluding exclusion rule qualifies how the
remainder is to be assessed.

The classification concerns the bearer's conditions as well as the
faculty named. “Born in a realm,” “an ordinary person,” “free from
desire,” and “established on a path” are not interchangeable ways
of stating one status.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The central determination is a necessary relation of possession
that leaves further possessions variable. The commentary then
makes that variability intelligible by specifying exclusions.
Necessity and conditional variation belong to the same analysis.

For Organon reconstruction, the first task is to preserve the
relation being asserted. A necessary possession is not automatically
a continuously occurring event; a possible additional faculty is
not an arbitrary addition; and absence can have different grounds.
The text provides concrete cases for each distinction.

The triad may suggest a useful organization of life, mind, and
feeling, but this unit does not derive a universal minimum ontology
of consciousness. Its precise claim is the necessary co-possession
of these faculties within the system being examined.
