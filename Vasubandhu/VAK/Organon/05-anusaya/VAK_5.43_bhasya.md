# VAK 5.43 Bhāṣya — The Five Lower Fetters and the Named Three

## Kārikā Anchor

> pañcadhāvarabhāgīyaṃ dvābhyāṃ kāmānatikramaḥ |
> tribhis tu punarāvṛttir mukha-mūla-grahāt trayam || 5.43 ||

> The lower fetters are fivefold. Two prevent passage beyond
> the desire domain; three bring return. The three are named
> because naming them takes the entrances and roots.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

Elsewhere the Blessed One taught five lower fetters:
personality view, grasping at discipline and observance,
doubt, desire for sense objects, and hostility. Why are
these called “lower”? They serve the lower part, the desire
domain, and conform to it. Through desire and hostility
one does not pass beyond that domain. Through the other
three one returns even after passing beyond, in the manner
of a gatekeeper and attendants.

Others explain “lower” along two axes: the three prevent
passing beyond the lower condition of a being, ordinary
person status; the two prevent passing beyond the lower
domain, the desire domain. On this account too they are
called lower fetters.

If a stream entrant's complete abandonment of the three
fetters abandons six afflictions, why are only personality
view, grasping at discipline and observance, and doubt
named, with three further views omitted? All six might
have been named. The answer is: the three are named because
the entrances and roots are taken. The afflictions are said
to occur in onefold, twofold, and fourfold kinds; these
three take the entrances to those kinds. Moreover, extreme
view is set in motion by personality view, grasping at views
by grasping at discipline and observance, and false view by
doubt. Thus the roots are taken.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The first account distinguishes **retention** from **return**.
Desire for sense objects and hostility hold one within the
desire domain; the other three can bring one back after
passage beyond it. The gatekeeper comparison belongs to
that account. The `apare` account instead distinguishes a
lower condition of being (`pṛthagjanatva`) from a lower
domain (`kāmadhātu`). Both explanations remain visible;
the passage does not resolve one into the other.

The later question concerns scriptural selection, not an
assertion that only three afflictions are abandoned at
stream entry. `Mukha` gives a representative entrance into
the several kinds of affliction, though their compressed
onefold/twofold/fourfold enumeration needs review.
`Mūla` is then specified through three explicit relations:

| Named root | View set in motion |
|---|---|
| Personality view | Extreme view |
| Grasping at discipline and observance | Grasping at views |
| Doubt | False view |

The source says the omitted views are `pravartita` by the
named factors. This explains why naming the roots can
account for the wider six, while leaving the exact force
of the causal verb and the abandonment relation for later
review.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Avarabhāga` is first identified
with the desire domain, then receives the `apare` reading
that also considers ordinary person status. `Mukha-mūla-
graha` explains the teacher's selection of names; it does
not say that these afflictions grasp entrances as objects.
“Set in motion” renders `pravartita` without replacing it
with a stronger causal theory. The malformed source reading
`vicikitsāpravatitā` is retained in the Sanskrit, while its
local sense informs the English.

**Organon reconstruction.** The passage suggests two
distinct ways an account can cover a field: listing every
member or naming representatives and their derivative
relations. For the Science of Citta, the latter requires a
traceable root-to-derived relation rather than a vague
claim of implicit inclusion. This is a project-level use
of the passage, separate from the source's own explanation
and from any implemented model.
