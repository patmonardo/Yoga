# VAK 5.23 Bhāṣya — Bondage to a Particular Object

## Kārikā Anchor

> rāgapratighamānaiḥ syād atītapratyupasthitaiḥ |
> yatrotpannā 'prahīṇās te tasmin vastuni saṃyutaḥ || 5.23 ||

> Through past or present attachment, aversion, and conceit,
> one is bound to the object with respect to which they arose,
> so long as they have not been abandoned.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

Whichever anuśaya of a person adheres to a given support connects
that person with that support through the anuśaya. What remains
to be stated is: with which object does a past affliction connect
one, and with which does a present affliction do so? The division
of the transmitted words that yields this question is editorial.

In summary, afflictions are of two kinds: particular-character
afflictions, here attachment, aversion, and conceit, and
general-character afflictions, view, doubt, and ignorance.
First, concerning the former: “Through past or presently arisen
attachment, aversion, and conceit, one is bound to the object
with respect to which those afflictions arose and remain
unabandoned.” Past and present instances of these three connect
one with the very object in respect of which they arose, if
they have not been abandoned. Because they are particular-character
afflictions, they do not necessarily arise for everyone with
respect to every object.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The transition links `anuśaya`, its `ālambana`, the person, and
the resulting bond. The rule then asks for a more exact account
across time and objects. The Bhāṣya first divides afflictions
by their scope. Attachment, aversion, and conceit are called
`svalakṣaṇakleśa`; view, doubt, and ignorance are grouped as
`sāmānyakleśa`. The latter's wider rule follows in 5.24.

For the first triad, the relation is indexed to an actual
arising toward a particular `vastu`. The relative-correlative
`yasmin ... tasmin` in the prose and `yatra ... tasmin` in
the verse keep the same object fixed. The required condition
is non-abandonment. Thus a past occurrence can still determine
bondage: no longer being presently arisen is not equivalent
to having been abandoned.

`Saṃprayuktaḥ` occurs in the transition, while the verse and
its prose use `saṃyutaḥ` and `saṃyuktaḥ` for connectedness
to the object. The passage should not erase that lexical
difference. The local argument establishes the person's
object-indexed bond through an affliction, without requiring
all these relation terms to be synonyms.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Pratyupasthita` in the verse is
explained by `pratyutpanna` in the prose, so “present” is
secure here. `Svalakṣaṇa` is rendered as particular-character
in this local classification; it concerns the non-universal
arising of these afflictions across persons and objects.
The damaged triad spellings and editorial division of the
temporal question remain visible rather than silently replaced.

**Organon reconstruction.** This gives the Science of Citta
a determinate relation: a particular person, affliction, and
object, an actual arising, and a continuing condition of
non-abandonment. It distinguishes the event's temporal status
from the status of the bond. This is a project-level formal
reading of the Bhāṣya, not an assertion that such a model
has already been implemented.
