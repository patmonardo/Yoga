# VAK 5.21 Bhāṣya — Rival Accounts of Indeterminate Roots

## Kārikā Anchor

> dvaidhordhvavṛtter nāto 'nyau catvāry eveti bāhyakāḥ |
> tṛṣṇādṛṅmānamohās te dhyāyitṛtvād avidyayā || 5.21 ||

> The other two are excluded because of divided and upward movement.
> Outside authorities instead say there are four: craving, view,
> conceit, and delusion, in their indeterminate forms, because of
> the three kinds of meditator under ignorance.

The verse continues 5.20's dispute about roots of the ethically
indeterminate. `Bāhyakāḥ` marks a rival position in this local debate.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

“The other two are not roots, because of divided and upward
movement.” Doubt, it is said, cannot be a root because its
movement is divided: it is unstable. Conceit, characterized
by elevation, cannot be a root because its movement is upward.
For roots [the text's qualifying expression is uncertain] are
observed in the world to be stable and to extend downward.

“The outside authorities say there are just four.” They hold
that four factors are roots of the indeterminate: “craving,
view, conceit, and delusion—those.” The pronoun indicates that
these are the indeterminate instances: indeterminate craving,
view, conceit, and ignorance. Why do they consider these roots
of the indeterminate? “Because of the meditators, through
ignorance.” There are three sorts of meditator, dominated
respectively by craving, view, and conceit; these arise under
the power of ignorance.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The opening defense of the Kāśmīra triad in 5.20 excludes two
candidates. `Vicikitsā` wavers between alternatives and is
therefore too unstable for roothood. `Māna` rises by its very
character; the root analogy instead requires steadiness and
downward extension. The Bhāṣya's analogy supports this local
classification, but the difficult `vividhasmṛtimūlāni` should
not be turned into a fully certain botanical doctrine.

The `bāhyakāḥ` supply another list. Their four are not simply
the Kāśmīra three plus one: `dṛṣṭi` and `māna` enter, while
`mati/prajñā` drops out. Their stated evidence is a threefold
classification of meditators by what predominates in them;
ignorance is the common condition under whose power they arise.
The text reports this argument without establishing a more
specific identity for the outside authorities or resolving
the rivalry in this unit.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Kila` marks the reported explanation.
`Dvaidha-vṛtti` describes doubt's divided movement; `ūrdhva-vṛtti`
describes conceit's upward movement. The prose interprets `te`
as limiting all four rival candidates to their indeterminate
instances. `Tṛṣṇā-dṛṣṭi-māna-uttara-dhyāyinaḥ` is read as three
meditator types distinguished by their predominant factor;
`avidyāvaśāt` gives their shared dependence on ignorance.

**Organon reconstruction.** This dispute tests what it means
for a factor to be a root. One account uses the formal behavior
of the factor; the rival invokes recurring configurations of
meditative experience. For the Science of Citta, those are
distinct kinds of evidence. This comparison is a project-level
reconstruction, separate from either school's stated doctrine.
