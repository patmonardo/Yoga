# VAK 5.07 Bhāṣya — The Five Views Defined

## Kārikā Anchor

> ātmātmīyadruvocchedanāstihīnāgradṛṣṭayaḥ |
> ahetvamārge taddṛṣṭir etās tāḥ pañca dṛṣṭayaḥ || 5.07 ||

> The views of self or mine, of permanence or annihilation, of
> nonexistence, of the inferior as supreme, and of a non-cause or
> non-path as cause or path: these are the five views.

The Bhāṣya moves from the names given in 5.03 to the determinate
character of each view.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

The five views have been specified by name, but not by their own
character. What is that character? The verse gives views concerning
self and what belongs to self; permanence and annihilation; the
assertion “it does not exist”; what is inferior being taken as
supreme; and a non-cause or non-path being taken as cause or path.

The view of a self or of what belongs to a self is satkāya-view.
`Sat` is explained by “sinks,” and `kāya` by collection or aggregate.
Thus `satkāya` denotes the five aggregates of appropriation. This
expression is meant to dispel notions of permanence and of a
compact whole, which precede grasping those aggregates as self.
Any view taking contaminated aggregates as its cognitive support
could broadly be called a view concerning satkāya, but the name
`satkāyadṛṣṭi` is restricted here to the view of self or mine. The
restriction shows that its object is neither an actual self nor
something belonging to one. The cited saying states that whoever
regards something as self regards these five aggregates of
appropriation.

The view that this very thing conceived as self is permanent or
annihilated is extreme-grasping view, because it takes the extremes
of eternalism and annihilationism.

When a truth such as suffering obtains, the view “it does not
exist” is false view. All inverted views could broadly bear that
name, but this one receives it especially because of its
predominance, like an especially foul wound. It denies what is
there; the other views superimpose what is not there.

The view that what is inferior is supreme is adherence to views.
What is inferior? Everything with outflows, because noble ones
abandon it. Grasping it as highest is this adherence. The name
compresses `dṛṣṭyādi-parāmarśa`, with “and so forth” omitted.

Taking what is not a cause as a cause, or what is not a path as a
path, is adherence to discipline and observance. The examples are
the views that Maheśvara, Prajāpati, or another is the cause of
worlds; that entering fire or water causes heavenly birth; or that
mere discipline and observances, or the knowledges of Sāṃkhya and
Yoga, constitute a path to liberation. Here too an “and so forth”
is said to be omitted. These are the five views.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The five forms are not interchangeable: satkāya-view appropriates
the aggregates as self or mine; extreme-grasping view fixes that
supposed self as permanent or annihilated; false view denies a truth
that obtains; adherence to views elevates what is `sāsrava` as
supreme; and adherence to discipline and observance attributes
causal or liberating efficacy where the source denies it.

The commentary's `apavādikā`/`samāropikāḥ` distinction is exact:
false view cancels a determination that obtains, while the other
views impose one that does not. This is a distinction within the
source's account of error, not a license to identify every act of
negation with false view. Likewise, `sāsrava` in the fourth
definition is a technical outflow-status, not a generic insult to
all finite existence.

The examples concerning divine cause and non-Buddhist practices
are Vasubandhu's polemical examples within this doctrinal account.
They should not be treated as an unargued verdict on our
transcendental use of Ātman, Sāṃkhya, or Yoga. The local target of
`satkāyadṛṣṭi` is specifically grasping the five appropriated
aggregates as a self or its possessions.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Satkāya` remains visible because its
Bhāṣya etymology and its reference to the five aggregates matter.
`Ātman` is not rendered as a generic psychological “ego.”
`Sāsrava` is glossed as “with outflows,” while the Sanskrit remains
the controlled term. `Apavāda` and `samāropa` name distinct
operations of denial and imposition in this passage.

**Organon reconstruction.** These five views expose different
failures of determination: mislocated self-relation, fixation at
opposed extremes, false cancellation, false elevation, and false
mediation between means and end. This is our logical use of the
Kośa's classification. It does not settle Māṇḍūkya 7's positive
Self-determination by substituting the aggregate-self targeted
here for that distinct question.
