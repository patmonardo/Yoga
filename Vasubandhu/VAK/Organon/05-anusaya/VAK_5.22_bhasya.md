# VAK 5.22 Bhāṣya — Four Ways to Answer a Question

## Kārikā Anchor

> ekāṃśato vyākaraṇaṃ vibhajya paripṛcchya ca |
> sthāpyaṃ ca maraṇotpatti-viśiṣṭātmānyatādivat || 5.22 ||

> A question calls for a categorical answer, an answer after
> division, an answer after asking in return, or setting aside;
> compare death, rebirth, relative superiority, and whether
> a being is other than the aggregates.

## Continuous Conventional Translation

The sūtra speaks of fourteen undetermined matters. Are they
called `avyākṛta` because they have the ethically indeterminate
status just discussed? No. Here an “undetermined” question is
one to be set aside. Questions have four forms: to be answered
categorically, after dividing the cases, after questioning in
return, or to be set aside. The verse illustrates these by
death, rebirth, comparative superiority, and self's otherness.

“Will all beings die?” receives the categorical answer “They
will die.” “Will all be born again?” requires division: those
with defilement will, those without it will not. “Is a human
superior or inferior?” requires asking “Compared with whom?”
Relative to gods, inferior; relative to bad destinies, superior.
“Is a being other than the aggregates, or not?” is set aside:
there is no being as an independently existing substance, just
as one cannot ask whether a barren woman's son is dark or fair.

An objection asks how calling a question undetermined can itself
be an answer. The reply says that this designation is precisely
the response. Another speaker disputes the examples: “Not all
will be reborn” could be answered categorically; division becomes
necessary if the question concerns those who die. Humans are
both inferior and superior relative to different comparators,
as cognition can be both effect and cause. But an unqualified
question still requires division if an unqualified answer cannot
be given. The question about a being apart from the aggregates
remains undetermined. Merely declining to answer, this speaker
adds, cannot itself count as an answer. The rejoinder asks why
identifying a question as one to be set aside would fail to
answer it.

The Ābhidharmikas then offer a more developed account. Categorical
answers concern such determinations as the Buddha's awakening,
the well-taught Dharma, the well-practicing community, and the
impermanence of form through consciousness; these bear directly
on the intended matter. An answer by division progressively
specifies a request to “speak of dharmas”: past, future, or
present; then form through consciousness; then wholesome,
unwholesome, or indeterminate form; then kinds of abstention;
then abstention arising from non-greed, non-hatred, or
non-delusion; and finally manifest and unmanifest forms of
non-greed-born abstention.

With a tricky questioner, one may instead keep asking which
dharmas are intended until the questioner falls silent or
supplies the needed specification. The text asks whether a
request such as “Tell me the road” already counts as a question,
and answers that replying through counter-question can therefore
count as a form of answer. Questions such as whether the world
is finite or infinite are set aside.

The venerable Mahāsāṅghikas are then said to recite a sūtra on
the four modes. “Are all conditioned things impermanent?” is
categorical. The result of intentional action calls for division.
A question about whether self and perceptions are the same or
different calls first for asking what the questioner takes the
self to be; the witness's quoted reply is damaged. Questions
about an eternal or non-eternal, finite or infinite world, the
Tathāgata after death, and whether life is other than the body
are set aside.

## Argument and Doctrinal Determination

The opening distinction is lexical and logical. `Avyākṛta`
in 5.19–5.21 classifies the ethical status of dharmas. Here it
classifies a **question** whose proposed answer is set aside.
Identity of term does not establish identity of function.

The four modes depend on the form of the question. A categorical
answer needs a determinate subject and predicate; division is
needed when a question suppresses relevant cases; a counter-question
elicits the missing comparison or intended subject. The initial
set-aside example rejects an independently existing `sattva-dravya`
as the subject of the proposed alternatives. This is the text's
reason for suspending that question; it does not erase every
ordinary or practical use of “being” or “self.”

The second speaker shows that examples can shift category when
their wording and intended scope change. The Ābhidharmika
expansion makes division a disciplined sequence of increasingly
specific determinations, reaching `vijñapti` and `avijñapti`.
The Mahāsāṅghika citation preserves another set of examples;
its damaged line about self should not carry more doctrinal
weight than the witness can support.

## Translation and Organon Study

**Translation decisions.** `Sthāpanīya` means that the question
is to be set aside as posed. `Vyākaraṇa` is an answer or
determination, whereas `avyākṛta` in the opening question also
recalls the different ethical sense. `Āpekṣika` makes superiority
relative to a comparison class. The four examples in the verse
are unpacked by the prose rather than read as four unrelated
topics. The corrupt forms in the running IAST source have been
kept visible in the Sanskrit and translated only where their
local sense is recoverable.

**Organon reconstruction.** This is a useful discipline for
Theoretical Reason: determine what sort of question has been
asked before producing a determination. The four answer modes
can be treated as operations on a question's subject, relevant
cases, comparison relation, and presuppositions. That is an
Organon reading of the Bhāṣya, not a source claim about a
completed formal system.
