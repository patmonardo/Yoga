# VAK_7.56 — Other-Mind Knowledge, Provenance, and Chapter Closure

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

```sanskrit
चेतोज्ञानं तु तत्त्रेधा तर्कविद्याकृतं च यत् ।
जानते नारका आदौ नृणां नोत्पत्तिलभिकम् ॥ ७.५६ ॥
```

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

```text
cetojñānaṃ tu tattredhā tarkavidyākṛtaṃ ca yat |
jānate nārakā ādau nṛṇāṃ notpattilabhikam || 7.56 ||
```

The Sanskrit follows the local kārikā witness, including
`utpattilabhikam`. The running source prints
`utpattilābhikam`, while the prose repeatedly uses
birth-acquisition expressions with `upapatti`. These
forms are distinguished rather than silently harmonized.

## 3. Padaccheda and Lexical Analysis

```text

jānate | nārakāḥ | ādau | nṛṇām | na | utpatti-labhikam
```

| Form | Morphology | Contextual sense |
|---|---|---|
| `cetojñānam` | nominative singular neuter compound | knowledge of mind, specified as another's mind |
| `tu` | contrastive particle | however |
| `tat` | nominative singular neuter pronoun | that [birth-acquired knowledge] |
| `tredhā` | adverb | threefold, here ethically |
| `tarkavidyākṛtam` | nominative singular neuter compound adjective | produced through inference or a vidyā technique |
| `ca` | additive conjunction | also |
| `yat` | nominative singular neuter relative pronoun | that which [is so produced] |
| `jānate` | third-person plural present middle of √jñā | they know |
| `nārakāḥ` | nominative plural masculine | hell-beings |
| `ādau` | locative singular of `ādi` | at the beginning |
| `nṛṇām` | genitive plural of `nṛ` | of humans; for humans |
| `utpattilabhikam` | nominative singular neuter adjective in the local reading | acquired through birth |

The printed `tattredhā` resolves as `tat + tredhā`,
not `tatra + tredhā`. The Bhāṣya expressly carries
forward `upapattyāptam`, “birth-acquired,” from VAK 7.55.
`Na + utpattilabhikam` yields `notpattilabhikam`.

## 4. Grammar

**The inherited qualification.** `Tat` resumes birth-
acquired other-mind knowledge. `Tredhā` gives its threefold
ethical status, expanded as wholesome, unwholesome,
and indeterminate. It does not by itself enumerate
three methods of knowing another mind.

**Two additional routes.** `Tarkavidyākṛtaṃ ca yat`
extends the same predicate to knowledge produced through
inference and through a vidyā. The prose separates
the inferential knowing of interpreters of signs from
vidyā-produced knowing. These are two routes, not a
mandatory joint procedure of reasoning plus technique.

**The knowing of hell-beings.** `Jānate nārakā ādau`
is a finite clause. The prose supplies two instrumental
means: birth-acquired other-mind knowledge and former-
abode recollection. Limiting the clause to other-mind
knowledge alone would omit half of its stated reference.

**The human restriction.** `Nṛṇām` identifies the bearer
class for whom birth-acquisition is denied. The prose
expands the subject to the previously described capacities
beginning with ṛddhi. It does not deny that humans can
acquire such capacities through cultivation or that
particular recollection can have another provenance.

**Close construe.** That birth-acquired other-mind knowledge
is threefold, as is what is produced through inference
or vidyā. Hell-beings know initially through the two
birth-acquired capacities. For humans, the described
capacities are not acquired through birth.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> That knowledge of another mind is threefold, as is
> what is produced through inference or vidyā. Hell-beings
> know at the beginning. For humans, it is not acquired
> through birth.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> Birth-acquired knowledge of another mind may be wholesome,
> unwholesome, or indeterminate. The inferential knowing
> of interpreters of signs and knowing produced through
> a vidyā technique have the same three ethical possibilities.
> Cultivation-result other-mind knowledge, by contrast,
> is wholesome only.
>
> Hell-beings initially know through birth-acquired other-
> mind knowledge and former-abode recollection, until
> painful feeling overwhelms them. Those in other destinies
> are not subject to that initial-only restriction in
> the stated account.
>
> Humans do not acquire the previously described capacities
> beginning with ṛddhi merely through birth. Their apparently
> natural recollection of former lives is explained instead
> as produced by a special karma. Former-abode recollection
> is therefore classified as cultivation-result, birth-
> acquired, or karma-produced.

Source: Pradhan 430|11–431|03, local Bhāṣya checked
against the running source. The prose's `nityaṃ jānate`
is treated with the immediate human qualification in
view, not as universal possession by every being in
every other destiny.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> Similar cognitive content does not settle how knowing
> was acquired or how it is ethically classified. A
> capacity's continued exercise also depends on conditions:
> the text distinguishes its initial availability from
> its interruption by overwhelming pain. An apparent
> exception to a birth-acquisition rule requires examination
> of its causal provenance.

The chapter closes by distinguishing the classifications
of knowing, not by making every occurrence of apparent
mental access into cultivated higher knowledge.

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**`Cetojñāna / paracittajñāna`.** Knowledge of mind,
expanded as knowledge of another mind. The broad object
description does not establish that all its listed forms
have identical directness, range, or reliability.

**`Tārkika`.** Inferential or reasoning-based, specified
here through the `naimittikas`. It should not be expanded
into an entire formal logic curriculum or reduced to
mere arbitrary guessing.

**`Naimittika`.** Interpreter of signs or indications.
This identifies the bearer of the inferential form in
the prose. The passage does not give a full theory of
which signs are used or how each inference is validated.

**`Vidyākṛta`.** Produced through a vidyā, here a special
technique or learned art. The term is not automatically
the liberative vidyā designation examined in 7.45–7.46.

**`Bhāvanāphala`.** Result of cultivation. In this sentence
the subject is other-mind knowledge, which is wholesome
only. VAK 7.53's indeterminate cultivation-result was
a construction-mind. Their different subjects prevent
contradiction between the two classifications.

**`Duḥkhavedanābhyāhata`.** Struck or overwhelmed by
painful feeling. This bounds the initial exercise of
the two capacities in hell-beings. The text does not
say that their acquisition was therefore unreal or
that their provenance changes when pain intervenes.

**`Prakṛtijātismara`.** One who naturally recollects
former births. This is the apparent human counterexample
raised by the prose, not a new sixth source of ṛddhi.

**`Karmaviśeṣaja`.** Produced by a special karma. This
is the reply's causal classification of that human
recollection. It does not identify the specific past
action or give a method of verifying such recollection.

## 8. Logical Determination

**Other-mind knowledge by provenance.**

| Provenance or route | Ethical classification stated here |
|---|---|
| Birth-acquired | Wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate |
| Inferential, associated with sign interpreters | Wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate |
| Vidyā-produced | Wholesome, unwholesome, or indeterminate |
| Cultivation-result | Wholesome only |

The threefold predicate is ethical. It is not the
number of rows in this table, and it is not the threefold
provenance of former-abode recollection given at the end.

**Conditions of exercise.** The prose pairs birth-acquired
other-mind knowledge with former-abode recollection.
Hell-beings exercise them initially until overwhelmed
by painful feeling. The following statement about other
destinies removes that specific initial-only restriction,
but the human exclusion immediately narrows any general
reading of birth-acquired availability.

The text does not supply a complete account of later
recovery, permanent loss, or the exact cognitive sequence
under pain. “Conditionally disabled” can be a project
analogy; it must not become an unreported source doctrine
of a latent mechanism that is guaranteed to reactivate.

**The human counterexample.**

```text
Claim:
    The described capacities are not human birth-acquisitions.

Question:
    What about humans who naturally recall former lives?

Reply:
    Their recollection is produced by a special karma.
```

The distinction is between causal categories in this
account. It does not deny karmic conditions of human
birth itself. Nor does apparently spontaneous occurrence
uniquely identify the technical category “birth-acquired.”

**The final threefold inventory.**

| Former-abode recollection | Causal designation |
|---|---|
| Cultivated | Result of cultivation |
| Birth-acquired | Acquired through mode of existence |
| Karma-produced | Produced by karma, including the special human case |

This final list classifies recollection by provenance,
not by wholesome, unwholesome, and indeterminate status.
The two different meanings of “threefold” must remain
explicit in the chapter's closing study.

## 9. Interpretive Note

**Corrections to the research analysis.** The research
padaccheda supplies `tatra` where the verse has `tat`
before `tredhā`. The prose controls the antecedent as
birth-acquired knowledge. Likewise, “three modes of
knowing mind” is too loose if it obscures that the
verse's threefold predicate initially means ethical
classification rather than acquisition routes.

**Witness cautions.** The local kārikā has
`utpattilabhikam`; the running verse has
`utpattilābhikam`, while the prose uses forms of
`upapattipratilambhika`. The intended birth-acquisition
category is explicit despite this variation. The running
prose also contains `ṛddhacyādikaṃ`; the reference to
the capacities beginning with ṛddhi remains clear.
The study preserves the local verse reading and does
not silently repair the underlying files.

**Organon reconstruction — Yoga Vidyā and Yoga Technē.**
Yoga Vidyā articulates the relation between cognitive
content, provenance, and warranted classification.
Yoga Technē, its kriyā–karma companion, concerns acquisition
and the conditions under which knowing can be exercised.
Their relation remains necessary even when two occurrences
appear to concern the same object.

For Organon, successful apparent access to another state
is not enough to identify the operation that produced
it. Sign-based inference, a cultivated capacity, and
a birth-acquired capacity receive distinct determinations
here. Conditions of interruption add a further question
that provenance alone does not answer.

These source distinctions can inform a project model
of capabilities, but they do not establish that every
route has identical outputs or translate directly into
software mechanisms. The chapter's positive lesson for
our reconstruction is to keep object, acquisition,
ethical class, bearer, and conditions of exercise related
without collapsing them.

**Verified chapter endpoint.** The running source closes
at Pradhan 431|04 with:

> abhidharmakośabhāṣye jñānanirdeśo nāma saptamaṃ kośasthānam

This identifies Jñānanirdeśa as the seventh Kośa section.
The source then marks the eighth section. VAK 7.56 is
therefore the final numbered kārikā of Chapter 7, not
a transition verse to be counted within Chapter 8.

**Review status.** Provisional, Bhāṣya-controlled study.
The ethical table, inherited birth-acquisition scope,
paired capacities of hell-beings, pain condition, human
restriction, karmic counterexample, final recollection
inventory, and chapter colophon have been checked against
the local extract and running source. Completion of the
study sequence does not remove its provisional review status.
