# VAK_7.43 — Higher Knowledges: Constitution, Ground, and Acquisition

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

```sanskrit
चतस्रः संवृतिज्ञानं चेतसि ज्ञानपञ्चकम् ।
क्षयाभिज्ञा बलं यद्वत् पञ्च ध्यानचतुष्टये ॥ ७.४३ ॥
```

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

```text
catasraḥ saṃvṛtijñānaṃ cetasi jñānapañcakam |
kṣayābhijñā balaṃ yadvat pañca dhyānacatuṣṭaye || 7.43 ||
```

The verse follows the local kārikā witness. The Devanāgarī
renders that reading. The prose contains damaged expressions;
these are identified below without changing the textual witness.

## 3. Padaccheda and Lexical Analysis

```text
catasraḥ | saṃvṛti-jñānam | cetasi | jñāna-pañcakam |
kṣaya-abhijñā | balam | yadvat | pañca | dhyāna-catuṣṭaye
```

| Form | Morphology | Contextual sense |
|---|---|---|
| `catasraḥ` | nominative plural feminine numeral | four [higher knowledges] |
| `saṃvṛtijñānam` | nominative singular neuter compound | conventional knowledge |
| `cetasi` | locative singular neuter of `cetas` | concerning mind [another's mental course] |
| `jñānapañcakam` | nominative singular neuter collective compound | a group of five knowledge-categories |
| `kṣayābhijñā` | nominative singular feminine compound | higher knowledge concerning exhaustion |
| `balam` | nominative singular neuter | the [corresponding Buddha-]power |
| `yadvat` | indeclinable comparative expression | just as; in the same manner as |
| `pañca` | nominative plural numeral here | five [higher knowledges] |
| `dhyānacatuṣṭaye` | locative singular neuter collective compound | in the group of four dhyānas |

`Kṣaya + abhijñā` yields `kṣayābhijñā`. The verse's
`saṃvṛtijñānaṃ` and `jñānapañcakam` name classificatory
constitutions; their singular form does not reduce the
plural higher knowledges to one occurrence of cognition.

## 4. Grammar

**Four, not an ordinal sequence.** `Catasraḥ` supplies a
feminine subject with `abhijñāḥ` understood. The Bhāṣya
specifies the set by exclusion: leave aside other-mind
knowledge and influx-exhaustion knowledge. Thus the four
are numbers 1, 2, 4, and 5 of VAK 7.42.

**The mind locative.** `Cetasi` means “in the case of
mind,” with other-mind higher knowledge supplied from
context. `Jñānapañcakam` is its nominal predicate. The
prose enumerates five established knowledge-categories;
it does not enumerate five psychological features of a mind.

**The comparison.** `Kṣayābhijñā` is the subject;
`balaṃ yadvat` elliptically refers back to the account of
the influx-exhaustion power. The prose supplies “it is
to be understood as that power was explained,” followed
by the six-or-ten classification and the supporting-level
reference. This is a controlled cross-reference.

**The second numerical set.** `Pañca` supplies `abhijñāḥ`
again. The prose's `śeṣās tu`, “the remaining ones,
however,” contrasts these five with exhaustion-knowledge.
`Dhyānacatuṣṭaye` locates them in the four dhyānas.
The four conventional knowledges and the five dhyāna-
supported knowledges are different sets.

**Close construe.** Four [higher knowledges] are conventional
knowledge; concerning [another's] mind, [there is] a group
of five knowledges. Exhaustion-higher-knowledge [is understood]
just as the [corresponding] power. Five [are] in the four dhyānas.

## 5. Translation

### Literal Translation

> Four are conventional knowledge; concerning mind,
> there is a group of five knowledges. The higher knowledge
> of exhaustion is as the corresponding power. Five are
> in the group of four dhyānas.

### Bhāṣya-informed study translation

> Extraordinary efficacy, divine hearing, former-abode
> recollection, and death-and-rebirth knowledge are
> conventional knowledge. Other-mind higher knowledge
> comprises five categories: dharma, anvaya, path,
> conventional, and other-mind knowledge.
>
> Influx-exhaustion higher knowledge is understood as
> the corresponding Buddha-power was explained: six or
> ten knowledge-categories, with its supporting levels
> likewise understood from that account. The other five
> higher knowledges have the four dhyānas as their grounds.

Source: the local Bhāṣya witness, Pradhan 421|13–422|14.
The cross-reference is controlled by the studies of
[VAK 7.29](./VAK_7.29.md) and [VAK 7.30](./VAK_7.30.md).
The following prose explains acquisition and the exclusion
of formless grounds; it is developed below rather than
silently inserted into the verse translation.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> A higher knowledge is determined through its cognitive
> constitution, its supporting level, and the procedure
> through which it is acquired. These determinations
> belong together without becoming interchangeable.
> What initially requires a bodily sign or a sequential
> reconstruction can, once accomplished, operate without
> repeating that same procedure.

The Bhāṣya illustrates this with other-mind knowledge
and former-abode recollection. Its distinction concerns
the acquisition and exercise of the described capacities;
it does not remove their supporting-level restrictions.

## 7. Technical Vocabulary

**`Saṃvṛtijñāna`.** Conventional knowledge, one of the ten
knowledge-categories. The extraordinary reach of a cognition
does not by itself place it outside this category. Nor
is “has a conditioned object” sufficient to explain this
classification: path-knowledge also has a conditioned object.

**`Jñānapañcaka`.** Five knowledge-categories: dharma,
anvaya, path, conventional, and other-mind knowledge.
This is a classification across the forms of the capacity,
not a claim that every occurrence contains five separate
simultaneous cognitive acts.

**`Kṣayābhijñā`.** Influx-exhaustion higher knowledge.
The six-or-ten alternative inherits the earlier semantic
distinction: knowledge taking cessation as object comprises
six categories; knowledge in the continuum of one whose
influxes are exhausted comprises all ten. The six are
dharma, anvaya, cessation, exhaustion, non-arising, and
conventional knowledge. Merely saying “depending on the
person” loses the distinction controlling the count.

**`Rūpatīrtha`.** A form-based route or point of access.
This provisional rendering is controlled by the following
account of taking up the sign of one's own body–mind
relation and applying it to another person.

**`Nimitta`.** A sign taken up in acquisition. In the
other-mind case the sign connects bodily and mental
conditions. Once the capacity is established, the prose
says that knowing occurs without dependence on form.

**`Abhiniṣpannā`.** Accomplished or established, qualifying
the higher knowledge. It marks the achieved condition
contrasted with the process of producing that capacity.

**`Vilaṅghya`.** Having skipped over. In recollection,
the accomplished capacity can bypass intervening stages;
the initial backward sequence need not be replayed in full.

**`Parasaṃtatyadhiṣṭhāna`.** Dependence upon another
continuum. The prose specifies this acquisition basis
for one reborn here after a formless existence, contrasting
it with the usual basis in one's own continuum.

## 8. Logical Determination

| Higher knowledge | Knowledge constitution | Supporting grounds here |
|---|---|---|
| Extraordinary efficacy | Conventional | Four dhyānas |
| Divine hearing | Conventional | Four dhyānas |
| Another mind | Dharma, anvaya, path, conventional, other-mind | Four dhyānas |
| Former-abode recollection | Conventional | Four dhyānas |
| Death and rebirth | Conventional | Four dhyānas |
| Influx-exhaustion | Six or ten, as previously distinguished | As in the corresponding power |

The last supporting-ground entry refers to the all-level
account of VAK 7.30. Its aggregate range must not become
an assertion that each individual knowledge-category is
available at every level.

**Why the first five are absent from formless grounds.**
The prose first excludes three because they take form
as object: extraordinary efficacy, divine hearing, and
death-and-rebirth knowledge. Here `rūpa` covers material
objects, including sound; it does not mean visible colour alone.

Other-mind knowledge takes mind as object, but is produced
through a form-based route. The practitioner takes up the
relation between his own bodily and mental conditions,
then attends to others through that relation. Once the
higher knowledge is established, its knowing is independent
of form in the stated sense. Acquisition through a sign
does not make every mature occurrence an inference from
that sign, and independence from that sign does not
establish a new formless supporting ground.

**Acquiring recollection.** The prose describes attention
backward from the immediately ceased mental cognition
through preceding states toward the linking mind and
intermediate existence. It also cites such objects as
place and lineage. The local wording around the transition
to intermediate existence and death is damaged; the exact
micro-sequence remains a textual-review question.

After accomplishment, recollection may skip intervening
stages. The prose nevertheless restricts recollection
to what has previously been experienced. Its answer about
the Pure Abodes invokes experience through hearing; it
does not require a former personal residence there.
For one arriving from a formless existence, acquisition
uses another continuum; other cases use one's own.

The closing statement names attention to lightness,
sound, and light as preparations for extraordinary efficacy
and the related powers. These are specific preparations,
not a complete universal procedure for all six capacities.

```text
Knowledge constitution ≠ supporting ground ≠ acquisition procedure

Other-mind knowledge:
    acquisition through body–mind signs
    accomplished knowing without dependence on form

Former-abode recollection:
    acquisition through backward succession
    accomplished recollection can skip intermediate stages

Both:
    altered exercise does not abolish the stated supporting grounds
```

## 9. Interpretive Note

**Corrections to the research kārikā.** The research file
calls the conventional group “the first four,” thereby
including other-mind knowledge and omitting death-and-
rebirth knowledge. The Bhāṣya's explicit exclusion corrects
this. Its fivefold list also replaces the research file's
five psychological descriptions with five named knowledge-
categories. The original research witness remains intact.

**Textual cautions.** The prose prints `caturthadhyānabhūmikāḥ`,
which on its surface suggests the fourth dhyāna. The verse's
unambiguous `dhyānacatuṣṭaye` controls the four-dhyāna
reading here; the prose form remains a witness problem.
Other irregularities include `jñānavalam`, `abhhijñā`,
`jñātvyā`, and `udgṛhlāti`. The expressions around
`anupūrvāvasthāntaramaraṇābhiniṣpatteḥ` and the subsequent
intermediate-existence sentence warrant further collation.
The broad backward-recollection argument is clearer than
these damaged details. No silent restoration is presented
as the transmitted Sanskrit.

**Organon reconstruction — Yoga Vidyā and Yoga Technē.**
Yoga Vidyā articulates the knowledge-field and its cognitive
constitution. Yoga Technē, its kriyā–karma companion,
articulates the cultivation through which that knowing
becomes an available capacity. This verse gives our project
a precise relation between the two: the conditions of
acquisition help determine what the achieved capacity is,
yet its exercise need not repeat every acquisition step.

The analogy to learned competence is useful at this
structural level. It does not establish a modern mechanism
for the powers described by the source. “Internalization
of mediation” is an Organon interpretation of the contrast;
the Bhāṣya explicitly states sign-independent knowing
and recollection that skips stages, without supplying
that philosophical terminology.

**Review status.** Provisional, Bhāṣya-controlled study.
The four-member set, five named categories, exhaustion-
power cross-reference, four dhyānas, and distinction
between acquisition and accomplishment have been checked
against the local witnesses. Damaged prose remains marked
for textual review.
