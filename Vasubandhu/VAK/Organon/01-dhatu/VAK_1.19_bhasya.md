# VAK_1.19 Bhāṣya — Paired Faculties and the Unity of a Domain

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> jātigocaravijñānasāmānyād ekadhātutā /
>
> dvitve 'pi cakṣurādīnāṃ śobhārthaṃ tu dvayodbhavaḥ // 1.19 //

> Because of commonality of kind, object-range, and cognition, the eye and
> the other faculties have one-domain status even though they are paired.
> Their arising as a pair, however, is for beauty.

The Bhāṣya tests the eighteenfold count against the bodily pairing of eye,
ear, and nose. It distinguishes the grounds of classificatory unity from
its stated explanation of paired bodily arrangement.

## 2. Continuous Sanskrit

> śrotraghrāṇayoś ca / na bhavitavyam / yasmāt /

> tatra jātisāmānyam ubhayoś cakṣuḥsvabhāvatvāt / gocarasāmānyam
> ubhayo rūpaviṣayatvāt / vijñānasāmānyam ubhayor
> ekacakṣurvijñānāśrayatvāt / tasmād eka eva cakṣurdhātuḥ / evaṃ
> śrotraghrāṇayor api yojyam /
>
> śobhārthaṃ tu dvayodbhavaḥ / ekadhātutve 'pi tu cakṣurādīnāṃ dvayoḥ
> saṃbhava āśrayasya śobhārtham / anyathā hy
> ekacakṣuḥśrotrādhiṣṭhānaikanāsikābilasaṃbhavāt mahad vairūpyaṃ syād iti /

The natural unit runs from the twenty-one-domain objection at printed
location 012.17 through the explanation of paired sites at 012.25–013.01.
The following statement that the aggregates, āyatanas, and dhātus have been
explained opens the inquiry into their meanings in 1.20.

Word division and punctuation are editorial. The running source's `tra`,
`tasmad`, `cakṣuradīnaṃ`/`cakṣurādīnaṃ`, `anya thā`, and `vairupyaṃ`
are normalized to `tatra`, `tasmād`, `cakṣurādīnāṃ`, `anyathā`, and
`vairūpyaṃ`. Quotation marks standing for avagrahas are regularized.
In the closing compound, `nāsikāvila` is read provisionally as
`nāsikābila`, nostril-opening. The research report retains `vila` but
translates it as an opening. That lexical repair remains marked for review.
The source's `ubhayo rūpaviṣayatvāt` is retained as sandhi of `ubhayoḥ`
before `rūpa`.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

> But should there not be twenty-one dhātus, because the eye is twofold,
> and likewise the ear and nose? There should not. For, because of commonality
> of kind, object-range, and cognition, the eye and the others have one-domain
> status even though they are paired.
>
> Here there is commonality of kind because both have the nature of eye;
> commonality of object-range because both have visible form as their object;
> and commonality of cognition because both are supports of one eye-cognition.
> Therefore there is just one eye-domain. The same is to be applied to the
> ear and nose.
>
> Their arising as a pair, however, is for beauty. Even though the eye and
> the others have one-domain status, their occurrence as two is for the
> beauty of the bodily support. Otherwise, through the occurrence of only
> one eye-site, one ear-site, and one nostril-opening, there would be great
> disfigurement.

## 4. Movement of the Commentary

The opening objection would add one domain for the second member of each
of three pairs: eighteen plus three gives twenty-one. The reply rejects
bodily number as a sufficient ground for increasing the count.

```text
paired eyes, ears, and nasal faculties
    → proposed increase from eighteen to twenty-one
three commonalities
    → one dhātu for each pair
continued bodily duality
    → explained through the beauty of the bodily support
```

The objection and reply are distinguishable voices within the exposition.
No historical opponent or school is explicitly named. The account of beauty
is the commentary's stated explanation; it follows the determination of
one-domain status rather than supplying its ground.

## 5. Three Grounds of One-Domain Status

The ablative `jātigocaravijñānasāmānyāt` gives the reason for
`ekadhātutā`, the status of being one dhātu. The locative `dvitve` with
`api` concedes the paired condition. Unity of domain and bodily duality
are therefore asserted together.

The prose divides the compound into three explanations, each referring to
both eyes through the dual `ubhayoḥ`:

| Commonality | Bhāṣya's reason | Classificatory significance |
|---|---|---|
| kind (`jāti`) | both have eye-nature | bodily distinction does not introduce another faculty-kind |
| object-range (`gocara`) | both have visible form as object | the relevant sensory domain is shared |
| cognition (`vijñāna`) | both support one eye-cognition | their cognitive support relation does not require two eye-domains |

`Jāti` receives the specific gloss of shared eye-nature. It does not mean
birth, caste, or merely a resemblance of outward shape here. `Gocara` is
controlled by `rūpaviṣayatva`, having visible form as object. This concerns
the kind of sensory object, not a claim that each eye has an identical
spatial view or receives every visible detail equally.

`Ekacakṣurvijñānāśrayatva` says that both are supports of one eye-cognition.
The continuous translation retains that wording. In the counting argument,
it explains the common cognitive relation of the pair. The passage does
not develop an account of visual fusion or assert that both eyes must be
active in every episode of seeing.

All three grounds belong to the answer. The text does not select cognition
as the sole criterion and discard kind and object-range. Its unity is
articulated through their conjunction.

## 6. Applying the Reasoning to Ear and Nose

`Evaṃ ... api yojyam`, “the same is also to be applied,” directs an
extension of the eye argument. The Bhāṣya does not repeat each clause.
Expanded in the analysis, the ear pair shares ear-nature, sound as its
object, and the corresponding auditory cognition; the nasal pair shares
the olfactory faculty's nature, odor as its object, and the corresponding
olfactory cognition.

The reference to nostril-openings in the closing sentence concerns the
bodily sites. It should not undo the distinction from 1.09 between the
sensory faculty as material clarity and the gross anatomical site.
The present objection is generated by bodily pairing, but the reply's
criteria concern the faculty and its sensory relations.

Thus the number of bodily sites does not itself determine the number of
dhātus. The same principle operates differently from 1.17: there, overlapping
cognitive contents retained distinct positions to make a support relation
explicit; here, two bodily instances retain one domain because the relevant
kind, object-range, and cognitive relation are common.

## 7. The Explanation through Beauty

The second half of the verse introduces `śobhārtham`, for beauty. The
prose supplies the genitive `āśrayasya`, of the support, here the bodily
basis whose arrangement is being discussed. `Dvayoḥ saṃbhavaḥ` states
the occurrence of two, while `ekadhātutve 'pi` preserves the already
established one-domain status.

The concluding counterfactual is expressed by `anyathā ... syāt`: otherwise,
there would be considerable `vairūpya`, disfigurement. The imagined single
sites explain what the passage means by its appeal to beauty. This is the
text's aesthetic judgment about bodily arrangement, retained as such.

“Symmetry” can help a reader understand the contrast, but the explicit
term is beauty. The translation does not replace it with a claim about
sensory efficiency, adaptive advantage, or physiological necessity. Those
would require a different explanation from the one given here.

The two halves therefore answer different questions: why the domain is
counted once, and why its bodily sites occur as a pair. A reason for the
latter is not automatically a reason for the former.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

1. The counting objection concerns three paired faculties and the proposed
   increase from eighteen to twenty-one.
2. The three commonalities are separately explained through faculty-nature,
   sensory object, and support of corresponding cognition.
3. `Ekadhātutā` preserves classificatory unity while admitting bodily duality.
4. The eye argument is expressly extended to ear and nose.
5. “One eye-cognition” is retained without importing a fuller physiological
   theory into the local statement.
6. Pairing is explained through the beauty of the bodily support; that
   explanation remains distinct from the grounds of domain-unity.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage gives a concrete test of domain identity. Counting visible or
bodily instances is insufficient; one must determine the kind, range, and
cognitive relation under which they belong. Two sites can instantiate the
same domain without ceasing to be two sites.

For the Organon reconstruction, this complements the previous account of
manas. Classification can preserve different roles where contents overlap,
and it can preserve one role across multiple instances. The ground of the
count must be stated in the relevant determination, rather than assumed
from the number of things encountered.

This is useful for our inquiry into learning because recognizing repeated
instances and understanding their domain are distinguishable achievements.
A domain is learned through the relations that establish its unity, not
merely by collecting examples. The Bhāṣya supplies a particular case of
those relations; it does not yet give a general account of how the capacity
to understand them develops.

Its threefold criterion also keeps our knowledge-domain interpretation
precise. Cognition belongs within the explanation of domain identity,
together with faculty-nature and object-range. Their systematic relation
is the positive content the Organon study can develop.
