# VAK_1.35 Bhāṣya — Primary Elements, Derived Matter, and Atomic Aggregation

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> spraṣṭavyaṃ dvividhaṃ śeṣā rūpiṇo nava bhautikāḥ /
>
> dharmadhātvekadeśaś ca saṃcitā daśa rūpiṇaḥ // 1.35 //

> The tangible is twofold. The other nine material domains are derived
> from the elements, as is a portion of the dharma-domain. The ten
> material domains are aggregated.

The predicate *bhautika* carries into “a portion of the dharma-domain.”
The Bhāṣya identifies that portion as avijñapti. Merely translating it
as “a portion is material” loses the more specific determination.
The final statement introduces aggregation as a separate classification.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

How many domains have the nature of primary elements, and how many
are derived from the elements?

The tangible is twofold: primary elements and derived matter. The
primary elements are four; derived tangible matter is sevenfold,
beginning with smoothness. [It is called derived] because it occurs
in the elements.

The other nine material domains are derived: the five faculty-domains
and the four object-domains. These nine are exclusively derived.
A portion of the dharma-domain is also derived—the portion designated
avijñapti. The remaining citta-domains and the dharma-domain apart from
avijñapti are neither of the two.

The venerable Buddhadeva says, “The ten bases are nothing but primary
elements.” This is not so: the sūtra determines the primary elements
as four, with hardness and the other characteristics; moreover, these
are tangible. Hardness and the like are not apprehended by the eye and
the other [non-bodily] faculties, nor are color and the like apprehended
by the body-faculty.

It is also said in a sūtra: “The eye, monk, is an internal base: a
sensitive clarity of matter dependent upon the four great elements,
[material], invisible, resistant.” So likewise through the body.
“Visible forms, monk, are an external base: dependent upon the four
great elements, material, visible, resistant. Sound, monk, is an external
base: dependent upon the four great elements, material, invisible,
resistant.” Likewise smells and tastes. “Tangibles, monk, are an external
base: the four great elements and what depends upon the four great
elements, material, invisible, resistant.”

Since the elements are included in just one portion of the tangible
base, it is clearly shown that the remainder are not primary elements.

But what of the sūtra's statement, “Whatever in the fleshy mass of the
eye is hard, has the character of hardness”? — This is an instruction
concerning the fleshy mass [occurring inseparably with that faculty].

“A person, monk, consists of six domains”: in the account of descent
into the womb, this serves to show the fundamental constituents of a
being. For the six bases of contact are mentioned again, and otherwise
the consequence would be the absence of mental factors.

Nor is it reasonable to admit that mental factors are just citta.
For a sūtra says, “Saṃjñā and feeling are mental dharmas, following citta
and dependent upon citta”; and there are statements about citta with
passion and the like. Thus the domains' being primary elements or
derived matter is as stated.

How many are aggregated, and how many unaggregated? The ten material
domains are aggregated: the five faculty-domains and five object-domains.
Because they are aggregates of atoms, it follows that the remaining
domains are not aggregated.

The bracketed flesh-clause translation expresses the contextual reply
to the objection, not a secure restoration of the damaged compound.
The text refers to an account of descent into the womb; no independent
identification of a particular sūtra edition is asserted here.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The exposition first gives the primary-element/derived-matter division.
Buddhadeva is then named as holding that the ten bases are primary
elements alone. The commentary rejects that position through elemental
characteristics, the differentiation of sensory apprehension, and an
extended sūtra citation.

Two further scriptural formulations are considered: hardness in the
fleshy eye and the person as six domains. The first is answered by
distinguishing the faculty from its inseparably accompanying flesh.
The second receives a contextual explanation and leads to an argument
for distinguishing mental factors from citta.

The attribution of Buddhadeva's initial statement is explicit. The
subsequent scriptural objections are not individually assigned to a
named speaker. The study therefore presents them as objections within
the discussion rather than new verbatim claims attributed to him.
The assertion “as stated” closes that discussion before the question
about aggregation begins.

## 5. The Distribution of Bhūta and Bhautika

The tangible domain comprises the four elements and seven derived
tangibles, here abbreviated as “beginning with smoothness.” The prose
relies on the earlier enumeration rather than repeating all seven.
*Bhūteṣu bhavatvāt* supplies the explanatory derivation of *bhautika*:
occurring in or depending upon the elements. “Material” alone would
fail to distinguish it from the elements themselves.

The other nine material domains are five faculties plus visible form,
sound, smell, and taste. Avijñapti belongs to the dharma-domain and is
also derived. The seven citta-domains and the remainder of the
dharma-domain are neither primary elements nor derived matter.

The rival statement uses *āyatana*, whereas the governing inventory
uses *dhātu*. Its ten are the material faculty-and-object bases relevant
to the argument. This shift of inventory vocabulary should be retained
rather than silently rewritten in the Sanskrit quotation.

## 6. Dependence, Apprehension, and the Scriptural Replies

The first refutation joins two determinations. Primary elements have
specified characteristics, and those characteristics are apprehended
in touch. The other material fields cannot simply be substituted for
them: color is not apprehended by the body-faculty as color, and
hardness is not apprehended by vision as hardness.

The sūtra formula reinforces the distinction through *upādāya*,
“depending upon.” For the faculties and non-tangible objects it gives
dependent matter; for the tangible base it expressly includes both
the elements and dependent matter. The distinction is therefore within
the cited classification itself.

The eye-flesh objection tests whether physical conjunction defeats
that separation. Its reply locates hardness in the fleshy mass
accompanying the faculty. Co-occurrence does not establish identity of
the two determinations. Because the transmitted sentence is damaged,
this study keeps that contextual construal provisional.

The six-domain statement is likewise given a limited explanatory task:
showing fundamental constituents in the context of embryonic descent.
The reference to the six contact-bases and the consequence concerning
mental factors resist treating the short formula as an exhaustive
reduction of every category. The text then expressly rejects the
identification of mental factors with citta, citing their dependence
on citta and expressions such as citta “with passion.” Dependence is
used to articulate a relation between distinguishable terms.

This restored section matters to the argument as a whole. The dispute
is not closed after the first elemental quotation; the commentary
also examines how abbreviated scriptural descriptions should be read.

## 7. Atomic Aggregation Is a Different Predicate

*Saṃcita* is explained by *paramāṇusaṃghātatva*, being an aggregate
of atoms. This is a technical material classification, not a general
claim about accumulation of experiences, karma, or conceptual content.

| Domain or portion | Elemental status | Aggregated? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tangible domain | Primary elements and derived matter | Yes |
| Other nine material domains | Derived matter | Yes |
| Avijñapti within dharma-domain | Derived matter | No |
| Seven citta-domains and remaining dharma-domain | Neither | No |

The first row prevents a misleading hierarchy in which all aggregated
matter would be derived matter alone. The ten aggregated domains
include the tangible domain and therefore its elemental portion.
The avijñapti row establishes the converse limit: being derived matter
does not entail being atomically aggregated.

The source distinguishes these predicates without making them three
successive stages of production. An arrow from primary elements to
derived matter to aggregation would conceal the crossing classifications.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

- “Twofold” divides the tangible into four primary elements and seven
  derived tangibles.
- The remaining nine are derived matter alone.
- *Bhautika* also governs the dharma-domain portion, identified as
  avijñapti.
- Buddhadeva's contrary thesis is explicitly attributed; the subsequent
  objections receive contextual replies that must not be omitted.
- The ten material domains are aggregated in the atomic sense.
- Avijñapti is derived but unaggregated; the tangible domain is
  aggregated and includes both elemental and derived portions.

The original research kārikā and commentary remain unchanged. Their
compressed translation and hierarchical reconstruction are refined
only in this official study.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage distinguishes dependence from identity through more than
one case: derived matter depends on elements, the faculty accompanies
flesh, and mental factors depend on citta. Each relation needs its own
terms. A common basis does not eliminate differentiated characteristics
or functions.

For the Organon inquiry into learning, the restored citta–mental-factor
argument is particularly relevant. It permits questions about how an
associated factor contributes to a cognition without dissolving that
factor into citta in general. It does not itself specify the acquisition
of prajñā, but it protects the distinctions that such an explanation
would require.

The crossing of material classifications supplies a second discipline.
Being derived and being aggregated cannot be substituted for each
other. In the same way, an account of manifestation within saṃvṛti
would need to distinguish causal dependence, constitution, and cognitive
function before relating them to paramārtha. That extension remains
Organon interpretation; the source's immediate conclusions concern
elements, derived matter, and atomic aggregates.
