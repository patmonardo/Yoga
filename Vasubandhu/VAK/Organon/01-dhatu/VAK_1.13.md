# VAK_1.13

## 1. Sanskrit (Devanāgarī)

> पृथिवी वर्णसंस्थानमुच्यते लोकसंज्ञया ।
>
> आपस्तेजश्च वायुस्तु धातुरेव तथापि च ॥ १.१३ ॥

## 2. Sanskrit (IAST)

> pṛthivī varṇasaṃsthānam ucyate lokasaṃjñayā /
>
> āpas tejaś ca vāyus tu dhātur eva tathāpi ca // 1.13 //

## 3. Lexical Analysis


| Form | Morphology | Lexical force here |
|---|---|---|
| pṛthivī | nominative feminine singular | earth as ordinarily designated |
| varṇa-saṃsthānam | nominative neuter singular compound | color and configuration considered together as a visible presentation |
| ucyate | third-person singular present passive of `√vac` | is called |
| loka-saṃjñayā | instrumental feminine singular | by worldly designation or common naming |
| āpaḥ | nominative plural form used for water | water |
| tejaḥ | nominative neuter singular | fire |
| ca | conjunction | and |
| vāyuḥ | nominative masculine singular | wind |
| tu | contrastive particle | but, however; marks wind as a special case |
| dhātuḥ | nominative masculine singular | elemental domain |
| eva | restrictive/emphatic particle | itself, precisely |
| tathā api ca | adverbial and conjunctive phrase | nevertheless also in the same way |

`Lokasaṃjñā` is the controlling term. It means neither falsehood nor an
arbitrary private name. It is the conventional designation determined by
what ordinary cognition can point out as color and configuration.

## 4. Grammar

The first pāda is a passive naming construction:

```text
varṇa-saṃsthānam
    pṛthivī ucyate
    loka-saṃjñayā

color and configuration
    are called “earth”
    by worldly designation
```

Although `pṛthivī` is feminine and `varṇa-saṃsthānam` is neuter, they do not
stand as two agreeing adjectives. The neuter compound names the presented
complex to which the conventional name “earth” is applied.

The Bhāṣya extends this construction elliptically to water and fire:

```text
earth, water, fire
    → worldly designation of visible color and configuration
```

`Tu` then gives wind a special syntax. `Vāyuḥ dhātuḥ eva`: what the world
calls wind may be the wind-domain itself, encountered as impulsion rather
than first as a stable visible mass.

`Tathā api ca` qualifies that exception rather than erasing it. Wind can
nevertheless be designated like the other three through associated visible
appearance, as in the Bhāṣya's examples of a “blue windstorm” or “circular
windstorm.” The full distribution is therefore:

```text
earth, water, fire
    ordinary designation → visible color and configuration

wind
    ordinary designation → wind-domain itself
    and also              → associated color and configuration
```

The Kārikā cannot be construed as saying that water, fire, and wind are
“likewise only domains.” That reading loses the contrast marked by `tu` and
the qualification carried by `tathā api ca`.

## 5. Translation

### Close syntactic construe

> By worldly designation, color and configuration are called “earth”; so too are water and fire. Wind, however, is the domain itself—although it also is designated in that other way.

### Bhāṣya-informed translation

> In ordinary usage, “earth,” “water,” and “fire” name visible complexes of color and configuration. “Wind” may name the wind-domain itself, encountered as impulsion, although visible formations associated with wind may also receive that conventional name.

The supplied phrase “so too are water and fire” follows the Bhāṣya's explicit
resolution of the compressed second line. The second translation makes the
referent of “that other way” readable without replacing the Kārikā's
technical distinction with an ontology of hidden substances.

## 6. Philosophical Translation

> Ordinary usage calls visible color and configuration “earth,” “water,” or
> “fire.” It can call the wind-dhātu itself “wind,” while also naming visible
> color and configuration as wind. The Bhāṣya then asks what unifies the
> entire rūpa aggregate, including avijñapti, under one designation.

Organon rendering (project reconstruction):

> Worldly naming and technical determination can refer to the same
> situation under different relations. Wind prevents a simple rule that
> every worldly name designates only an appearance. The ensuing debate
> tests whether the proposed definition of rūpa really reaches each member
> of the aggregate, especially non-disclosive avijñapti.

The Bhāṣya then asks why the entire series from the five faculties through
avijñapti is called `rūpa`. It proposes `raupaṇa`, being affected, and
explains the affecting of material form as production of alteration. A
second account, attributed to others, appeals to obstruction. The rest of
the unit tests how far these accounts can extend.

## 8. Logical Determination

The kārikā and Bhāṣya first distribute ordinary designation and technical
determination asymmetrically:

```text
earth / water / fire as worldly names
    → visible color and configuration

wind as worldly name
    → wind-dhātu itself
    → also visible color and configuration in some usages

DhatuDetermination(d)
    → HasIntrinsicCharacteristic(d, s)
    ∧ HasFunction(d, k)
```

Thus:

```text
ordinary earth
    = visible complex called “earth”

earth-domain
    = hardness functioning as support

ordinary earth ≠ earth-domain
```

The Bhāṣya proposes and tests a common criterion for the rūpa aggregate:

```text
proposed: rūpa is susceptible to being affected
local gloss: affecting of rūpa = production of alteration
others' account: obstruction by material form
```

Objections test its range:

```text
isolated atom?
    → reply: no separately existing single material atom;
      the atom is affected within an aggregate

past and future form?
    → affected / to-be-affected / belonging to the same kind

avijñapti?
    → being affected through manifestation is proposed, then rejected
    → classification through affected elemental support is debated
```

The avijñapti debate distinguishes proposed explanations from objections:

```text
shadow/tree and radiance/jewel analogy
    → rejected: their material basis and cessation conditions differ

final reply by others
    → cognition has differentiated supports, including mind;
      avijñapti does not have that same range of supports
```

The cognition objection also establishes a limit on the proposed support
criterion:

```text
DependsForArising(cognition, materialFaculty)
    ↛ Material(cognition)
```

The final differentiated-support reply is reported as another position. It
does not reinstate the rejected analogies or give an unrestricted theory of
how every later moment of avijñapti is supported.

## 9. Interpretive Note

VAK 1.13 brings the distinction between outward appearance and inward
Dharma-determination into the text's own method. Ordinary language points to
a color and configuration and calls it earth. Abhidharma asks what
characteristic and function make the elemental complex intelligible. Dhātu
therefore names a domain of determination, not a subtler piece of matter
hidden behind the visible piece.

The extended Bhāṣya makes the result stronger. Rūpa is unified by its
susceptibility to alteration, yet avijñapti resists easy inclusion under that
definition. The proposed analogies of tree and shadow or jewel and radiance
are criticized because avijñapti may continue after its originating material
supports have ceased. The classification is retained, but its exact
ontological defense remains disputed. Our study should preserve that
difficulty rather than pretending that “Dharma-controlled reality” has
already solved the Vaibhāṣika problem.

The Organon result is nevertheless powerful. Dharma is real as lawful
determination, as number is real, without becoming a sensible object. Its
dialectic relates material faculty and cognition while preventing their
collapse:

```text
material faculty
    = condition for the arising of sensory cognition

sensory cognition
    ≠ material effect contained in the faculty
```

In this exact sense Dharma keeps spirit and matter apart. It does not isolate
them into unrelated worlds; it maintains their determinate difference within
one articulated relation. The inward turn of Dhātu is therefore not an
erasure of matter but comprehension of the lawful distinctions through which
matter, appearance, faculty, and cognition can stand together.
