# VAK_1.12 Bhāṣya — The Great Elements: Sustaining, Function, and Own-Nature

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> bhūtāni pṛthivīdhātur aptejovāyudhātavaḥ /
>
> dhṛtyādikarmasaṃsiddhāḥ kharasnehoṣṇateraṇāḥ // 1.12 //

> The elements are the earth-dhātu and the water-, fire-, and wind-dhātus.
> They are established in functions beginning with sustaining; their
> own-natures are hardness, cohesiveness, heat, and impulsion.

The Bhāṣya answers the question generated by avijñapti's dependence on the
great elements. It explains the designations `dhātu` and `mahābhūta`,
distinguishes function from own-nature, and examines how the definition of
wind agrees with cited descriptions.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

> “Depending upon the great elements” was stated. Which elements are these?
> The elements are the earth-dhātu and the water-, fire-, and wind-dhātus.
> These four are dhātus because they sustain their own characteristics and
> derived material form; they are called the four great elements. Their
> greatness is due to their magnitude as the support of all other material
> form. Alternatively, it is because of their extensive presence in masses
> of earth, water, fire, and wind in which the corresponding element operates
> prominently.
>
> In what functions are these dhātus established, and what are their
> own-natures? It is said: established in functions beginning with sustaining.
> The earth-, water-, fire-, and wind-dhātus are respectively established in
> the functions of sustaining, gathering together, maturing, and expanding.
> Expanding is to be understood as increase and spreading. These are their
> functions. Their own-natures, respectively, are hardness, cohesiveness,
> heat, and impulsion.
>
> Hardness is the earth-dhātu. Cohesiveness is the water-dhātu. Heat is the
> fire-dhātu. Impulsion is the wind-dhātu. It is called impulsion because
> through it the stream of elements is impelled, by being produced in another
> place, as in the impulsion of a lamp flame.
>
> In the treatises and in a sūtra it is stated: “What is the wind-dhātu?
> Light movement.” But that lightness is also said in the treatises to be
> derived material form. Therefore wind is the dharma whose own-nature is
> impulsion: its own-nature is made evident through its function.

## 4. Movement of the Commentary

The exposition connects the material dependence stated in 1.11 with an
analysis of the basis upon which derived form depends:

```text
Which great elements?
    → earth-, water-, fire-, and wind-dhātus
Why dhātus?
    → sustaining own-characteristics and derived form
Why great?
    → magnitude as support; alternatively, extensive presence in masses
What functions and what own-natures?
    → two corresponding fourfold lists
How does the wind definition fit the cited wording?
    → distinguish derived lightness from elemental impulsion
    → function makes own-nature evident
```

The voice remains explanatory. A formulation from treatises and a sūtra is
cited and examined, but no separate speaker is explicitly introduced as an
objector. Unlike 1.11, this unit does not expressly name the Vaibhāṣikas.
The attribution should therefore remain at the level the prose supplies.

## 5. Sustaining and the Designation “Great”

**Dhātu through dhāraṇa.** The compound
`svalakṣaṇopādāyarūpadhāraṇāt` explains the designation through sustaining
own-characteristics and derived material form. Its ablative ending gives
the reason for calling the four `dhātavaḥ`. It joins the characteristic of
the elemental basis to the material form dependent upon that basis.

This is the local support for interpreting an elemental dhātu as a sustaining
domain. The continuous translation retains `dhātu`; the domain rendering
in the Organon analysis must carry the active relation supplied by
`dhāraṇa`. An inert enclosure would lose that relation.

`Upādāyarūpa` names derived material form, continuing the dependence stated
in 1.11 and already encountered in the sensory-faculty account. The passage
thus gives the same four elements both their own determination and a
supporting relation to other material form.

**Two explanations of greatness.** In
`sarvānyarūpāśrayatvenaudārikatvāt`, the instrumental
`sarvānyarūpāśrayatvena` connects being the support of all other material
form with `audārikatva`, magnitude or grossness. The translation reads
“magnitude as the support of all other material form.” The syntax does not
simply list support and grossness as two independent reasons joined by
“and.” “Magnitude” here expresses the stated greatness; it does not
introduce a measured physical size.

The explicit alternative begins with `atha vā`. It concerns extensive
presence, `mahāsaṃniveśa`, in the masses of earth, water, fire, and wind.
`Tadudbhūtavṛttiṣu` is provisionally understood as referring to the
prominent operation of the corresponding element within those masses.
The research report's “masses that arise from them” instead emphasizes
derivation. The present reading preserves the link between prominence,
operation, and extensive presence, while leaving the compound marked for
philological review.

The word `skandha` in this compound denotes a mass or accumulation. The
passage is not enumerating another set of psychological aggregates.
The exact relation of conventional earth and the earth-dhātu becomes the
next unit's explicit question.

## 6. Function and Own-Nature

The commentary asks two questions with `kasmin karmaṇi` and
`kiṃsvabhāvāḥ`: in what function are they established, and of what
own-nature are they? It then marks the division again with
`idam eṣāṃ karma / svabhāvas tu`, “these are their functions; their
own-natures, however.”

| Dhātu | Function (`karma`) | Own-nature (`svabhāva`) |
|---|---|---|
| earth | `dhṛti`: sustaining | `khara`: hardness |
| water | `saṃgraha`: gathering together | `sneha`: cohesiveness |
| fire | `pakti`: maturing | `uṣṇatā`: heat |
| wind | `vyūhana`: expanding | `īraṇā`: impulsion |

`Yathākramam`, “respectively,” governs the correspondences. The kārikā's
`dhṛtyādi` supplies only the opening member of the function list; the
Bhāṣya expands the remaining three. Its following terms specify the
own-natures, not further examples of functions.

The water terms are closely related, but rendering both simply “cohesion”
would obscure their different roles. “Gathering together” preserves the
operative force of `saṃgraha`, while “cohesiveness” identifies the
material nature named `sneha`. Moistness is another possible explanatory
rendering of the latter; the passage offers no separate gloss deciding
between those English nuances.

For wind, `vyūhana` is expressly explained through `vṛddhi` and
`prasarpaṇa`, increase and spreading. “Motion” alone would omit the
expansive function being specified. The element's own-nature, impulsion,
receives a further explanation in the next sentence.

## 7. Impulsion, Lightness, and the Disclosure of Nature

**A stream produced elsewhere.** The instrumental `anayā`, “through this,”
in the derivation of `īraṇā` connects impulsion with the impelling of a
`bhūtasrotas`, a stream of elements. `Deśāntarotpādanāt` specifies
production in another place. The lamp-flame comparison illustrates that
relation.

The explanatory emphasis falls on a stream's production at another location.
This supports a process reading of movement. A complete theory of
momentariness or a general denial of enduring substances would require more
than this local derivation; neither is inserted into the translation.

**The cited definition.** `Laghusamudīraṇatvam` is retained as a compound
and rendered provisionally “light movement.” The research report's
“lightness and movement” makes its components a coordinate pair; the
compound itself also allows a qualifying relation, movement characterized
as light. The following prose singles out `laghutva`, lightness, and says
that it is derived material form in the treatises. Whichever English
construal is adopted for the quotation, that subsequent distinction remains
explicit.

This returns to the tangible list in 1.10, where lightness was enumerated
in addition to the four elements. The present clarification prevents its
appearance in a description of wind from erasing the distinction between
elemental impulsion and derived lightness.

The conclusion is not merely another list entry:

> karmaṇā 'sya svabhāvo 'bhivyaktaḥ

Its own-nature is made evident through its function. The instrumental
`karmaṇā` states how the nature becomes evident; `svabhāvaḥ` remains what
is disclosed. The two determinations are distinguishable and connected.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

1. The elemental names receive their local explanation through sustaining
   own-characteristics and derived form.
2. The explanations of greatness distinguish magnitude as support from the
   explicitly alternative account of extensive presence in material masses.
3. Function and own-nature require separate lists. The verse's final four
   terms cannot simply be appended to the function list after a colon.
4. `Dhṛtyādi` is expanded as sustaining, gathering, maturing, and expanding;
   expansion specifically includes increase and spreading.
5. Wind's impulsion is explained through production of an elemental stream
   elsewhere, illustrated by the lamp flame.
6. Lightness is identified as derived form, while wind is determined by
   impulsion. The cited compound's English construal remains provisional.
7. The concluding relation is that function makes own-nature evident.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

A domain here has its own determinate character, sustains dependent form,
and operates in a specified way. These relations give “domain” a concrete
content. The explanatory task is to understand how sustaining, functioning,
and characteristic belong together without substituting one for another.

The wind analysis is especially instructive because the commentary explicitly
connects function to the disclosure of nature. Operation is a means through
which a determination becomes evident. This gives our Organon inquiry into
knowing a definite relation to investigate: how an operation makes the
character of its supporting domain intelligible.

The claim remains more precise than identifying every dhātu with cognition.
The passage determines material elements and their functions. The Organon
reconstruction asks how those determinations become contents of systematic
understanding, and how learning comes to discriminate function, own-nature,
and dependence within a material field.

Read with 1.10 and 1.11, the progression is articulated: sensory domains
admit internally differentiated contents; avijñapti adds a continuing
material determination without outward disclosure; the elements supply the
basis whose own-natures and operations are now distinguished. The question
of manifestation can therefore be pursued through these concrete relations,
without treating all forms of disclosure as the same operation.
