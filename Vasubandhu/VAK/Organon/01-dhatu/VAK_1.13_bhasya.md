# VAK_1.13 Bhāṣya — Conventional Names and the Contested Unity of Rūpa

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> pṛthivī varṇasaṃsthānam ucyate lokasaṃjñayā /
>
> āpas tejaś ca vāyus tu dhātur eva tathāpi ca // 1.13 //

> By worldly designation, color and configuration are called earth; so too
> water and fire. Wind, however, is the dhātu itself, yet is also designated
> in that way.

The Bhāṣya distinguishes worldly elemental names from the dhātus defined
in 1.12, preserving wind's special case. It then asks why the entire series
ending in avijñapti is called the rūpa aggregate and tests the proposed
definition against atoms, past and future form, and avijñapti.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

> What, then, is the difference between earth and the rest and the
> earth-dhātu and the rest? By worldly designation, color and configuration
> are called earth. For when people point out earth, they point out color
> and configuration. As with earth, so also with water and fire: color and
> configuration alone are called by those names in worldly designation.
>
> Wind, however, is the dhātu itself. The very wind-dhātu is what is called
> wind in the world. Yet it is also designated in that way. Just as color
> and configuration are called earth by worldly designation, so also wind:
> “a blue windstorm,” “a circular windstorm.”
>
> Why is this series, extending as far as avijñapti, called the aggregate of
> rūpa? Because of being affected. The Blessed One said: “It is affected,
> it is affected, monks; therefore it is called the aggregate of appropriation
> consisting of rūpa. By what is it affected? Even when touched by the hand,
> it is affected,” and so forth. “It is affected” means “it is afflicted.”
> For it is said in the Arthavargīya verses: “If the desires of that embodied
> person who desires, in whom longing has arisen, are not fulfilled, he is
> afflicted as though pierced by a dart.”
>
> What, then, is the affliction of rūpa? The production of alteration.
> Others say: obstruction by material form. Then atomic form would not
> qualify as rūpa, because it is not affected. But a single atomic form does
> not exist separately; situated in an aggregate, it is indeed affected.
> Then past and future form would not qualify as rūpa. That too has been
> affected, will be affected, and belongs to that kind; hence it is rūpa,
> as with fuel.
>
> Then avijñapti would not qualify. It too becomes affected through the
> affecting of manifestation, as a shadow moves when a tree moves. No,
> because it does not undergo such modification. Moreover, when manifestation
> ceased, avijñapti would cease, as a shadow is absent when the tree is absent.
>
> Others say: because the elements serving as its support are affected.
> Then eye-cognition and the rest would also have to be rūpa, because their
> supports are affected. This comparison is unequal. Avijñapti continues
> depending upon the elements as a shadow upon a tree or radiance upon a
> jewel. Eye-cognition and the rest do not continue depending upon the eye
> and the other faculties in that way; those faculties are merely conditions
> for their arising.
>
> To begin with, the claim that a shadow continues depending upon a tree and
> radiance upon a jewel is not Vaibhāṣika, since it is accepted that each
> color atom of shadow and the rest depends upon its own set of four elements.
> Even if shadow and radiance did depend upon those things, avijñapti could
> not reasonably be dependent in just that way. It is accepted that avijñapti
> does not cease even when the great elements supporting it have ceased.
> Therefore this is not a successful reply.
>
> Others offer a reply here: the support of eye-cognition and the rest is
> differentiated. Some support, such as the eye, is affected; some, such as
> mind, is not affected. This is not the case with avijñapti. Therefore the
> consequence is not parallel. On this ground it is reasonable to call it
> rūpa because its support is affected.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

Two questions organize the unit: how ordinary elemental names relate to
technical dhātus, and what makes the entire rūpa aggregate one category.
The second develops through successive tests of the proposed criterion.

| Movement | Voice or status indicated locally |
|---|---|
| worldly names and wind's qualification | explanatory prose |
| derivation through being affected | prose supported by scriptural citations |
| obstruction by material form | others' explanation |
| atomic and temporal objections and replies | unassigned dialectical exchange |
| manifestation and tree-shadow analogy | proposed reply, then rejected |
| elemental support and cognition objection | others' reply, then a challenge |
| continuous dependence and jewel-radiance analogy | proposed defense |
| non-Vaibhāṣika analogy and ceased supports | explicit criticism of that defense |
| differentiated supports | another reply attributed to others |

The phrase `avaibhāṣikīyam` explicitly characterizes one proposed analogy
as non-Vaibhāṣika. It does not justify assigning every preceding reply to a
named Vaibhāṣika speaker. The last reply is left standing within this unit;
its presentation as others' reply should remain visible.

## 5. Worldly Designation and Wind's Special Case

`Lokasaṃjñayā` gives the mode of naming: by worldly designation. The
explanation appeals to an act of showing. When people point out earth,
they show color and configuration. Water and fire follow that pattern.
The dhātus defined in 1.12 by hardness, cohesiveness, and heat are therefore
distinguished from what is visually indicated under the ordinary names.

The verse's syntax is asymmetric. `Āpas tejaś ca` continues the first
clause; `vāyus tu` introduces the contrast. The prose's
`ya eva ... sa eva` identifies the wind-dhātu itself with what the world
calls wind. `Tathāpi ca` then extends designation through color and
configuration to wind as well. The blue and circular windstorms illustrate
the two visible determinations.

The research kārikā's literal translation groups water, fire, and wind
together as “only domains.” The Bhāṣya requires the corrected grouping in
the anchor above. The worldly name can coincide with a technical
determination in wind's case while also admitting a visible use.

This is a local account of conventional naming. It supplies no explicit
classification of each worldly name under saṃvṛti and each dhātu under
paramārtha. That further relation belongs to philosophical investigation.

## 6. Rūpa through Being Affected

The question `avijñaptiparyantaḥ` gathers the whole series through its last
member, avijñapti. `Raupaṇāt` supplies the derivational answer. The
scriptural `rūpyate` is glossed by `bādhyate`, being afflicted or affected;
the Arthavargīya verse illustrates this usage through frustrated desire.
That illustration establishes a verbal sense. It does not make frustrated
desire an additional material constituent.

The next question specifies what affliction means for rūpa:
`vipariṇāmotpādanā`, production of alteration. Another account gives
`pratighāto rūpeṇa`, obstruction by material form. The two formulations
remain distinguishable even though both concern being acted upon.

The scriptural citation speaks of the rūpa aggregate of appropriation,
whereas the question concerns the entire rūpa aggregate. The wording of the
citation is preserved; the earlier distinction between aggregates and
aggregates of appropriation is not erased by this etymological use.

**Atoms and temporal status.** The atomic objection is answered through
aggregation: the reply denies a separately existing single atom and locates
its being affected within a material aggregate. It does not deny atoms
as constituents. The next objection asks about past and future form. The
answer appeals to having been affected, going to be affected, and belonging
to the same kind, `tajjātīya`. Fuel supplies the analogy for designation
beyond a presently occurring operation.

Thus present alteration is not demanded of every instance at every time.
The criterion is extended through aggregation and temporal-kind relations
before avijñapti presents the more difficult test.

## 7. Avijñapti and the Failed Dependence Analogies

**Dependence upon manifestation.** The first reply makes avijñapti affected
through the affecting of vijñapti, as a shadow moves with a tree. The
compressed `nāvikārāt` is construed as `na / avikārāt`: no, because of
non-modification. A second criticism tests cessation: the analogy would
require avijñapti to cease with manifestation. That consequence conflicts
with the continuing stream the account is meant to explain.

**Dependence upon elemental support.** The next reply shifts the basis to
the elements. The objection then invokes eye-cognition and the other
cognitions: if an affected support suffices, would these also become rūpa?
The proposed defense distinguishes continuing dependence from a mere
condition of arising, `utpattinimittamātra`. It illustrates the former by
shadow/tree and radiance/jewel.

That defense is then criticized twice. First, its examples do not match
the Vaibhāṣika account: the color atoms of shadows and radiance each have
their own four-element basis. Second, even granting the examples, avijñapti
is accepted as continuing after its supporting great elements have ceased.
The modeled dependence therefore does not fit the case being defended.

The criticism establishes what these analogies cannot explain. It does not
supply a complete replacement theory of the elements that sustain later
moments of avijñapti. In particular, continuation after the cited supports
cease is not silently expanded into independence from every material basis.

## 8. The Final Reply: Different Kinds of Support

The final `anye ... āhuḥ` introduces a further reply by others. It compares
the range of supports: those of cognition include affected supports such
as the eye and an unaffected support, mind. Avijñapti does not have that
same differentiation. Hence the proposed consequence is not parallel,
and classification as rūpa through its affected support is defended.

The force of “unaffected” here follows the local rūpa criterion. The
sentence does not assert that mind is unconditioned or incapable of any
change. It distinguishes mind from the material supports susceptible to
the kind of affecting under discussion.

The final reply changes the ground of the defense. It does not rehabilitate
the shadow and radiance analogies already criticized. It instead appeals
to the difference between the kinds of support available in the two cases.
No further objection follows before the next unit begins. The edition
therefore preserves both the local affirmative conclusion and its explicit
attribution to others.

## 9. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

1. Water and fire continue the worldly color/configuration clause. Wind
   receives its own contrasting clause and subsequent qualification.
2. `Tathāpi ca` allows worldly designation of wind through color and
   configuration; it does not flatten the four names into one formula.
3. The prose extends beyond the verse's naming distinction to examine the
   unity of the rūpa aggregate, including avijñapti.
4. Being affected is explained as production of alteration; obstruction is
   recorded as another account.
5. The replies concerning aggregation and temporal kind broaden application
   beyond an isolated present occurrence of alteration.
6. The avijñapti debate must retain the proposals, objections, rejected
   analogies, and final differentiated-support reply in their sequence.

## 10. Philosophical and Organon Study

The first movement shows that a conventional name and a technical
determination have to be compared in their actual use. Wind's special case
prevents a uniform separation of worldly names from dhātus. What is pointed
out, what characteristic is determined, and how the name applies are
related questions with different answers across the examples.

The second movement puts the unity of a domain under argumentative pressure.
A proposed common determination must account for its limiting cases:
atomic constituents, temporal modes, and a continuing non-disclosive form.
The meaning of the category develops through those tests.

For the Organon reconstruction, the crucial lesson concerns dependence.
An origin, an ongoing support, and a classificatory basis cannot simply be
exchanged. The question is how a particular dependence explains the
continuation and character of what depends. The criticism of the shadow
analogy demonstrates the work required: the example must agree with the
case both in constitution and in cessation conditions.

This bears on our inquiry into learning and the manifestation of prajñā
within saṃvṛti. A claim that knowing manifests under certain conditions
still needs an account of its origin, continuation, and determination.
The present debate supplies distinctions for posing that question; it does
not identify prajñā with avijñapti or settle the relation by a single
material-support analogy.
