# VAK_1.47 Bhāṣya — Hearing, Bodily Cognition, and Manas Across Levels

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> tathā śrotraṃ trayāṇāṃ tu sarvam eva svabhūmikam /
>
> kāyavijñānam adharasvabhūmy aniyataṃ manaḥ // 1.47 //

> Likewise the ear. For the three, however, everything belongs
> to its own level. Bodily cognition belongs to a lower or its
> own level; manas has no fixed level-relation.

The Bhāṣya identifies the three as nose, tongue, and body, then
expressly qualifies the general rule through bodily cognition.
*Aniyata* concerns the level-relation of manas, not ethical
indeterminacy or absence of conditions.

The unit begins with the transition to hearing at printed 36.03
and ends with the explicit closure of the incidental discussion
at 36.20. The new question at 36.21 concerning which cognition
knows which domain belongs to 1.48.

The running text's damaged *yathā cecaṃ cakṣuruttaṃ* is normalized
as *yathā cedaṃ cakṣur uktaṃ*, following the research witness.
*Atihahugrantha* is repaired to *atibahugrantha*, and the defective
ending of *kāmadhātuprathamadhyānopapannāṃ* is supplied as
*-opapannānām*, supported by the parallel *dvitīyādidhyānopapannānām*.
Word division is regularized. These are repairs of the local
transcription rather than a critical collation.

The compressed *utsargaviśeṣeṇa kṛtvā* is retained. Its translation
follows the explicit sequence of general statement and exception
without treating the difficult compound as evidence for a further
unstated category.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

As this has been stated for the eye, so it is to be understood
in detail for the ear: “The ear is not lower than the body;
sound is not higher than the ear, and neither is cognition.
But relative to that cognition, sound may be from any direction;
relative to the body, both may be from any direction.” The account
is to be applied in detail in this way.

For the three, however, everything belongs to its own level.
For the nose-, tongue-, and body-domains, body, object, and
cognition belong to their own level. Having stated the general
rule in this way, an exception is introduced to qualify it
further: bodily cognition belongs to a lower or its own level.

The body, the body-domain, and the tangible object always belong
to their own level. Bodily cognition, however, belongs to the
same level for some, such as those born in the desire realm or
the first dhyāna; for others it belongs to a lower level, such
as those born in the second or subsequent dhyānas.

Manas has no fixed level-relation. Sometimes manas belongs to the
same level as the body, mental cognition, and dharmas; sometimes
it belongs to a higher or lower level. For even with a body
belonging to the five levels, manas and the others can belong
to all levels, as appropriate at the time of meditative attainment
and rebirth. This will be explained in detail in the Kośasthāna
on meditative attainments.

To avoid the burden of an excessively lengthy text, it is not
explained again here. The benefit would be small and the labor
great. Thus the incidental discussion is concluded.

“From any direction” retains the higher/equal/lower comparison
explained in 1.46. “As appropriate” limits the statement about
manas and the others; it is not a claim that every combination
of levels occurs without restriction.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The passage begins by applying the previous rule to hearing.
It actually supplies a parallel verse-form formulation rather
than merely saying “likewise.” The continuous translation retains
that formulation so that the extension is readable on its own.

The next movement states a general rule for three faculties and
then expressly introduces an exception. The exception concerns
bodily cognition. It does not displace the rule for body,
body-faculty, and tangible object.

Finally, the exposition distinguishes manas, defers the detailed
distributions to the chapter on meditative attainments, and
closes the incidental discussion. No separate school or anonymous
rival position is named here. The deferral is part of the text's
own exposition and should remain in the readable edition.

## 5. The Extension to Hearing

| Comparison | Rule carried over from seeing |
|---|---|
| Ear relative to body | Same level or higher |
| Sound relative to ear | Same level or lower |
| Auditory cognition relative to ear | Same level or lower |
| Sound relative to auditory cognition | Higher, equal, or lower, as applicable |
| Sound and auditory cognition relative to body | Higher, equal, or lower, as applicable |

The terms change from eye, visible form, and visual cognition to
ear, sound, and auditory cognition. The relations are carried
over; their terms are not identified. *Asya* in the parallel
formulation refers to the cognition just mentioned, and *ubhe*
to sound and cognition.

As in 1.46, the final alternatives do not cancel the preceding
inequalities or the relevant ranges. “Likewise” is an instruction
to apply the complete account, not to retain only its least
restrictive clause.

## 6. The Three Faculties and the Bodily Exception

The prose explicitly distinguishes *kāya*, the body, from
*kāyadhātu*, the body-faculty domain. *Spraṣṭavya*, the tangible
object, is a third term; bodily cognition is a fourth. Translating
all of these through an undifferentiated “body” would conceal
the exception's exact scope.

| Case | Body, body-faculty, tangible | Bodily cognition |
|---|---|---|
| Desire-realm birth | Own level | Own level |
| First-dhyāna birth | Own level | Own level |
| Second or higher dhyāna birth | Own level | Lower level |

The table follows the explicit examples. The present prose says
“lower” for the last case; a fuller enumeration of cognition's
levels belongs to the wider classification, rather than being
silently inserted into this translation.

*Adharasvabhūmi* is resolved by the commentary as “of a lower or
its own level.” The general rule and exception must be read
together. Nor does the general statement establish that every
one of the three faculties exists at every level: it classifies
the relations where the relevant faculty and cognition occur.

## 7. Manas and the Deferred Distribution

*Aniyatam* is explained relationally: same, higher, or lower
relative to body, mental cognition, and dharmas. It should not
be translated in a way that confuses it with *avyākṛta*, ethical
indeterminacy, or with uncertainty about the existence of manas.

The comparison keeps manas distinct from mental cognition and
from the dharmas taken as objects. That distinction continues
the earlier treatment of manas as support rather than making
“mind” a single term that absorbs every mental function.

The text invokes meditative attainment and rebirth and qualifies
the distributions by *yathāyogam*, “as appropriate.” Its reference
to all levels must be read within that account. It does not
present a Cartesian product in which every support, object,
cognition, and bodily level can be independently combined.

The five-level body premise is retained from the preceding
material-body discussion. The clause is not an enumeration of
all possible modes of existence. The chapter explicitly promises
the fuller account elsewhere, and the present study preserves
that boundary.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

- Expand “likewise the ear” through the full parallel formulation.
- Identify the three as nose, tongue, and body.
- Read the bodily-cognition clause as an explicit exception.
- Keep body, body-faculty, tangible object, and cognition distinct.
- Construe *aniyata* as variable level-relation, not ethical status.
- Retain the qualification “as appropriate” for mental distributions.
- Preserve the author's deferral and closure of the incidental discussion.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

This unit displays three different kinds of rule: transfer of an
established relational pattern, a general statement qualified by
an exception, and a variable relation whose details are deferred.
They should not be forced into one uniform scheme.

For Organon study, the bodily exception is especially instructive.
A change in the classification of cognition does not require the
same change in body, faculty, and object. The terms participate
in one event while retaining distinct determinations.

Manas adds variability without abolishing those distinctions.
Its account is conditional and relational, not a declaration of
unlimited mental power. The textual result is a differentiated
classification of levels; a modern account of cognitive flexibility
or a comparative Yoga reconstruction would require further work.
