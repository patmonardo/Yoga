# VAK_1.38 Bhāṣya — The First Outflow-Free Moment, Acquisition, and Possession

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> tridhānye dravyavān ekaḥ kṣaṇikāḥ paścimās trayaḥ /
>
> cakṣurvijñānadhātvoḥ syāt pṛthag lābhaḥ sahāpi ca // 1.38 //

> The others are threefold. One possesses substance. The last three
> are momentary. Acquisition of the eye-domain and visual-cognition
> domain may occur separately and also together.

The Bhāṣya limits each compact expression. “Others” means four material
object-domains; “possesses substance” refers to the dharma-domain's
inclusion of the unconditioned; “momentary” has a special causal sense
at the first outflow-free moment. The last half begins a new inquiry
into acquisition, followed by a distinct inquiry into possession.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

The others are threefold. The other four remaining domains are visible
form, taste, smell, and tangible. They admit maturation-born,
reinforcement-produced, and homogeneous-continuation instances.

One possesses substance. The unconditioned is called substance because
of its substantial core or firmness. It is present in the dharma-domain;
therefore the dharma-domain alone contains substance [in this sense].

The last three are momentary. Manas, dharma, and mental cognition are
the last in the order of recitation. In the first outflow-free complex,
the receptivity to dharma-knowledge concerning suffering, they are
without homogeneous continuation for one moment. Therefore they are
called momentary. [Apart from this exception,] no conditioned dharma
is without homogeneous continuation.

There, the citta associated with receptivity to dharma-knowledge
concerning suffering is the manas-domain and mental-cognition domain.
The remaining dharmas arising together with it belong to the
dharma-domain.

This is considered: does one who, previously not possessing the
eye-domain, acquires possession of it also acquire possession of the
visual-cognition domain? Conversely, does one acquiring the latter
also acquire the eye-domain? It is said: acquisition of the eye-domain
and visual-cognition domain may occur separately and also together.

First, one may acquire the eye-domain without the visual-cognition
domain: when gradually acquiring the eye-faculty in the desire realm,
or when passing from the formless realm and being born in the second
or higher dhyānas.

One may acquire the visual-cognition domain without the eye-domain:
when born in the second or higher dhyānas and bringing visual cognition
into presence, or when passing from there and being born below.

One may acquire possession of both together: when passing from the
formless realm and being born in the desire realm or Brahmā world.
Neither: apart from the stated cases.

Does one possessing the eye-domain also possess the visual-cognition
domain? There are four cases. The first is one born in the second or
higher dhyānas who is not bringing visual cognition into presence.
The second is one in the desire realm who has not acquired the eye
or has lost it. The third is one in the desire realm who has acquired
and not lost the eye, one born in the first dhyāna, or one born in the
second or higher dhyānas who is seeing. The fourth comprises cases
other than these.

Likewise, acquisition and possession should be worked out as appropriate
for the eye-domain and visible-form domain, and for the visual-cognition
domain and visible-form domain. The word “and” in “also together and”
serves to include this further discussion.

The supplied exception phrase translates the difficult sentence
contextually. “Substantial core or firmness” is a provisional gloss of
*sāra*, not a physical property attributed to the unconditioned.

## 4. Movement and Voices of the Commentary

The first three determinations complete the classificatory questions
announced in 1.37. The text then explicitly opens a consideration with
*idaṃ vicāryate*. Its initial pair of questions concerns coming into
possession; the later question concerns already possessing.

The answers give realm-conditioned cases rather than a debate between
named schools. No rival voice is explicitly identified in this unit.
The final instruction extends the method to related pairs without
spelling out all their cases. The study preserves that instruction
rather than inventing a completed enumeration for them.

## 5. Three Causal Classes and One Domain Containing Dravya

The four remaining material domains complete the preceding distribution:
five faculties, sound, eight non-resistant domains, and these four
account for eighteen. Sound's exclusion from maturation-born status
in 1.37 explains why it is not among the four.

The next predicate changes the question. *Dravyavān* is explained by
the presence of the unconditioned within the dharma-domain. The prose
first gives *sāratvād dravyam* and then locates that content in one
domain. It does not assert that every member of the dharma-domain is
unconditioned, or that all other domains are unreal.

“Contains substance in this sense” therefore preserves both the local
use of *dravya* and the containment relation. Importing every other
Abhidharma use of substantial existence into this one count would
obscure the reason actually supplied.

## 6. The Special Sense of Kṣaṇika

The first outflow-free complex is named *duḥkhe dharmajñānakṣānti*.
“Receptivity to dharma-knowledge concerning suffering” keeps *kṣānti*
distinct from the knowledge it precedes in the technical expression.
The text identifies this particular event; it does not describe an
arbitrary first insight or every new act of learning.

*Kṣaṇam ekam anaiḥṣyandikāḥ* supplies the special criterion: for one
moment these dharmas are not products of homogeneous continuation.
This does not mean that only the last three domains have momentary
conditioned members while material dharmas endure unchanged.

Nor does absence of this causal relation mean absence of all causes.
The event is expressly within the discussion of conditioned dharmas.
The interpretive explanation is that a first outflow-free occurrence
has no earlier outflow-free homogeneous predecessor; that explanation
must remain narrower than spontaneous or unconditioned origination.

The citta counts under manas and mental cognition, and the remaining
co-arisen dharmas under the dharma-domain. The latter phrase is
*tatsahabhuvaḥ*, co-arisen with it. It should not be narrowed to
associated mental factors alone. Similarly, counting citta under two
domain headings does not posit two independent cittas in the event.

## 7. Acquisition Is Not the Same Question as Possession

The acquisition question begins with *asamanvāgataḥ*, not possessing,
and asks about *samanvāgaṃ pratilabhate*, obtaining possession. The
four cases compare changes in possession:

| Newly acquired | Examples given |
| --- | --- |
| Eye-domain alone | Gradual acquisition in the desire realm; rebirth from the formless realm in second or higher dhyānas |
| Visual-cognition domain alone | Manifesting visual cognition when born in second or higher dhyānas; rebirth from those levels below |
| Both | Rebirth from the formless realm in the desire realm or Brahmā world |
| Neither | Cases outside those specified |

“Visual-cognition domain alone” means no new acquisition of the eye
in those cases. It does not mean that visual cognition is produced
without an eye-faculty. The examples already differentiate what is
possessed from what is newly acquired.

The separate possession question then gives:

| Possessed | Examples given |
| --- | --- |
| Eye-domain alone | Birth in second or higher dhyānas without manifest visual cognition |
| Visual-cognition domain alone | Desire-realm existence without an acquired eye or after its loss |
| Both | Desire-realm existence with an acquired, unlost eye; first-dhyāna birth; seeing when born in second or higher dhyānas |
| Neither | Cases outside those specified |

The second row must remain a possession statement. It does not report
present seeing without eyes. Conversely, the desire-realm case in the
third row is not restricted by a requirement of currently seeing.
The source expressly adds *paśyan*, seeing, to the higher-dhyāna case.

Thus *pratilambha*, acquisition, *samanvāgama*, possession, and
*saṃmukhīkaraṇa*, bringing into presence, cannot be translated as one
undifferentiated occurrence. Manifestation enters certain examples
without becoming the definition of possession everywhere.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

- “The others” means visible form, taste, smell, and tangible, each
  admitting the three previously defined causal classes.
- The dharma-domain alone contains *dravya* in the locally specified
  sense because it includes the unconditioned.
- The last three are called momentary through a one-moment exception
  to homogeneous continuation at the first outflow-free complex.
- The associated citta and its co-arisen dharmas supply the three-domain
  distribution without multiplying cittas or restricting all companions
  to associated factors.
- Separate acquisition does not imply absence of the other domain.
- The possession analysis must be retained separately, with its actual
  realm conditions and references to manifestation.

These decisions refine this study while preserving the original
kārikā and research commentary.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The first outflow-free moment bears directly on the project's question
of how a new determination of understanding can arise. The source
recognizes a conditioned beginning without an earlier homogeneous
outflow-free predecessor. That gives a precise problem for further
study: which conditions make the beginning possible when reproduction
of an earlier instance does not explain it?

It does not follow that ordinary learning and entry into the path are
the same process. Their comparison is an Organon question, requiring
attention to the specific role of *dharmajñānakṣānti* here. The text
also does not identify this causal exception with paramārtha simply
because it is outflow-free.

The acquisition and possession analyses provide another indispensable
distinction. Coming to possess a capacity, possessing a cognition-domain,
and manifesting a cognition are different predicates. An account of
prajñā's manifestation within saṃvṛti should be equally precise about
what is acquired, what is retained, and what is actually occurring.
The fourfold cases show why these questions deserve separate answers.
