# VAK_1.27 Bhāṣya — Assigning Scriptural Categories by Characteristic and Scope

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

> tathānye 'pi yathāyogaṃ skandhāyatanadhātavaḥ /
>
> pratipādyā yathokteṣu saṃpradhārya svalakṣaṇam // 1.27 //

> Likewise, other skandhas, āyatanas, and dhātus are to be assigned, as
> appropriate, among those already stated, after carefully considering
> their own-characteristic.

The Bhāṣya extends the Dharma-collection example into a general rule and
applies it to several scriptural classifications. Its examples distinguish
principal nature from a complex that includes attendants.

## 3. Continuous Conventional Translation

> Just as these eighty thousand Dharma-collections have been assigned within
> these very five aggregates, so other skandhas, āyatanas, and dhātus are
> to be assigned, as appropriate, among those already stated, after carefully
> considering their own-characteristic. Whatever other skandhas, āyatanas,
> and dhātus are stated in other sūtras are to be assigned within these
> very categories, after carefully considering each one's characteristic
> as established in this treatise.
>
> First, among the five aggregates of discipline, samādhi, prajñā,
> liberation, and knowledge-and-vision [of liberation], the discipline
> aggregate is included in the rūpa aggregate; the remainder in the
> saṃskāra aggregate. Of the ten totality-bases, eight are included in
> dharmāyatana because their nature is non-greed. With their attendants,
> however, they are included in the mind- and dharma-bases because they
> have the nature of five aggregates. The same applies to the bases of
> mastery.
>
> The totalities of infinite space and infinite cognition, and the four
> attainments beginning with the base of infinite space, are included in
> the mind- and dharma-bases because they have the nature of four aggregates.
> The five bases of liberation are included in dharmāyatana because their
> nature is prajñā. With their attendants, however, they are included in
> the sound-, mind-, and dharma-bases.
>
> Of the two further bases, non-percipient beings are included through ten
> āyatanas because the odor- and taste-bases are absent. Those who have
> reached the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception are included
> through the mind- and dharma-bases. Likewise, sixty-two dhātus are taught
> in the Bahudhātuka discourse. Their inclusion is to be understood as
> appropriate in each case.

## 4. Movement of the Commentary

The verse's general rule is followed by applications rather than by a new
argument against an opponent. Each application determines the intended
content and then states its inclusion:

```text
other scriptural designation
    → consider its characteristic as established in the treatise
    → distinguish principal factor from factor with attendants
    → assign it to the appropriate existing categories
```

No separate school is named. The final Bahudhātuka reference states a count
and directs appropriate inclusion; it does not enumerate all sixty-two
within this unit. The following study begins a specific inquiry arising
from that broader scriptural field.

## 5. Characteristic before Assignment

`Saṃpradhārya`, an absolutive, makes careful consideration prior to
`pratipādyāḥ`, the required assignment. The prose expands `svalakṣaṇam`
through `svaṃ svaṃ lakṣaṇam`, each one's characteristic, and qualifies
it by `yathāvihitam asmiñ śāstre`, as established in this treatise.

`Yathāyogam` therefore does not permit classification by resemblance of
names alone. The relevant characteristic and scope constrain the assignment.
The opening comparison takes the already classified Dharma-collections as
its model, without making every new scriptural use of skandha a teaching-
collection of the same sort.

The five practice aggregates illustrate the method. Discipline is included
under rūpa; the remaining four under saṃskāra. Their names retain their
scriptural use, but they do not add five new fundamental aggregates to the
established list. The compact wording leaves the exact expansion of the
final compound marked in the reading text.

## 6. Principal Nature and the Accompanying Complex

The recurring `saparivāra`, with attendants, changes the scope of what is
classified. The preceding description of a principal nature is not discarded;
the classification is expanded to cover the accompanying complex.

| Case | Principal account | With attendants |
|---|---|---|
| eight totality-bases | non-greed; dharmāyatana | five-aggregate nature; mind- and dharma-bases |
| bases of mastery | same treatment by explicit analogy | same treatment by explicit analogy |
| five liberation-bases | prajñā; dharmāyatana | sound-, mind-, and dharma-bases |

The five-aggregate complex of the first row does not require all ten
material āyatanas as constituents. Its rūpa component can be included
through dharmāyatana, as the earlier avijñapti classification makes possible.
The local sentence gives the resulting inclusion but does not enumerate
each attendant or explicitly name that material component. This is an
explanation of compatibility with the preceding scheme, not an added
sentence in the translation.

Similarly, the sound-base in the liberation-base account is explicitly
present in the transmitted classification. The unit does not list the
five practices or explain which attendant supplies sound in each case.
The edition preserves the expanded classification without manufacturing
those missing details.

## 7. Four-Aggregate Attainments and the Two Further Bases

The remaining two totalities concern infinite space and infinite vijñāna.
They are grouped with the four attainments beginning with infinite space
and assigned to mind and dharma because their nature is four aggregates.
The omitted aggregate is rūpa within this formless classification. “Infinite
cognition” retains the controlled rendering of vijñāna; the title of this
attainment does not define every cognition as infinite.

The two further bases are explained through their occupants or those who
have reached them. For non-percipient beings, the stated absence of odor
and taste yields ten āyatanas; for those reaching neither-perception-nor-
non-perception, the assignment is mind and dharma.

The former statement must not be turned into a claim of presently occurring
cognition during non-perception merely because the tenfold count includes
the mind-base. The sentence states classification and explicitly explains
two exclusions; it does not explain the mode in which every remaining
base is present or acquired. The distinction between classification and
current operation remains necessary for reviewing this compact account.

The latter likewise names an attainment and its occupants under the mental
bases; it does not make the attainment an extra āyatana outside the twelve.
The final sixty-two-domain statement generalizes the method without supplying
a complete table for that discourse.

## 8. The Bhāṣya's Decisions for the Kārikā

1. The additional categories are scriptural designations to be assigned
   within the established arrangements, not unspecified new dharmas.
2. Careful consideration of each characteristic precedes assignment.
3. Principal factor and accompanying complex can yield different valid
   inclusions because their scope differs.
4. Non-greed and prajñā supply explicit principal natures in the examples;
   the expanded complexes receive correspondingly broader assignments.
5. Four-aggregate constitution and the absence of odor and taste provide
   other stated grounds of inclusion.
6. The sixty-two-domain reference directs further application without
   claiming to have enumerated every mapping in this unit.

## 9. Philosophical and Organon Study

The passage makes domain assignment depend on what a name actually denotes
in context. A principal factor and that factor with attendants do not have
the same extension, even when the scriptural heading remains unchanged.
The arrangement must preserve that difference before giving its count.

For the Organon reconstruction, this is a concrete method of semantic
determination. First establish the content and the scope; then establish
its systematic placement. The broader complex neither erases the principal
factor's nature nor borrows that nature for every attendant.

This bears directly on our inquiry into prajñā and its manifestation.
The five liberation-bases are classified principally through prajñā, while
their accompanying complex includes sound, mind, and dharma bases. The
passage thus distinguishes a knowing operation from the wider complex in
which the named practice is considered. It does not itself label that
relation as manifestation of paramārtha within saṃvṛti, but it supplies
an articulated case through which our question can be investigated.

For learning, the further task is to understand how that complex supports
the relevant operation. The present text fixes its classification; the
operation's formation and achievement require their own analysis.
