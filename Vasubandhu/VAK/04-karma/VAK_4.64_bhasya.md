# VAK_4.64_bhasya — The Three Silences and Threefold Purity

## Kārikā Anchor

**IAST**

> *aśaikṣaṃ kāyavākkarma manaś caiva yathākramam /*  
> *maunatrayaṃ tridhā śaucaṃ sarvaṃ sucaritatrayam // 4.64 //*

Best working translation:

> The bodily action, verbal action, and mind of one beyond training are, respectively, the three silences. Threefold purity is the complete threefold good conduct.

See: `VAK_4.64.md`.

## Bhāṣya Source

The Bhāṣya first explains the three silences:

> *aśaikṣe kāyavākkarmaṇī kāyavāṅmauneye /*

> *aśaikṣaṃ tu mana eva manomauneyaṃ na manaskarmeti /*

Thus bodily and verbal karma in the one beyond training are bodily and verbal silence, but mental silence is **mind itself**, not mental karma.

Vasubandhu immediately asks why:

> *kiṃ kāraṇam /*  
> *cittaṃ hi paramārthamuniḥ /*

and adds:

> *tat kila kāyavākkarmabhyāmanumīyata iti /*

The mind is the *paramārtha-muni*, the sage in the highest sense, and it is inferred from bodily and verbal action.

A second argument follows:

> *api khalu kāyavākkarmaṇī viratisvabhāvena na manaskarma /*

> *cittā vijñaptyabhāvāt /*

> *viramārthena ca maunam /*

> *ato mana eva virataṃ maunamityucyate /*

Bodily and verbal karma can have abstention (*virati*) as their nature, but mental karma cannot in the same way, because there is no mental *vijñapti*. Since *mauna* is understood in the sense of abstention, the mind itself when restrained is called silence.

The Bhāṣya then asks why this is restricted to the one beyond training:

> *kasmādaśaikṣameva nānyat /*

The answer:

> *arhataḥ paramārthamunitvāt /*  
> *sarvakleśajalpoparateḥ /*

The arhat is the sage in the highest sense because all the discursive activity or chatter (*jalpa*) of the afflictions has ceased.

The second half of the verse is then explained from a sūtra teaching of three purities:

> *trīṇi śauceyāni uktāni sūtre /*  
> *kāyaśauceyaṃ vākśauceyaṃ manaḥśauceyaṃ ca /*

and identified as:

> *tridhā śaucaṃ sarvaṃ sucaritatrayam /*

The Bhāṣya glosses:

> *sarvaṃ kāyasucaritaṃ kāyaśaucam /*  
> *sarvaṃ vāṅmanaḥ sucaritaṃ vāṅmanaḥ śaucam /*

All bodily good conduct is bodily purity; all verbal and mental good conduct are verbal and mental purity.

The ground is:

> *duścaritamalāpakarṣaṇāttāvatkālamatyantaṃ vā /*

They are called purity because they remove the stain of bad conduct, either for a time or completely.

Finally, Vasubandhu states the pedagogical purpose of the teaching:

> *eṣā ca deśanā mithyāmaunaśaucādhimuktikānāṃ vivecanārtham /*

This teaching is for the discrimination of those attached to false conceptions of silence and purity.

## Source Cautions

The e-text is based on the Pradhan and Shastri editions and is not being treated as a critical edition. The forms `mauneya` and `śauceya` are preserved here provisionally rather than silently normalized.

The source clearly distinguishes two claims that should not be collapsed:

```text
mauna
    → specifically aśaikṣa perfection

śauca
    → the whole field of good bodily, verbal, and mental conduct
```

The Bhāṣya therefore gives *mauna* a stricter range than *śauca*.

## Natural Argument

The argument unfolds in four steps.

### 1. The three silences are not structurally symmetrical

The Kārikā presents:

```text
bodily karma
verbal karma
mind
    → three silences
```

The Bhāṣya insists upon the asymmetry.

```text
aśaikṣa bodily karma
    → bodily silence

aśaikṣa verbal karma
    → verbal silence

aśaikṣa mind itself
    → mental silence
```

Mental silence is **not** simply `mano-karman`.

This confirms the importance of the Kārikā's choice of `manas` rather than a symmetric compound such as `manaskarma`.

### 2. Mind is primary; body and speech are signs

The decisive sentence is:

```text
cittaṃ hi paramārthamuniḥ
```

A careful working translation is:

> For mind is the sage in the highest sense.

The next sentence supplies the relation:

```text
tat kila kāyavākkarmabhyām anumīyate
```

> It is inferred from bodily and verbal actions.

The direction of determination is therefore:

```text
mind
    ↓
body / speech
```

while the epistemic direction available to another observer is:

```text
body / speech
    ↓ inference
mind
```

The outward acts are manifestations from which the inward condition is known; they are not what makes the mind a sage.

### 3. Mauna means abstention, but the mental case cannot be another avijñapti

The Bhāṣya gives a second, technical reason.

Bodily and verbal karma may be `virati-svabhāva`, abstention by nature. Mental karma is not treated that way because:

```text
cittā vijñaptyabhāvāt
```

There is no mental *vijñapti* corresponding to bodily and verbal manifestation.

Since `mauna` is taken in the sense of `virama`, abstention or refraining, the inward member must be the **restrained mind itself**.

This prevents an artificial threefold replication of the bodily/verbal avijñapti model inside mind.

### 4. The arhat alone is the paramārtha-muni

Why only `aśaikṣa`?

Because the arhat has reached the condition in which:

```text
sarva-kleśa-jalpa-uparati
```

all afflictive chatter has ceased.

Thus the Bhāṣya does not define true silence acoustically.

```text
mauna
    ≠ absence of sound
    ≠ mere outward non-action

mauna
    = perfected restraint grounded in the cessation of kleśa-jalpa
```

This is why the category belongs properly to the one beyond training.

## Threefold Purity

The Bhāṣya next distinguishes *śauca* from *mauna*.

The three purities are:

```text
kāya-śauca
vāk-śauca
manaḥ-śauca
```

and these correspond to the complete threefold good conduct:

```text
kāya-sucarita
vāk-sucarita
manaḥ-sucarita
```

The explanatory principle is removal of stain:

```text
duścarita
    = mala

sucarita
    → apakarṣaṇa of that mala
    → śauca
```

The Bhāṣya adds an important qualification:

```text
tāvat-kālam
    → for a time

atyantam
    → completely / ultimately
```

Thus purity can name either temporary removal of bad conduct or its complete removal. This gives `śauca` a wider extension than the strictly aśaikṣa `mauna`.

## Pedagogical Function

The final sentence tells us why this terminology is being taught:

```text
mithyā-mauna-śauca-adhimukti
    ↓
vivecana
```

The point is discrimination against false constructions of silence and purity.

This is not decorative ethical vocabulary. Vasubandhu is controlling the extension of two terms that can easily be misunderstood through external religious marks:

```text
false silence
    → merely not speaking / merely external restraint

true silence
    → perfected aśaikṣa restraint grounded in freedom from kleśa

false purity
    → merely external cleanliness or ritual purity

true purity
    → good conduct that removes the stain of bad conduct
```

The Bhāṣya therefore converts apparently ascetic vocabulary into precise Abhidharma determinations.

## Voice

**Voice:** Vasubandhu / explanatory Bhāṣya voice.

No competing school position is introduced in the main explanation of 4.64. The phrase `kila` marks reported doctrinal explanation, but the passage functions as the accepted exposition here rather than as a sustained objection.

## New Technical Terms

- **mauneya** — that which constitutes or belongs to silence; applied to bodily, verbal, and mental silence in the explanatory prose.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-specific form in this passage.
  - **function:** explanatory derivation of the Kārikā's `maunatraya`.

- **paramārtha-muni** — sage in the highest or strict sense.
  - **status:** introduced in the Bhāṣya explanation.
  - **function:** grounds the restriction of genuine `mauna` to the aśaikṣa/arhat and identifies mind as the inward bearer of sagehood.

- **virati-svabhāva** — having abstention/refraining as its nature.
  - **status:** doctrinal explanatory vocabulary.
  - **function:** distinguishes bodily and verbal karmic restraint from the mental member.

- **virama** — abstention, refraining, stopping.
  - **status:** explanatory vocabulary.
  - **function:** gives the operative sense of `mauna`.

- **kleśa-jalpa** — afflictive chatter, discursive agitation, or verbalized activity of the afflictions.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya expansion.
  - **function:** explains why the arhat alone is the sage in the highest sense.

- **śauceya** — purity; the threefold purity of body, speech, and mind.
  - **status:** sūtra-derived term cited by the Bhāṣya.
  - **function:** connects the Kārikā's `śauca` with threefold good conduct.

- **duścarita-mala** — the stain of bad conduct.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya expansion.
  - **function:** explains purity genetically as removal of impurity.

- **vivecana** — discrimination, distinguishing.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya pedagogical vocabulary.
  - **function:** states the purpose of the teaching: distinguishing true from false silence and purity.

## Canonical Translation Decision

The Bhāṣya strongly confirms the first-pass translation of `aśaikṣa` as **beyond training** rather than “untrained.”

It also corrects any temptation to translate `mauna` merely as “muteness” or “silence” in the acoustic sense. The preferred interpretive gloss is:

```text
mauna
    = perfected restraint / true silence
```

For the verse itself, however, **silence** remains suitable if the Bhāṣya's technical meaning is immediately explained.

The Bhāṣya also confirms that the third member must remain **mind**, not “mental action”:

```text
manaḥ
    ≠ manaskarma
```

This is now a textually explicit distinction, not merely an inference from the Kārikā grammar.

## Logical Determination

The verse and Bhāṣya establish a relation between inward determination and outward manifestation.

```text
restrained / perfected mind
    ↓
perfected bodily action
perfected verbal action
```

but knowledge of the inward state by another proceeds reflectively:

```text
bodily action + verbal action
    ↓ anumāna
mind
```

This is a clean distinction between ontological priority and epistemic access.

The second half gives purity a genetic structure:

```text
duścarita-mala
    ↓ removal
sucarita
    ↓
śauca
```

Purity is therefore not an immediate static property. It is determined through the removal of its contrary.

## Organon Contact Point

The strongest Organon contact point is not “silence = consciousness” or any static identity. The text itself gives a mediated relation:

```text
inward perfected determination
    → outward bodily/verbal manifestation

outward manifestation
    → inferential return to inward determination
```

That reciprocity is philosophically important because the inner is neither reducible to the outer nor sealed away from it. The body and speech express the inward state, while the inward state is known through those expressions.

A second contact point is the determination of purity through negation of impurity:

```text
mala
    → apakarṣaṇa
    → śauca
```

The positive determination is generated through the removal of its contrary rather than merely asserted as an abstract predicate.

These are legitimate structural contacts generated by the Bhāṣya itself; they should not yet be expanded into a larger Organon synthesis.

## Bhāṣya Pass Result

VAK 4.64 opens the new karmapatha block by first fixing the positive normative field.

The Bhāṣya sharpens the Kārikā in two decisive ways:

1. **Mental silence is mind itself, not mental karma.**
2. **True silence belongs strictly to the arhat because all afflictive chatter has ceased.**

It then defines threefold purity through the complete field of bodily, verbal, and mental good conduct, understood genetically as removal of the stain of bad conduct.

The closing pedagogical statement is crucial: the whole teaching exists to discriminate genuine silence and purity from false constructions of them.

This makes 4.64 an excellent threshold verse. Before the chapter analyzes the ten wholesome and unwholesome karmapathas in detail, Vasubandhu first establishes what perfected restraint and purity actually mean.
