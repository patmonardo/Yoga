# VAK_4.27_bhasya — Duration of Restraint and Non-Restraint

## Kārikā Anchor

**VAK 4.27**

```iast
yāvajjīvaṃ samādānam ahorātraṃ ca saṃvṛteḥ |
nāsaṃvaro 'sty ahorātraṃ na kilaivaṃ pragṛhyate ||
```

Working translation after the Bhāṣya pass:

> Prātimokṣa restraint is undertaken either for life or, in the case of upavāsa restraint, for one day and night. Non-restraint is not ordinarily acquired as a one-day-and-night determination, because it is not in fact undertaken in that manner.

## Source Cautions

The e-text contains several evident or possible irregularities. At [212|14] it reads `ahārotrāṇāṃ`, evidently in a context requiring `ahorātrāṇāṃ`. At [212|19-212|20] the form `śāstisma` is transmitted and is not silently normalized here. At [212|20-212|22] the e-text has `anutpartti`, evidently for `anutpatti`. At [213|08] it reads `edadṛṣṭam`; the intended segmentation or normalization is uncertain, so the source reading is preserved.

The Kārikā source has `saṃvṛteḥ`. In this passage the Bhāṣya makes clear that the subject is prātimokṣa restraint, especially the lifelong forms and the day-and-night upavāsa restraint; the form should not be confused with `saṃvṛti` in the technical sense of convention.

## Natural Argument

Vasubandhu begins from the question opened at the end of VAK 4.26: once prātimokṣa restraint is acquired, for how long is it undertaken?

The initial rule is twofold. The seven classes of prātimokṣa restraint are undertaken for life, whereas upavāsa restraint is undertaken for one complete day and night. Vasubandhu formulates this through two temporal limits (`kālaparyanta`): the limit of a day-and-night and the limit of a lifetime.

He then pauses over the meaning of time itself. `Kāla` is not introduced as an independently existing substance. It is an expression that indicates conditioned processes (`saṃskāraparidīpanādhivacana`). Day is the illuminated state on the inhabited continents; night is the state of darkness. The analysis therefore explains the practical temporal limit through states of conditioned phenomena rather than by positing time as a separate entity.

The lifelong limit is comparatively intelligible. Even if an undertaking were verbally projected beyond death, the restraint would not arise beyond that life because the support (`āśraya`) has become dissimilar, because no corresponding practical application occurs there, and because there is no recollection of the undertaking.

The day-and-night limit, however, is harder. Why could one not undertake upavāsa for five or ten nights and thereby generate a succession of upavāsa restraints? A first answer appeals to scripture: the Buddha teaches the upavāsa specifically as a one-day-and-night observance.

But Vasubandhu does not leave the matter there. He explicitly says that this must be considered (`idam idānīṃ saṃpradhāryam`). Did the Tathāgata prescribe only a one-day upavāsa because he saw that restraint literally cannot arise beyond one day and night? Or was the one-day form taught in order to connect persons of weak faculties with restraint even through a manageable one-day undertaking? The question is legitimate because there is no contradiction in reason (`yuktyavirodha`) in restraint arising beyond a single day and night.

The Vaibhāṣikas reject this opening. They do not accept an instruction of upavāsa extending beyond one day and night for anyone. The passage therefore distinguishes the received Vaibhāṣika rule from the logical question Vasubandhu has raised about whether the rule expresses an intrinsic impossibility.

The argument then turns to the temporal rule for `asaṃvara`, non-restraint. The standard account says that non-restraint arises through undertaking evil action as a standing way of life and is not acquired for merely a day and night in the manner of upavāsa. Why? Because no one actually undertakes non-restraint in the explicit form, “May I be unrestrained for this one day and night.” Evil action is blameworthy, so it is not normally formalized in the same ritual manner.

An immediate difficulty follows. People also do not normally formulate the lifelong undertaking, “May I be unrestrained for as long as I live.” If explicit temporal declaration were necessary, lifelong non-restraint would not be acquired either.

The answer shifts from verbal undertaking to the depth and temporal structure of intention (`āśaya`). A person who performs the relevant activity with an utterly corrupted intention (`atyantavipannenāśayena`) acquires non-restraint, whereas one whose corrupted intention is only temporary (`kālāntaravipanna`) does not. Upavāsa restraint differs because the force imparted by formal undertaking (`samādānabalādhāna`) can produce restraint even where the underlying intention is not absolute or lifelong, precisely because the person seeks restraint (`saṃvarārthitva`).

Vasubandhu then makes the asymmetry less absolute. If someone actually desired non-restraint and deliberately undertook it for a limited time, that person would necessarily acquire it. The reason this is not established as a recognized temporal category is empirical-doctrinal: such a case is not observed. Thus the rule “there is no one-day non-restraint” does not amount to a metaphysical impossibility corresponding exactly to the one-day upavāsa rule.

Finally the Sautrāntikas reopen the ontology of the whole discussion. Just as they deny that avijñapti exists as a separate real entity, they deny that non-restraint exists substantially (`dravyataḥ`). For them, `asaṃvara` is the evil practical intention (`pāpakriyābhisaṃdhi`) itself together with its continuing connection (`sānubandha`). Because that intention has not been repudiated, the person is still said to possess non-restraint even when a wholesome mind presently occurs.

The next question — how a one-day upavāsa is actually to be undertaken — opens VAK 4.28.

## Voice Identification

**Voice: Vasubandhu** — asks how long prātimokṣa restraint is undertaken and states the received two-limit rule.

**Voice: Vasubandhu / received Abhidharma analysis** — explains the two temporal limits and analyzes `kāla` as an expression indicating conditioned processes.

**Voice: Objector / Vasubandhu's dialectical question** — asks why upavāsa could not extend for five or ten nights and whether the one-day prescription expresses impossibility or pedagogical accommodation. The exact dramatic attribution is not explicitly marked, but Vasubandhu clearly preserves the question as a genuine logical problem.

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika position** — rejects instruction of upavāsa beyond one day and night.

**Voice: Vasubandhu / received account** — explains why one-day non-restraint is not ordinarily established, then qualifies the explanation by appealing to the depth of intention and by conceding that a deliberately time-bounded undertaking of non-restraint would generate it if such a case occurred.

**Voice: Sautrāntika position** — denies substantial non-restraint and identifies it with an evil practical intention together with its continuing connection.

## New Technical Terms

- **kālaparyanta** — temporal limit or terminus; **status:** new in this immediate sequence; **function:** distinguishes the two recognized durations of prātimokṣa undertaking.
- **saṃskāraparidīpanādhivacana** — an expression indicating conditioned processes; **status:** new; **function:** explains what the word `kāla` designates without treating time as a separate substance.
- **durbalendriya** — one of weak faculties; **status:** previously familiar doctrinal type, newly operative here; **function:** supports the possible pedagogical explanation of the one-day upavāsa prescription.
- **yuktyavirodha** — non-contradiction with reasoning; **status:** important reflective vocabulary; **function:** marks Vasubandhu's reason for questioning whether a longer temporary restraint is intrinsically impossible.
- **āśaya** — underlying intention / practical disposition; **status:** important but newly decisive here; **function:** explains acquisition of non-restraint independently of an explicit verbal formula.
- **atyantavipannāśaya** — utterly corrupted intention; **status:** new compound in this discussion; **function:** grounds acquisition of non-restraint as an enduring practical orientation.
- **kālāntaravipannāśaya** — intention corrupted only for a limited interval; **status:** new; **function:** distinguishes temporary evil activity from standing non-restraint.
- **samādānabalādhāna** — imparting force through formal undertaking; **status:** new; **function:** explains how upavāsa restraint can be acquired even without an absolutely settled underlying disposition.
- **saṃvarārthitva** — seeking or intending restraint; **status:** new; **function:** explains the efficacy of the upavāsa undertaking.
- **pāpakriyābhisaṃdhi** — evil practical intention; **status:** new Sautrāntika formulation; **function:** replaces substantial `asaṃvara` in the Sautrāntika account.
- **sānubandha** — accompanied by a continuing connection / continuation; **status:** related to earlier `anubandha`; **function:** explains persistence of non-restraint across presently wholesome mental states in the Sautrāntika account.

## Doctrinal Determination

### Recognized durations of prātimokṣa restraint

```text
seven lifelong prātimokṣa classes
    → yāvajjīvam

upavāsa restraint
    → ahorātram
```

The Bhāṣya calls these two `kālaparyanta`, temporal termini.

### Time is indicated through conditioned states

```text
kāla
    ≠ independently posited substance

kāla
    = expression indicating saṃskāras
```

Day and night are explained through illuminated and dark states of the inhabited world.

### Why lifelong restraint ends with life

```text
death
    → support becomes dissimilar
    → no corresponding application
    → no recollection of the undertaking
    → restraint does not arise beyond that life
```

### The one-day upavāsa rule is questioned

```text
scriptural prescription
    one day + one night

but:
reason contains no contradiction
in restraint arising beyond that duration
```

Hence two possible explanations are distinguished:

```text
one-day rule
    because longer upavāsa is impossible

or

one-day rule
    because it enables persons of weak faculties
    to undertake restraint
```

The Vaibhāṣikas reject extending the prescribed observance beyond one day and night.

### Non-restraint and temporal intention

The simple statement:

```text
asaṃvara
    not acquired for one day and night
```

is refined into:

```text
utterly corrupted practical intention
    → standing asaṃvara

temporarily corrupted intention
    → harmful action may occur
    → no standing asaṃvara
```

This is a significant sharpening of the Kārikā's `na kilaivaṃ pragṛhyate`.

### Upavāsa differs through the force of undertaking

```text
seeking restraint
    + formal undertaking
    + force imparted by samādāna
        → upavāsa restraint
```

The underlying disposition need not already be absolute or lifelong.

### Limited non-restraint is not intrinsically impossible

```text
if someone genuinely sought asaṃvara
and undertook it for a limited period
    → asaṃvara would be acquired
```

But this is not established as a standard category because such a case is not observed.

### Sautrāntika reconstruction

```text
asaṃvara
    not a separate dravya

asaṃvara
    = pāpakriyābhisaṃdhi
      + continuing anubandha
```

Thus a person can presently have a wholesome mind and still be described as possessing non-restraint so long as the evil practical intention has not been repudiated.

## Effect on Kārikā Translation

The Kārikā pass correctly distinguished lifelong restraint, one-day upavāsa, and the absence of a recognized one-day non-restraint. The Bhāṣya, however, makes the last claim less absolute than the earlier philosophical translation suggested.

The earlier rendering:

> Non-restraint, however, is not recognized as a temporary one-day institution, because it is not formally taken up under such a limited determination.

is broadly correct, but the Bhāṣya adds two qualifications:

1. acquisition of standing non-restraint is grounded in the depth and persistence of `āśaya`, not simply in explicit verbal undertaking;
2. deliberately undertaken temporary non-restraint is not declared intrinsically impossible — Vasubandhu says that if someone genuinely undertook it, it would be acquired, but such a case is not observed.

A better working translation is therefore:

> Prātimokṣa restraint is undertaken either for life or, in the case of upavāsa restraint, for one day and night. Non-restraint is not ordinarily acquired as a one-day-and-night determination, because it is not in fact undertaken in that manner.

The adverb “ordinarily” is interpretive but captures the Bhāṣya's explicit qualification at [213|07-213|08].

## Logical Determination

The Bhāṣya transforms a simple temporal classification into a genetic inquiry.

The decisive distinctions are:

```text
duration as prescribed form
    ≠
duration as intrinsic possibility
```

and:

```text
explicit undertaking
    ≠
underlying practical disposition
```

For upavāsa:

```text
samādāna
    actively imparts force
        ↓
finite restraint-form
```

For non-restraint:

```text
persistent corrupted āśaya
        ↓
standing asaṃvara
```

Temporary evil intention does not automatically generate the standing determination.

The asymmetry between restraint and non-restraint is therefore genetic rather than merely classificatory. The two are not mirror images generated by reversing a moral predicate.

The Sautrāntika intervention radicalizes this point by relocating persistence from a separate non-manifest entity to the continuing structure of intention itself.

## Organon Contact Point

The safe contact point is the distinction between an occurrence and a practical determination that persists through time.

```text
temporary harmful occurrence
    ≠
standing non-restraint
```

What matters is how the practical form is generated and maintained:

```text
restraint
    may be generated by the force of explicit undertaking

non-restraint
    may be generated by a deeply settled corrupted intention
```

This is useful for the Rational Kośa because practical determination is shown to have an internal genesis: temporal form is not merely attached externally to an already complete act.

The Sautrāntika view is especially important but should remain textually separate from our own reconstruction. It offers a model in which persistence is carried by an intentional continuum rather than by a distinct avijñapti-dravya.
