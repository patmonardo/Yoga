## Natural Segmentation

The Bhāṣya has two movements:

1. It explains the fourfold classification of giving according to who receives benefit: self, other, both, or neither.
2. It begins the analysis of why one act of giving is more excellent than another, first from the distinction of the giver, before 4.115 continues with the giver’s manner of giving.

## Literal Prose Translation

Giving may be for one’s own benefit, for another’s benefit, for the benefit of both, or for the benefit of neither.

When a noble who has not yet become dispassionate, or an ordinary person even if dispassionate, gives to a stūpa, that giving is for his own benefit alone, because no benefit is thereby conferred upon others.

When a dispassionate noble gives to other sentient beings—apart from a result to be experienced in this very life—that giving is for the benefit of others. It benefits them, but not himself, because he has completely passed beyond the domain in which its maturation-result would occur.

When an ordinary person, or a noble who has not yet become dispassionate, gives to other sentient beings, that giving is for the benefit of both.

When a dispassionate noble gives to a stūpa—again apart from a result to be experienced in this very life—that giving is for the benefit of neither. For it is given solely from reverence and gratitude.

Giving has already been said in general to have great enjoyment as its fruit. Its particular excellence, however, depends upon the distinction of the giver, the gift-object, and the recipient-field.

First, the giver is distinguished by faith and related qualities. A giver endowed with faith, discipline, learning, and other qualities is an excellent giver. Because of the excellence of the giver, the fruit yielded by that giving is correspondingly distinguished.

## Argument Reconstruction

### 1. Benefit and merit are not identical predicates

The Bhāṣya’s fourfold scheme distinguishes two questions that the first-pass Kārikā analysis tended to merge:

- Who is materially or causally benefited by the act now?
- Does the donor still stand within a domain in which that karma can mature for him later?

The answer depends both on the recipient and on the donor’s spiritual status.

### 2. Self-benefit only

A stūpa cannot literally enjoy or use the gift. Thus when an ordinary person or a non-dispassionate noble makes an offering to a stūpa, the giving generates merit for the donor but provides no corresponding material benefit to a living recipient.

This is the technical sense of `svārthāya` here.

### 3. Other-benefit only

A dispassionate noble who gives to living beings materially benefits them. But, except for a result experienced in this very life, he has transcended the rebirth-domain in which the gift-karma would mature for himself.

Hence:

`recipient benefit remains`  
`future donor maturation no longer remains`

The act is therefore `parārthāya`.

### 4. Benefit to both

Where a donor still capable of future karmic maturation gives to living beings, both sides obtain benefit:

- the recipient receives actual assistance;
- the donor receives the karmic fruit of giving.

### 5. Benefit to neither

This is the most important correction to the first-pass reading. `Nobhayārthāya` is not a deficient, failed, harmful, or ethically compromised gift.

When a dispassionate noble gives to a stūpa:

- the stūpa does not materially benefit;
- the noble does not acquire future maturation in a rebirth-domain already transcended.

Yet the act remains wholesome because it is performed solely from:

- `gaurava` — reverence;
- `kṛtajñatā` — gratitude / recognition of benefit received.

Thus an act may be ethically perfect even where neither party receives the ordinary benefit measured by future enjoyment or material assistance.

## Fourfold Matrix

| Donor | Recipient | Donor's future maturation | Recipient benefit | Classification |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Ordinary person or non-dispassionate noble | Stūpa | Yes | No | self only |
| Dispassionate noble | Living beings | No, except present-life result | Yes | other only |
| Ordinary person or non-dispassionate noble | Living beings | Yes | Yes | both |
| Dispassionate noble | Stūpa | No, except present-life result | No | neither |

The classification therefore combines:

`donor-status + recipient-type + possibility of maturation + actual benefit`

## The Three Determinants of Giving's Excellence

After classifying benefit-scope, the Kārikā identifies three further variables:

1. `dātṛ-viśeṣa` — distinction of the giver;
2. `vastu-viśeṣa` — distinction of the gift-object;
3. `kṣetra-viśeṣa` — distinction of the recipient-field.

The present Bhāṣya begins only the first.

A distinguished giver is one endowed with qualities such as:

- `śraddhā` — faith/confidence;
- `śīla` — discipline;
- `śruta` — learning;
- and related excellences.

The fruit of the giving is intensified because the giver himself is ethically and cognitively distinguished.

VAK 4.115 will continue this analysis by examining the manner in which such a giver gives.

## Voice Identification

**Voice: Vasubandhu** — explanatory classification of the four benefit-scopes and the beginning of the threefold analysis of excellence.

No explicit objection or competing school position appears in this unit.

## New Technical Terms

- **gaurava** — reverence, respectful regard.  
  **Status:** previously encountered conceptually, locally important here.  
  **Function:** explains wholesome giving where neither donor nor recipient obtains the ordinary benefit classified in the verse.

- **kṛtajñatā** — gratitude; recognition of benefit received.  
  **Status:** rare / Bhāṣya-specific in this local analysis.  
  **Function:** motive for stūpa-giving by a dispassionate noble.

- **dātṛviśeṣa** — excellence or distinction of the giver.  
  **Status:** introduced explicitly here.  
  **Function:** one of three determinants of differentiated fruit from giving.

- **dṛṣṭadharmavedanīya** — to be experienced in this very life.  
  **Status:** previously encountered karmic classification.  
  **Function:** exception preserving possible present-life result even for a dispassionate noble.

## Doctrinal and Logical Determination

The verse gives a genuinely relational theory of giving, but not in the simple form proposed by the first-pass file.

The karmic event has at least four independently variable dimensions:

```text
DonorStatus
RecipientType
RecipientBenefit
DonorMaturationPossibility
```

These determine whether the act is:

```text
Self
Other
Both
Neither
```

A second layer then evaluates the magnitude or excellence of the same giving through:

```text
GiverQuality
GiftObjectQuality
RecipientFieldQuality
```

Thus `benefit-scope` and `karmic excellence` are distinct analyses.

Most importantly:

```text
no ordinary benefit to either party
    does not imply
no wholesome value
```

Reverence and gratitude can determine wholesome activity independently of material assistance and future karmic acquisition.

## Translation Consequences for the Kārikā

The Kārikā translation itself can remain:

> Giving is performed for one’s own benefit, for another’s benefit, for the benefit of both, or for the benefit of neither. Its distinction, moreover, arises from the distinction of the giver, the object, and the recipient-field.

But the first-pass explanation of `nobhayārthāya` should be corrected. It must not be glossed as failed, harmful, or deficient giving. The Bhāṣya’s paradigm is a wholesome offering made by a dispassionate noble to a stūpa, solely from reverence and gratitude.

Likewise, `svārtha`, `parārtha`, and `ubhayārtha` are not merely intentional aims. They classify actual benefit and karmic maturation relative to the donor’s spiritual status.

## Light Organon Contact

The most useful logical distinction here is between **value**, **benefit**, and **result**.

An activity can remain ethically determined even when ordinary benefit has fallen away on both sides:

```text
reverence / gratitude
    → wholesome action
    even where
material benefit = 0
future maturation = 0
```

This prevents a crude consequentialism. The practical determination of the act is not exhausted by measurable gain to donor or recipient.

The second half then adds a relational measure of intensity:

```text
GivingExcellence =
    f(Giver, Gift, Field)
```

The truth of the giving is therefore neither subjective intention alone nor objective transfer alone, but the fully determined relation among agent, middle, and field.
