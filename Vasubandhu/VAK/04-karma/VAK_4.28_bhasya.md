# VAK_4.28_bhasya — The Complete Procedure of Upavāsa Restraint

## Kārikā Anchor

**VAK 4.28**

```iast
kālyaṃ grāhyo 'nyato nīcaiḥ sthitenoktānuvāditā |
upavāsaḥ samagrāṅgo nirbhūṣeṇāniśākṣayāt ||
```

Working translation after the Bhāṣya pass:

> The upavāsa restraint is to be received at the proper morning time from another, by one positioned deferentially and repeating the words after they have been spoken. It is to be undertaken complete in all eight limbs, without exceptional adornment, and maintained until the following sunrise.

## Source Cautions

The e-text reads `nirbhuṣeṇa` in the Kārikā at [213|13-213|14], while the expected normalized form is `nirbhūṣeṇa`. The exact source reading is preserved above.

At [213|17-213|18], `otkuṭṭakena` appears in the e-text; the passage clearly concerns a lowered or crouching posture, but no silent normalization is imposed here.

The long compounds and sandhi in the closing etymological discussion are preserved as transmitted. The source itself is a corrected reference e-text rather than a critical edition.

## Natural Argument

Vasubandhu takes up the practical question left by VAK 4.27: if upavāsa restraint is acquired for one complete day and night, exactly how is that restraint to be received?

The first condition is temporal. `kālyam` means at sunrise, because the restraint is defined as belonging to a day-and-night period. Vasubandhu immediately adds an exception: one who has previously formed the standing undertaking, “I shall always observe the eight,” may receive the restraint even after eating. The rule therefore concerns the normal institution of the observance, not an absolute incapacity after the morning has passed.

The second condition is interpersonal. The restraint is to be received `anyataḥ`, from another, and not simply assumed by oneself while disregarding another. Vasubandhu gives the practical reason: this serves to prevent transgression when the relevant conditions are present. The external giver therefore belongs to the disciplinary procedure by which the undertaking is stabilized.

The third condition is bodily deference. The recipient stands lower, crouches, or kneels, forming the prescribed gesture of joined hands. Vasubandhu states the doctrinal point directly: restraint does not arise in one who lacks respect (`agaurava`). The posture is therefore not treated merely as decoration; it expresses a condition of reception.

The fourth condition is verbal sequence. The recipient repeats the giver's words, but neither before the giver nor simultaneously with the giver. The words must first be given and then repeated. Only in this way is the restraint actually received from another; otherwise the distinction between giving and receiving (`dāna-grahaṇa`) would not be established.

The fifth condition is completeness. `samagrāṅga` means that the observance must be received with all eight limbs. A deficient or selectively truncated observance (`vikalāṅga`) is not the complete upavāsa restraint.

The sixth condition concerns adornment. `nirbhūṣeṇa` does not require abandoning one's ordinary or habitual adornment (`ājasrika-alaṅkāra`). Vasubandhu explicitly exempts habitual adornment because it does not strongly generate intoxication or vanity (`mada`). The restriction concerns exceptional adornment rather than every ordinary form of presentation.

The final condition is temporal completion. `āniśākṣayāt` is explained as continuing through the exhaustion of the night until sunrise occurs again. Thus the full span is:

```text
sunrise
    ↓
day
    ↓
night
    ↓
next sunrise
```

Vasubandhu then draws a strong boundary: someone who behaves otherwise may possess merely good conduct (`sucarita-mātra`), but does not thereby possess the upavāsa restraint itself. This is an important distinction between morally good behavior and the technically constituted restraint.

He closes with two explanations of `upavāsa`: one lives near the Arhats by imitating their discipline, or, according to another explanation, one lives near lifelong restraint through this temporary observance. He also offers the name `poṣadha`: it nourishes the wholesome roots of those whose wholesome roots are small.

## Voice Identification

**Voice: Vasubandhu** — asks how the one-day-and-night upavāsa is to be received and explains each procedural condition.

**Voice: Vasubandhu** — gives the exception for someone with a previously formed standing undertaking to observe the eight limbs.

**Voice: Vasubandhu** — explains the need for reception from another, bodily respect, sequential repetition, completeness, adornment, and the temporal terminus.

**Voice: uncertain / alternative explanation** — `yāvajjīvikasaṃvarasamīpe vasantyanene tyapare`: others derive `upavāsa` as dwelling near lifelong restraint.

**Voice: Vasubandhu reporting traditional etymology** — explains `poṣadha` through nourishment of wholesome roots and cites a verse in support.

## New Technical Terms

- **agaurava** — absence of respect or reverence.
  - **status:** new operative term in this passage.
  - **function:** condition explaining why restraint fails to arise when the undertaking is received without the required respectful disposition.

- **dāna-grahaṇa** — giving and receiving.
  - **status:** ordinary terms made technically operative here.
  - **function:** explains why the verbal formula must be sequential rather than prior or simultaneous.

- **vikalāṅga** — deficient in limbs; incomplete in constituent parts.
  - **status:** new here.
  - **function:** contrasts an incomplete observance with the required `samagrāṅga` upavāsa.

- **ājasrika-alaṅkāra** — habitual / ordinary adornment.
  - **status:** new here.
  - **function:** qualifies the apparent absolute prohibition implied by `nirbhūṣeṇa`.

- **mada** — intoxication, pride, or self-exalting excitement.
  - **status:** previously encountered elsewhere but newly operative here.
  - **function:** gives the reason exceptional adornment is restricted.

- **sucarita-mātra** — mere good conduct.
  - **status:** important contrastive expression.
  - **function:** distinguishes ethically good behavior from formally acquired upavāsa restraint.

- **poṣadha** — observance understood as nourishment or strengthening.
  - **status:** new term in this local discussion.
  - **function:** alternative name for the observance, explained through nourishment of wholesome roots.

## Doctrinal Determination

### Proper temporal institution

```text
ordinary acquisition
    sunrise
        ↓
upavāsa restraint
        ↓
until next sunrise
```

But:

```text
prior standing undertaking
    “I shall always observe the eight”
        ↓
permits reception even after eating
```

The morning rule is therefore procedurally normal rather than an absolute metaphysical impossibility of later acquisition.

### Reception from another

```text
giver speaks
    ↓
recipient listens
    ↓
recipient repeats
    ↓
restraint is received from another
```

Not:

```text
recipient speaks first
recipient speaks simultaneously
```

because then:

```text
dāna / grahaṇa
    giver / receiver distinction
        fails
```

### Respect as a condition

```text
lowered posture
    + deferential gesture
    + respectful disposition
        ↓
adequate reception of restraint
```

Vasubandhu states explicitly:

```text
agaurava
    → saṃvara does not arise
```

### Integral constitution

```text
upavāsa
    = eight-limbed whole
```

Therefore:

```text
selected observances
    ≠ complete upavāsa restraint
```

### Adornment qualified

The Kārikā's `nirbhūṣeṇa` is not interpreted as requiring removal of every habitual adornment.

```text
habitual adornment
    → does not strongly produce mada
    → may remain

exceptional adornment
    → restricted
```

### Conduct versus restraint

A particularly important doctrinal distinction emerges:

```text
good conduct
    sucarita
        ≠
formally constituted upavāsa-saṃvara
```

Correct behavior alone does not automatically instantiate the technical restraint if the conditions governing its acquisition are absent.

## Effect on Kārikā Translation

The Kārikā pass captured the procedural structure well, but the Bhāṣya requires several refinements.

First, `kālyam` should be rendered more specifically as “at the proper morning time / at sunrise,” while noting Vasubandhu's explicit exception for one who already maintains the standing undertaking to observe the eight limbs.

Second, `anyataḥ` is stronger than merely “social mediation.” The restraint is technically received from another, and Vasubandhu connects this with protection against transgression.

Third, `uktānuvāditā` requires temporal sequencing: the recipient repeats the giver's speech after it has been spoken, not before or simultaneously.

Fourth, `samagrāṅga` specifically means all eight limbs.

Fifth, `nirbhūṣeṇa` must not be translated as an absolute ban on every adornment. Habitual adornment is expressly exempted.

Finally, `āniśākṣayāt` is concretely interpreted as extending until the following sunrise.

A stronger translation is therefore:

> The upavāsa restraint is to be received at sunrise from another, by one positioned deferentially and repeating the words after they have been spoken. It is to be undertaken complete in all eight limbs, without exceptional adornment, and maintained until the following sunrise.

## Logical Determination

VAK 4.28 distinguishes a practical determination from mere conformity of behavior.

```text
external behavioral conformity
    may yield
sucarita

but does not by itself yield
upavāsa-saṃvara
```

The restraint has determinate conditions of genesis:

```text
time
+ relation to another
+ respectful disposition
+ bodily enactment
+ sequential verbal reception
+ completeness of content
+ regulated presentation
+ temporal persistence
    ↓
upavāsa-saṃvara
```

The crucial point is not that ritual magically adds moral value. It is that the Kośa treats restraint as a specifically generated practical determination whose acquisition cannot be identified with isolated good acts.

The giver-receiver structure also displays a genuine mediation:

```text
spoken determination of another
    ↓
repetition by recipient
    ↓
determination possessed by recipient
```

The middle term is not mere duplication. The sequential act of repetition is what constitutes reception as reception.

## Organon Contact Point

The safest contact point is the distinction between:

```text
conduct
    and
practical form
```

Vasubandhu's account shows that a practical form is not exhausted by the extensional set of acts conforming to it. It has a genesis, conditions of acquisition, an articulated content, and a determinate duration.

The living mediation here is especially clear in the giver-receiver relation:

```text
form first articulated by another
    ↓
received through responsive repetition
    ↓
becomes the recipient's own practical determination
```

No larger metaphysical identification is required at this pass.
