# VAK_4.31_bhasya — Complete Restraint, Partial Practice, and Degrees of Strength

## Kārikā Anchor

**VAK 4.31**

```iast
sarve cet saṃvṛtā ekadeśakāryādayaḥ katham |
tatpālanāt kila proktā mṛdvāditvaṃ yathā manaḥ ||
```

Working translation after the Bhāṣya pass:

> If all upāsakas are restrained, how can some be called one-part practitioners and so forth? They are said to be such according to what they preserve; while restraint itself, like mind, may be weak, medium, or strong.

Kārikā analysis: `VAK_4.31.md`.

## Source Cautions

The e-text preserves several compressed or potentially irregular forms. At [216|02-216|03] the expected reading is contextually `upāsakasaṃvaraṃ`; the transmitted text may show minor orthographic irregularity in some witnesses/e-text forms. No silent normalization is imposed beyond the quoted source above.

The expression `pūripūrṇakārī` at [215|15-215|16] is preserved as transmitted. The doctrinal sense is the fully or completely practicing upāsaka.

The dialogue after `idam utsūtraṃ vartate` changes voices rapidly. Attribution is therefore kept cautious rather than forcing every sentence into a single speaker.

## Natural Argument

The verse begins with an objection against the Kāśmīra/Vaibhāṣika position established in the preceding discussion: if every upāsaka necessarily stands in upāsaka restraint, why does the Buddha distinguish an `ekadeśakārī`, `pradeśakārī`, `yadbhūyaḥkārī`, and `pūripūrṇakārī`—roughly, one-part, partial, predominantly practicing, and fully practicing lay followers?

The initial answer is that these designations concern **preservation of the training rules**, not unequal acquisition of restraint. A person is called a practitioner of whichever training rule he preserves. On this answer, all are equally established in restraint (`sarve tu samaṃ saṃvarasthāḥ`), although their actual guarding of the rules differs.

The Bhāṣya does not allow that answer to stand uncontested. It immediately introduces the criticism `idam utsūtraṃ vartate`—“this runs contrary to the sūtra.” The dispute then turns to the exact scriptural basis for deriving restraint directly from the undertaking of upāsaka status. A Mahānāma-sūtra reading is invoked, and the discussion distinguishes a passage describing steadfast refuge and confidence from a genuine definition of restraint.

A further objection sharpens the issue. If the classification into one-part and complete practitioners referred merely to people who had already broken some training rules, the original question would be pointless: anyone who knows the lay restraint already knows that one is called the practitioner of whichever precept one has not broken. The question is meaningful, rather, when directed toward persons who do not know the full measure (`parimāṇa`) of the upāsaka restraint and who are capable only of that much training.

The debate then reaches the conventional constitution of religious status. If an upāsaka could exist without complete restraint or with defective restraint, would the same not follow for a bhikṣu or śrāmaṇera? The reply appeals to `śāstṛprajñapti`, the Teacher's institutional designation: the number and allocation of limbs are fixed through the Teacher's prescription. The opposing side therefore argues that upāsaka-status too may be fixed by prescription, such that someone may conventionally (`prajñaptitaḥ`) be an upāsaka even without restraint, while this is not allowed for bhikṣus or śrāmaṇeras. The Kāśmīras explicitly reject this solution.

The final pāda introduces a distinct issue: even when the structural restraint is the same kind, its **strength** differs. All restraints may be weak (`mṛdu`), medium (`madhya`), or strong (`adhimātra`) according to the mind of the individual's own continuum (`sasaṃtānacittavaśāt`). Hence even an arhat may possess a weak prātimokṣa restraint, while an ordinary person may possess a strong one. Spiritual rank and the intensity of this particular restraint are therefore not simply identical scales.

The passage then transitions to VAK 4.32 by asking whether restraint alone, without going for refuge, is sufficient to make one an upāsaka.

## Voice Identification

**Voice: Objector** — raises the problem: if all upāsakas possess restraint, how can the sūtra distinguish one-part and complete practitioners?

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika position** — initial response: the names are based on which training rules are preserved; all are equally established in restraint.

**Voice: Objector / uncertain** — `idam utsūtraṃ vartate` and the ensuing sūtra-based criticism of deriving complete restraint simply from undertaking upāsaka status.

**Voice: uncertain** — the dialectical exchange concerning the Mahānāma-sūtra, the meaningfulness of the partial-practitioner question, and whether upāsaka status may exist conventionally without restraint.

**Voice: Kāśmīra position** — explicitly rejects the view that an upāsaka may be conventionally designated without restraint.

**Voice: Vasubandhu** — gives the general determination that all restraints can be weak, medium, or strong according to the mind of the individual's continuum, and then opens the refuge question of VAK 4.32.

## New Technical Terms

- **ekadeśakārī** — one-part practitioner.
  - **status:** operative here as a sūtra classification.
  - **function:** generates the objection to universal possession of complete lay restraint.

- **pradeśakārī** — partial practitioner.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya classification term.
  - **function:** marks an intermediate degree of actual training/practice.

- **yadbhūyaḥkārī** — one who practices the greater part / predominantly practices.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya classification term.
  - **function:** further differentiates degrees of practice.

- **pūripūrṇakārī** — fully practicing / complete practitioner.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya classification term.
  - **function:** upper end of the sūtra classification.

- **pālana** — guarding, preserving, maintaining a training rule.
  - **status:** made technically operative here.
  - **function:** initial Vaibhāṣika explanation of the practitioner classifications.

- **utsūtra** — contrary to / outside the sūtra.
  - **status:** explicit dialectical judgment in the Bhāṣya.
  - **function:** challenges an exegetical resolution as scripturally unsupported.

- **parimāṇa** — measure, full extent.
  - **status:** new in this local argument.
  - **function:** distinguishes ignorance of the total scope of lay restraint from deliberate breaking of already-known rules.

- **śāstṛprajñapti** — designation/prescription by the Teacher.
  - **status:** new institutional term in this sequence.
  - **function:** explains how the number of limbs and religious statuses are conventionally fixed.

- **prajñaptitaḥ** — by designation / conventionally.
  - **status:** important ontological-institutional qualifier.
  - **function:** supports the disputed claim that upāsaka status might be established without restraint.

- **mṛdu / madhya / adhimātra** — weak / medium / strong.
  - **status:** intensity terms encountered elsewhere, here applied specifically to restraint.
  - **function:** distinguishes the strength of restraint from its structural type and from spiritual rank.

## Doctrinal Determination

The first distinction is between **possession** and **preservation**:

```text
upāsakasaṃvara-lābha
    possession / acquisition of lay restraint

śikṣāpada-pālana
    actual guarding of particular training rules
```

The initial Vaibhāṣika resolution states:

```text
all upāsakas
    → equally established in lay restraint

but

actual guarding differs
    → ekadeśakārī
    → pradeśakārī
    → yadbhūyaḥkārī
    → pūripūrṇakārī
```

But the Bhāṣya immediately records a sūtra-based objection to this neat separation. The question is not closed simply by asserting:

```text
complete restraint acquired
    + partial observance afterward
```

because the scriptural classification may concern persons whose relation to the full measure of restraint is itself at issue.

A second doctrinal dispute concerns religious status:

```text
Kāśmīra position:
    upāsaka
        → necessarily possesses upāsaka restraint

opposing proposal:
    upāsaka may exist prajñaptitaḥ
        even without restraint
    because status boundaries are fixed by śāstṛprajñapti
```

The Kāśmīras reject the latter proposal.

A third and independent determination concerns intensity:

```text
saṃvara
    weak | medium | strong

strength determined by:
    sasaṃtānacitta
    the mind of one's own continuum
```

Therefore:

```text
spiritual rank
    ≠
strength of prātimokṣa restraint
```

An arhat's prātimokṣa restraint can be weak, while an ordinary person's can be strong.

## Effect on Kārikā Translation

The Kārikā pass was too smooth in saying simply that all lay followers acquire the whole restraint and differ only in actual realization. The Bhāṣya presents that as an **initial doctrinal answer that is itself challenged**.

A better translation is:

> If all are restrained, how can there be one-part practitioners and the rest? They are said to be such according to what they preserve, it is claimed; while restraint itself, like mind, may be weak, medium, or strong.

The reportive `kila` matters. It marks `tatpālanāt ... proktāḥ` as a reported doctrinal explanation rather than a completely unqualified final resolution.

The second half should also not be read as if “partial” and “complete” were themselves the weak/medium/strong grades. The Bhāṣya separates these two classifications:

```text
one-part / partial / predominantly / complete
    → classification by practice or preservation

weak / medium / strong
    → intensity of restraint itself
```

## Logical Determination

VAK 4.31 distinguishes several relations that the first pass tended to compress:

```text
status
    ≠
restraint
    ≠
preservation of particular rules
    ≠
strength of restraint
```

The interesting dialectical pressure is that a universal practical form cannot be understood merely by asserting its formal possession and then treating every empirical difference as imperfect execution. The sūtra classifications force the system to account for how the **measure of the form itself is known, undertaken, and institutionally designated**.

At the same time, the final intensity doctrine prevents another collapse:

```text
higher person
    does not necessarily mean
stronger prātimokṣa restraint
```

The determination is relational to the individual's own continuum and to this specific form of restraint.

## Organon Contact Point

The restrained contact point is a useful fourfold distinction:

```text
normative status
formal possession
actual observance
intensity of determination
```

These cannot be identified without residue.

The Bhāṣya also shows that practical forms are not merely private intentions. Their determinacy involves `śāstṛprajñapti`: a socially and doctrinally instituted articulation of who counts as what and which limbs constitute the form. But this institutional moment remains contested within the passage and should not yet be expanded into a general theory.
