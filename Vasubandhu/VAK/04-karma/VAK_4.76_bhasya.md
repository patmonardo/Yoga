# VAK 4.76 — Bhāṣya Study

## Kārikā Anchor

**VAK 4.76**

**IAST**

> paiśunyaṃ kliṣṭacittasya vacanaṃ parabhedane /
> pāruṣyam apriyaṃ sarvaṃ kliṣṭaṃ bhinnapralāpitā // 4.76 //

**Best current translation**

Divisive speech is the utterance of one whose mind is afflicted, directed toward dividing others. Harsh speech is disagreeable speech. All afflicted speech is disordered chatter.

Kārikā study: `VAK_4.76.md`

---

## Bhāṣya Source

The Bhāṣya continues directly from the discussion of false speech:

> paiśunyaṃ kliṣṭacittasya vacanaṃ parabhedane /
>
> yat kliṣṭacittasya pareṣāṃ bhedāya vacanam aprītaye tat paiśunyam /
>
> arthabhijña iti vartate abhrāntyeti ca /
>
> pāruṣyam apriyam /
>
> vacanāt / kliṣṭacittasyeti vartate / arthābhijñe abhrāntyeti ca /
>
> yat kliṣṭacittasya pareṣām apriyavacanam arthābhijñe tatraiva ca yad vivakṣitaṃ tat pāruṣyam /
>
> sarvaṃ kliṣṭaṃ bhinnapralāpitā //
>
> vākyam ity adhikāro vartate / sarvaṃ kliṣṭaṃ vacanaṃ saṃbhinnapralāpaḥ /
>
> saiva saṃbhinnapralāpitā / tadyogena hi saṃbhinnapralāpī bhavati /
>
> ato 'nyat kliṣṭam ity anye /
>
> ato mṛṣāvādādikād vākkarmaṇo yad anyat kliṣṭaṃ vacanaṃ tat saṃbhinnapralāpa ity apare /
>
> lapanā-gīta-nāṭyavat /
>
> kuśāstravac ca /

The prose then gives examples: flattering talk by a monk for wrong livelihood, songs sung under passion, theatrical speech aimed at entertaining others, heterodox treatises, lamentation, social chatter, and similar afflicted speech. It also notes that in the age of a cakravartin songs can exist without being `saṃbhinnapralāpa` when they concern renunciation rather than sensual taste.

---

## Prose Translation

“Divisive speech is the utterance of one with an afflicted mind, directed toward dividing others.”

An utterance made by one whose mind is afflicted, for the sake of producing division among others and causing lack of affection, is divisive speech. The conditions already stated—namely, that the hearer understands the meaning and that there is no mistake concerning the intended object—continue to apply.

“Harsh speech is disagreeable.”

Because the text says “speech,” the qualification “of one with an afflicted mind” continues here as well, as do the requirements that the hearer understand the meaning and that there be no mistake concerning the intended person. Speech from an afflicted mind that is disagreeable to others, understood by the hearer, and directed toward the very person intended is harsh speech.

“All afflicted speech is disordered chatter.”

The governing term “speech” continues. According to one explanation, every afflicted utterance is `saṃbhinnapralāpa`; this condition itself is called `saṃbhinnapralāpitā`, since by possessing it one becomes a person given to such chatter.

Others say instead: only afflicted speech other than the verbal actions beginning with false speech is `saṃbhinnapralāpa`.

Examples include flattering talk, erotic or passion-driven song, theatrical speech, and heterodox treatises. Thus a monk practicing wrong livelihood may flatter; people under passion may sing; actors may speak for the entertainment of others; adherents of bad treatises may expound them. Lamentation, social chatter, and other afflicted verbal acts distinct from false speech and the other specified courses are likewise `saṃbhinnapralāpa`.

An objection asks how song can exist in the age of a cakravartin without being `saṃbhinnapralāpa`. The reply is that such songs are then connected with renunciation, not with sensual or vulgar taste. Another view still allows some `saṃbhinnapralāpa` then in connection with weddings, marriages, and similar talk, though not necessarily as a full karmapatha.

## Argument Reconstruction

### 1. Divisive speech is functionally directed toward rupture

The Bhāṣya defines `paiśunya` by practical end:

```text
kliṣṭacitta
    ↓
vacana
    ↓
para-bhedana
    ↓
aprīti
```

The speech aims at breaking a relation among others and producing non-affection.

This is stronger than merely “saying something negative about someone.” The determining feature is the intended relational effect.

### 2. Earlier completion-conditions carry forward

The Bhāṣya explicitly says that two conditions from the discussion of false speech remain in force:

```text
arthābhijña
    the hearer understands the meaning

abhrānti
    no mistake concerning the intended object/person
```

Thus divisive speech and harsh speech are not constituted by sound alone. They are completed verbal acts only when their meaning reaches the relevant recipient under the intended object-relation.

### 3. Harsh speech is relationally specific injury

`Pāruṣya` is not simply language someone happens to dislike. The Bhāṣya combines four conditions:

```text
kliṣṭacitta
+
apriya-vacana
+
arthābhijña
+
abhrānti
```

The speech must arise from an afflicted mind, be disagreeable or injurious, be understood, and reach the very person intended.

### 4. `Saṃbhinnapralāpa` is disputed in extension

The Bhāṣya records two positions.

**Position A:**

```text
all afflicted speech
    = saṃbhinnapralāpa
```

Under this reading, the expression is maximally broad and overlaps the other verbal bad courses.

**Position B:**

```text
afflicted speech
minus
false speech
divisive speech
harsh speech
    = saṃbhinnapralāpa
```

Under this reading, it functions as the residual fourth verbal karmapatha.

The Bhāṣya does not erase this disagreement.

### 5. The examples show that the category is practical, not merely syntactic

The examples include:

```text
lapanā
    flattering / ingratiating talk

gīta
    passion-driven song

nāṭya
    theatrical chatter for entertainment

kuśāstra
    bad or heterodox treatises

parideva
    lamentation

saṃgaṇikā
    social chatter / conversational gossip
```

This makes it unsafe to translate `saṃbhinnapralāpa` simply as “incoherent speech” or “semantic disorder.” Much of the speech can be perfectly intelligible and coherent while remaining ethically afflicted or practically frivolous.

### 6. Content and ethical function are separable

The cakravartin example is decisive. Song as such is not automatically afflicted speech.

```text
song + grāmyarasa
    → afflicted

song + naiṣkramya
    → not afflicted in that way
```

The form of expression does not determine its karmic status by itself; the ethical orientation and function matter.

## Voice Identification

**Voice: Vasubandhu / expository voice** — definitions of divisive and harsh speech and transmission of prior conditions.

**Voice: one doctrinal interpretation** — all afflicted speech counts as `saṃbhinnapralāpa`.

**Voice: alternative doctrinal interpretation (`anye`, `apare`)** — only afflicted speech other than the first three verbal karmapathas counts as `saṃbhinnapralāpa`.

**Voice: Objector** — asks how song can exist in the cakravartin age without constituting afflicted chatter.

**Voice: reply / transmitted position** — songs connected with renunciation differ from songs connected with vulgar sensual taste.

---

## New Technical Terms

- **para-bhedana** — division or rupture of others; **status:** implicit in Kārikā, elaborated in Bhāṣya; **function:** defines the practical end of divisive speech.
- **aprīti** — lack of affection, estrangement; **status:** Bhāṣya addition; **function:** specifies the relational effect aimed at by divisive speech.
- **lapanā** — flattering or ingratiating speech; **status:** Bhāṣya-only here; **function:** example of afflicted residual verbal activity, especially wrong livelihood.
- **grāmyarasa** — vulgar or sensual taste; **status:** Bhāṣya-only here; **function:** distinguishes afflicted song from renunciation-oriented song.
- **naiṣkramyopasaṃhita** — connected with renunciation; **status:** Bhāṣya-only here; **function:** shows that the same expressive form can be ethically different according to orientation.
- **kuśāstra** — bad / heterodox treatise; **status:** Bhāṣya-only here; **function:** example showing `saṃbhinnapralāpa` need not be grammatically incoherent.

## Doctrinal Determination

The four verbal bad karmapathas can now be differentiated more precisely:

```text
mṛṣāvāda
    false relation between recognition and statement

paiśunya
    speech directed toward relational rupture

pāruṣya
    afflicted injurious speech reaching the intended person

saṃbhinnapralāpa
    afflicted discourse, either broadly all such speech
    or residually speech outside the first three courses
```

The Bhāṣya confirms that the completion-conditions developed under false speech carry into `paiśunya` and `pāruṣya`:

```text
meaning must be understood
+
object/person relation must not be mistaken
```

Therefore verbal karma is again shown to be semantically and relationally constituted.

The most important revision concerns the fourth member. The first-pass Kārikā file treated it as “disconnected” or semantically dissipated speech. That is too narrow. The Bhāṣya examples show that `saṃbhinnapralāpa` includes speech that may be coherent but ethically afflicted, purposeless, sensual, ingratiating, entertainment-driven, doctrinally corrupt, lamenting, or socially frivolous.

A safer canonical rendering at this stage is:

```text
saṃbhinnapralāpa
    disordered / frivolous afflicted speech
```

with the doctrinal extension left explicit rather than forced into one English word.

---

## Logical Determination

The verbal karmapathas are differentiated by the relation that speech practically determines:

```text
false speech
    corrupts speaker ↔ meaning relation

divisive speech
    corrupts person ↔ person relation

harsh speech
    corrupts speaker ↔ recipient relation through injury

saṃbhinnapralāpa
    corrupts or dissipates the practical orientation of speech itself
```

But the Bhāṣya prevents us from making the last member purely formal or semantic. The same expressive form can shift ethical status according to its practical orientation:

```text
same form
    song

different orientation
    sensual entertainment
    vs
    renunciation

→ different karmic status
```

Thus moral classification requires more than surface form.

---

## Translation Consequences

1. `paiśunya` should remain **divisive speech**, with emphasis on intended relational rupture.
2. `pāruṣya` is **harsh / injurious speech**, but the Bhāṣya requires recipient understanding and correct targeting.
3. `saṃbhinnapralāpa` should not be reduced to “nonsense,” “incoherence,” or “semantic disconnection.”
4. The disagreement over whether it includes all afflicted speech or only the residual class must remain visible.
5. The examples show that ethically afflicted speech can be semantically coherent.

---

## Light Organon Contact Point

The text yields a useful distinction among three layers of verbal action:

```text
semantic content
relational target
governing practical orientation
```

An utterance may be semantically coherent yet practically corrupt because it aims to divide, injure, flatter for gain, excite passion, distract, or sustain a false doctrine.

The Bhāṣya therefore resists any model in which speech ethics is reduced to propositional truth alone.

A text-grounded structural schema is:

```text
rooted intention
    ↓
meaning-bearing expression
    ↓
recipient comprehension
    ↓
relational / practical effect
    ↓
karmic determination
```

This is continuity with 4.74: verbal karma exists as successfully mediated practical meaning.

---

## Conclusion

VAK 4.76 completes the definitions of the remaining verbal bad karmapathas. Divisive speech is afflicted speech directed toward breaking relations; harsh speech is afflicted, injurious speech reaching and being understood by its intended recipient. `Saṃbhinnapralāpa` is more difficult: one interpretation makes all afflicted speech fall under it, while another restricts it to afflicted speech outside false, divisive, and harsh speech.

The Bhāṣya examples decisively show that the fourth category cannot simply mean semantic incoherence. Its field includes intelligible but ethically disordered or frivolous discourse. The decisive criterion is the practical and affective orientation of speech, not its surface linguistic form alone.
