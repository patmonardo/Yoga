# VAK 4.111 — Bhāṣya Study

## Kārikā Anchor

**VAK 4.111**

**sarvatra sarvaṃ dadataḥ kāruṇyād dānapūraṇam |**  
**aṅgacchede ’py akopāt tu rāgiṇaḥ kṣāntiśīlayoḥ || 4.111 ||**

> For one giving everything everywhere from compassion, generosity is perfected. And for one still possessing passion, through absence of anger even when his limbs are cut off, patience and discipline are perfected.

See: `VAK_4.111.md`.

## Natural Segmentation

The commentary asks a general question: **at what stage does the Bodhisattva complete each pāramitā?** Verse 4.111 answers for three of them:

1. **dāna-pāramitā** — perfected through unrestricted giving grounded solely in compassion;
2. **kṣānti-pāramitā** — perfected when even extreme bodily injury produces no anger;
3. **śīla-pāramitā** — perfected in that same event, because no retaliatory transgression arises.

The remaining three pāramitās are treated immediately afterward in 4.112.

## Literal Prose Translation

Then, at what stage does the Bodhisattva complete which perfection?

“When, everywhere, he gives everything out of compassion, giving is completed.”

When he gives everything to everyone—even as far as his eye and marrow—out of compassion, **without desiring some superior worldly prosperity or distinction**, then to that extent the perfection of giving is complete.

“And for one still possessing passion, through absence of anger even when his limbs are cut off, patience and discipline are completed.”

When, although he has not yet become free from passion, not even the slightest anger arises while his limbs are being cut apart, then his perfections of patience and discipline are complete.

## Course of Argument

### 1. Completion is tested through extremity

Vasubandhu does not define a pāramitā merely by repeated ordinary instances of its corresponding virtue. He identifies a point at which the virtue has become practically unsurpassable within the Bodhisattva career.

The tests are therefore maximal:

```text
all recipients + all possessions
    → test of dāna

extreme bodily destruction + zero anger
    → test of kṣānti and śīla
```

### 2. Generosity requires both universality and purified motive

The Kārikā gives `sarvatra sarvam`: everywhere, everything. The Bhāṣya expands this to `sarvasmai sarvaṃ` — everything to everyone — and dramatizes its completeness with “as far as the eye, as far as the marrow.”

But the crucial addition is negative:

`nābhyudayaviśeṣaṃ lipsamānaḥ`

The Bodhisattva is **not seeking a superior form of worldly prosperity or distinction** through the gift.

Thus:

```text
complete material relinquishment
    +
compassion
    +
absence of self-directed worldly reward
        ↓
dāna-pāramitā
```

The perfection is therefore not measured by quantity alone. Its determining ground is compassion purified of acquisitive return.

### 3. Patience is perfected before passion is completely abandoned

The word `rāgiṇaḥ` is doctrinally important. Vasubandhu glosses it with `avītarāga`: the Bodhisattva is **not yet free from passion**.

Yet even in that condition, while his limbs are being severed:

`nālpam api kupyati`

— “he does not become angry even in the slightest degree.”

This sharply distinguishes:

```text
complete destruction of every afflictive disposition
    ≠
perfection of the pāramitā as practical conduct
```

A latent capacity for affliction may remain, while the decisive conduct has become unshakable.

### 4. One event perfects both kṣānti and śīla

The genitive dual `kṣāntiśīlayoḥ` is intentional. The same non-angry response under extreme injury completes two perfections:

- **kṣānti**: the injury is endured without anger;
- **śīla**: the Bodhisattva does not retaliate through misconduct.

Thus patience concerns the internal non-arising of hostility, while discipline concerns the preservation of right conduct under the same pressure.

## Voice Identification

**Voice: Vasubandhu** — framing question and explanatory prose.

The verse and commentary present the standard Bodhisattva pāramitā account without an explicit objection in this unit.

## New Technical Terms

- **pāramitā-paripūraṇa** — completion of a perfection.
  - **status:** previously encountered conceptually, here given explicit completion criteria.
  - **function:** doctrinal definition.

- **abhyudaya-viśeṣa** — superior worldly prosperity, elevation, or distinction.
  - **status:** rare in this local discussion.
  - **function:** excluded motive; marks the difference between compassionate giving and reward-seeking generosity.

- **avītarāga** — not yet free from passion.
  - **status:** previously encountered doctrinal vocabulary.
  - **function:** qualification showing that perfection of conduct can precede complete eradication of passion.

- **akopa** — non-arising of anger.
  - **status:** rare in the Kārikās.
  - **function:** decisive criterion for completion of both patience and discipline.

## Doctrinal and Logical Determination

The verse yields two distinct models of perfection.

### Positive universality

```text
all beings
    ×
all that can be given
    ×
compassion alone
        ↓
perfected generosity
```

### Negative invariance

```text
maximal bodily injury
    +
continued susceptibility to passion
    +
zero anger
        ↓
perfected patience
        +
perfected discipline
```

The second is especially important. A pāramitā is not necessarily the state in which contrary psychological possibilities have become metaphysically impossible. Rather, it can be the state in which the governing practical principle remains invariant even under the strongest opposition.

So:

```text
remaining afflictive capacity
    does not imply
remaining practical incompletion
```

The relevant perfection is demonstrated in actuality of conduct.

## Translation Consequences for the Kārikā

The existing Kārikā translation is fundamentally sound, but the Bhāṣya requires two additions to its interpretation:

1. `kāruṇyāt` excludes giving for the sake of superior worldly reward or distinction;
2. `rāgiṇaḥ` must be retained strongly: patience and discipline are perfected **while passion has not yet been completely abandoned**.

A fuller translation is therefore:

> **For one who, everywhere, gives everything to everyone out of compassion, seeking no superior worldly reward, generosity is perfected. And for one still not free from passion, when not even the slightest anger arises though his limbs are cut apart, patience and moral discipline are perfected.**

## Light Organon Contact

The two halves display complementary forms of practical universality.

Generosity is **extensive universality**: no recipient and no possession is excluded.

Patience and discipline are **intensive stability**: no degree of opposition is sufficient to reverse the governing practical principle.

```text
universality over the field of recipients
    → dāna

invariance under maximal opposition
    → kṣānti / śīla
```

The bodily form may literally be divided while the practical determination remains undivided. This is an especially sharp contrast with the preceding Saṅgha-schism sequence: there, division of normative recognition dissolves collective unity; here, division of the body fails to dissolve ethical unity.
