# VAK 4.75 — Bhāṣya Study

## Kārikā Anchor

**VAK 4.75**

> cakṣuḥ śrotramanaścittair anubhūtaṃ tribhiś ca yat /
> tad dṛṣṭaśrutavijñātaṃ mataṃ coktaṃ yathākramam // 4.75 //

**Revised working translation:**

> What is experienced by eye-knowledge is called **seen**; by ear-knowledge, **heard**; by mental knowledge, **known**; and by the other three [nose-, tongue-, and body-knowledges], **thought**, respectively.

Existing Kārikā analysis: `VAK_4.75.md`

The Bhāṣya requires a substantial correction to the first-pass interpretation. The verse is not primarily laying out four general epistemic provenance-statuses. It presents a specific Vaibhāṣika allocation of four conventional expressions across the six knowledge-bases, and the prose immediately subjects that allocation to criticism.

## Bhāṣya Source

The natural argumentative unit begins with the sūtra classification of sixteen modes of statement and continues through the alternative explanations following the Kārikā.

The sūtra gives eight non-noble (`anārya`) usages and eight noble (`ārya`) usages, depending on whether one says of what is seen, heard, thought, or known that it is—or is not—seen, heard, thought, or known.

Vasubandhu then asks:

> `tatraiṣāṃ dṛṣṭaśrutamatavijñātānāṃ kiṃ lakṣaṇam /`
>
> What are the characteristics of these: seen, heard, thought, and known?

The Kārikā follows.

The Bhāṣya expands it:

- what is experienced by **eye-knowledge** (`cakṣurvijñāna`) is called **seen** (`dṛṣṭa`);
- what is experienced by **ear-knowledge** (`śrotravijñāna`) is **heard** (`śruta`);
- what is experienced by **mental knowledge** (`manovijñāna`) is **known** (`vijñāta`);
- what is experienced by **nose-, tongue-, and body-knowledge** (`ghrāṇa-jihvā-kāya-vijñāna`) is **thought** (`mata`).

The prose explicitly attributes the final assignment to the Vaibhāṣikas.

## Literal Prose Translation

What is experienced by eye-knowledge is called “seen.” What is experienced by ear-knowledge is called “heard.” What is experienced by mental knowledge is called “known.” What is experienced by nose-, tongue-, and body-knowledge is called “thought.”

Why?

Odors, tastes, and tangibles, because they are ethically indeterminate, are like dead things; therefore, with respect to these, the designation “thought” is used—so say the Vaibhāṣikas.

What establishes this?

Scripture and reasoning.

A sūtra is cited in which the Buddha distinguishes what has not been seen by the eye, heard by the ear, or known by the mind, and says that in the seen there will be merely the seen, in the heard merely the heard, in the thought merely the thought, and in the known merely the known.

From the fact that the terms “seen,” “heard,” and “known” are applied to three object-fields, the Vaibhāṣikas infer that the remaining objects—odor and the rest—receive the designation “thought.” Their reasoning is that otherwise odors, tastes, and tangibles would fall outside all four conventional expressions.

The reply is that the cited sūtra is not probative for this purpose, because it has another meaning. The Buddha is not there teaching the technical characteristics of the four conventional expressions. Rather, in relation to the sixfold object-field, he teaches that there should be merely the conventional recognition “seen,” etc., without superimposing agreeable or disagreeable signs upon them.

Another group says instead:

- what is directly apprehended by the five senses is **seen**;
- what is received from another is **heard**;
- what one accepts for oneself through reasoning and inference is **thought**;
- what is inwardly directly realized is **known**.

On this account, each of the five sensory object-fields may be spoken of as seen, heard, thought, or known depending on the mode of access, and the sixth object-field is only excluded from “seen.” Thus the Vaibhāṣika argument that otherwise odors and the rest would lack conventional designation fails.

The earlier teachers (`pūrvācāryāḥ`) are then reported as saying:

- **seen**: what has been directly made present through the eye;
- **heard**: what is received through the ear and from another;
- **thought**: what one has oneself considered;
- **known**: what has been inwardly experienced or attained.

## Argument Reconstruction

The passage has four distinct moments.

### 1. Practical context: truthful and false modes of statement

The four expressions arise inside the preceding discussion of false speech. The sūtra distinguishes truthful and false use of:

```text
dṛṣṭa   seen
śruta   heard
mata    thought
vijñāta known
```

The question is therefore not abstract epistemology for its own sake. It is: **what must these predicates mean if truthful and false assertion are to be distinguished?**

### 2. Vaibhāṣika technical allocation

The Vaibhāṣika account maps the six knowledge-bases into four conventional expressions:

```text
cakṣurvijñāna      → dṛṣṭa
śrotravijñāna      → śruta
manovijñāna        → vijñāta

ghrāṇavijñāna
jihvāvijñāna       → mata
kāyavijñāna
```

This is what the compressed Kārikā states.

### 3. Vaibhāṣika proof and Vasubandhu's criticism

The Vaibhāṣikas appeal to both sūtra and reasoning.

Their reasoning is exhaustive classification:

```text
six object-fields
    ↓
three explicitly named by
seen / heard / known
    ↓
remaining three
must fall under thought
```

But Vasubandhu challenges both supports.

First, the sūtra has another purpose. It concerns the refusal to add attachment-producing evaluative superimpositions to experience; it is not a technical definition of the four terms.

Second, the alleged exhaustive classification is unnecessary if the expressions are not tied one-to-one to object-fields. On an alternative account, the same sensory object can be “seen,” “heard,” “thought,” or “known” according to **how one stands in relation to it**.

### 4. Alternative semantic-pragmatic analyses

The alternative accounts shift the organizing principle away from fixed sense-faculty allocation and toward mode of acquisition or realization.

One account gives:

```text
five-sense directness   → seen
report from another     → heard
reasoned acceptance     → thought
inner direct realization→ known
```

The earlier teachers give a closely related but not identical account:

```text
eye-directness          → seen
ear / report            → heard
self-consideration      → thought
inward realization      → known
```

The Bhāṣya therefore deliberately leaves a live doctrinal contrast around the Kārikā's compact Vaibhāṣika formulation.

## Voice Identification

**Voice: Vaibhāṣika position**

The assignment of odor, taste, and tangibles to `mata`, together with the appeal to scripture and reasoning, is explicitly attributed to the Vaibhāṣikas.

**Voice: Vasubandhu / critical examiner**

The claim that the cited sūtra has another purpose and does not define the four expressions functions as criticism of the Vaibhāṣika proof.

**Voice: uncertain / alternative teachers**

`kecit tāvad āhuḥ` introduces an alternative position without secure school attribution.

**Voice: earlier teachers**

`pūrvācāryā evam āhuḥ` introduces the account of the earlier teachers.

## New Technical Terms

- **ṣoḍaśa vyavahārāḥ** — the sixteen modes/usages of assertion, eight non-noble and eight noble
  - **status:** introduced in the Bhāṣya around this Kārikā
  - **function:** scriptural classification / ethical-semantic context

- **anārya-vyavahāra / ārya-vyavahāra** — non-noble and noble modes of speaking
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-operational here
  - **function:** distinction between false and truthful assertion

- **pratyakṣa / pratyakṣīkṛta** — direct apprehension / made directly present
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only in this local argument
  - **function:** alternative definition of `dṛṣṭa`

- **yuktyanumāna** — reasoning and inference
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only in this local argument
  - **function:** alternative definition of `mata`

- **pratyātmavedya / pratyātmapratisaṃvedita** — inwardly or personally experienced
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only in this local argument
  - **function:** alternative definition of `vijñāta`

- **pūrvācārya** — earlier teacher(s)
  - **status:** Bhāṣya attributional term
  - **function:** doctrinal attribution

## Doctrinal and Logical Determination

The major doctrinal result is that the Kārikā's fourfold terminology cannot safely be converted into a single finalized epistemology without preserving the Bhāṣya's dispute.

The verse itself presents the Vaibhāṣika mapping:

```text
knowledge-base
    ↓
fixed conventional predicate
```

But the prose opens another possibility:

```text
same object
    ↓
different mode of access
    ↓
different legitimate predicate
```

This distinction is important.

Under the Vaibhāṣika account, the classification is principally **object/faculty indexed**.

Under the alternative accounts, it is principally **relation/mode-of-access indexed**.

The Bhāṣya does not permit us simply to identify these two models.

The passage also gives a methodological lesson. A scriptural phrase cannot automatically be used as a technical Abhidharma definition merely because its vocabulary matches. One must first establish the **purpose (`artha`) of the sūtra in context**.

That is the strongest logical movement in the prose:

```text
same words
    ≠
same doctrinal function

citation
    requires
contextual determination of purpose
```

## Translation Consequences for the Kārikā

The first-pass philosophical translation should be revised.

It had treated 4.75 mainly as a taxonomy of epistemic provenance:

```text
I saw it
I heard it
I know it
I thought it
```

That becomes legitimate only as an **alternative Bhāṣya-level interpretation**, not as the immediate meaning of the Kārikā.

The primary translation should instead preserve the Vaibhāṣika allocation actually stated by the verse:

> What is experienced by eye-knowledge is called seen; by ear-knowledge, heard; by mental knowledge, known; and by the three remaining knowledges, thought, respectively.

Then the Bhāṣya note should state that Vasubandhu challenges the proof offered for this allocation and records alternative accounts based on mode of access.

The Kārikā source itself confirms the compressed sequence of eye, ear, mind, and the remaining three together with `dṛṣṭa / śruta / vijñāta / mata`. The Bhāṣya explicitly expands the mapping and attributes the contested rationale to the Vaibhāṣikas.

## Light Organon Contact Point

The strongest Organon contact is not a ready-made provenance ontology but a more basic logical rule:

```text
Expression
    cannot be interpreted
    apart from its governing use
```

The same term can function under different principles of classification.

Here:

```text
Model A
faculty/object indexed

Model B
mode-of-access indexed
```

Vasubandhu's criticism requires us to ask which principle actually generates the distinctions rather than merely accepting an inherited table.

This is precisely the kind of reflective mediation the Bhāṣya pass is meant to expose:

```text
Kārikā determination
    ↓
Vaibhāṣika explanation
    ↓
proof offered
    ↓
proof criticized
    ↓
alternative principle disclosed
```

The result is not skepticism about the four terms. It is a demand that their **ground of differentiation** be made explicit.

## Conclusion

VAK 4.75 does not simply define four epistemic statuses. It records a Vaibhāṣika fourfold allocation within the ethics of truthful speech and then opens that allocation to criticism.

The stable result for this pass is:

```text
Kārikā:
    eye → seen
    ear → heard
    mind → known
    nose/tongue/body → thought

Bhāṣya:
    this is the Vaibhāṣika allocation
    its scriptural proof is challenged
    its classificatory reasoning is challenged
    alternative access-based definitions are preserved
```

This is a material correction to the first-pass interpretation and should be carried into the later Super Kārikās cleanup.
