# VAK_4.121_bhasya

## Kārikā Anchor

**VAK 4.121**  
**caitye tyāgānvayaṃ puṇyaṃ maitryādivad agṛhṇati |**  
**kukṣetre ’pīṣṭaphalatā phalabījāviparyayāt || 4.121 ||**

> At a shrine there is merit connected with relinquishment, even though no one receives the gift, as with loving-kindness and the other immeasurables. Even in a bad field there is desirable fruit, because seed and fruit are not inverted.

## Natural Segmentation

1. Objection: how can a stūpa-offering produce merit when there is no living enjoyer?
2. Distinction between merit from relinquishment and merit from recipient-use.
3. Refutation of the thesis that benefit to another is the sole cause of merit.
4. Comparison with loving-kindness, the other immeasurables, and cultivation of right view.
5. Second objection: if merit arises from one’s own mind, is bodily/verbal offering superfluous?
6. Reply: enacted body/speech intensifies the devotion that generates it.
7. Analogy with murderous intention versus bodily/verbal enactment.
8. Final objection: can a bad field reverse a wholesome gift into a bad result?
9. Seed–fruit argument: field affects magnitude, not the basic polarity of the seed.

## Literal Prose Translation

It was said that for one still possessing passion, a gift to a shrine is for one’s own benefit. But when there is no enjoyer there, how can merit arise?

Merit is of two kinds. One is connected with relinquishment: it arises from the relinquishment itself. The other is connected with enjoyment: it arises from the recipient’s use of the thing given.

At a shrine there is merit connected with relinquishment; there is no merit connected with enjoyment.

How, then, can there be merit when no one receives anything there?

Why should merit necessarily exist when there is a recipient but not when there is none?

Because no one is benefited. But this is not a reason. If merit arose only from benefiting others, there would be no merit in the cultivation of loving-kindness and the other immeasurables, nor in the cultivation of right view.

Therefore merit must be admitted even with respect to a shrine.

Just as in loving-kindness and the other immeasurables merit arises from one’s own mind even without a recipient or the benefit of another, so too, with regard to one formerly possessed of qualities, merit made through devotion to him arises from one’s own mind.

Would not bodily and verbal acts of giving and honoring therefore be useless?

No. The devotion that gives rise to those acts is more intense. Just as one who intends to kill an enemy generates more demerit when, even after that enemy has died, he performs bodily and verbal karma toward the corpse while taking it to be the enemy, than from the mere murderous intention alone, so too, when the Teacher has passed away, one who performs bodily and verbal acts of giving and honoring generated by devotion to him produces more merit than from devotion alone.

If the seed consisting in an act of giving produces a desirable fruit in a good field, would it then produce an undesirable fruit in a bad field?

Even in a bad field there is a desirable fruit, because fruit does not reverse the nature of its seed. From a grape seed arises a sweet grape fruit; from a neem seed arises a bitter neem fruit. Likewise, even in a bad field, a seed of giving undertaken from an orientation toward the welfare of others produces a desirable, not undesirable, fruit. Because of the defect of the field, however, that seed may have little fruit or no fruit.

## Argument Reconstruction

The Bhāṣya distinguishes two causal contributions to giving:

```text
tyāga
    relinquishment by the donor
        → tyāgānvaya-puṇya

paribhoga
    use/enjoyment by the recipient
        → paribhogānvaya-puṇya
```

A stūpa-offering lacks the second but not the first.

Thus:

```text
no living recipient
    ≠
no merit
```

The opponent proposes:

```text
merit requires actual benefit to another
```

Vasubandhu rejects this by counterexample:

```text
maitrī and the other apramāṇas
samyagdṛṣṭi-bhāvanā
```

can be meritorious without a recipient accepting or consuming anything. Their merit is `svacittaprabhava`: produced from the agent’s own mind.

But this does not reduce merit to an inward intention alone. Bodily and verbal enactment intensifies the generating devotion:

```text
bhakti
    ↓ generates
body/speech offering
    ↓
more intense merit
```

The killing analogy makes the principle symmetrical across wholesome and unwholesome karma:

```text
intention alone
    <
intention expressed in body/speech
```

The final argument then restricts the causal role of the field:

```text
seed determines basic ethical polarity
field conditions magnitude/effectiveness
```

A bad field cannot turn a wholesome seed into an unwholesome fruit, though it may reduce the fruit or render the act fruitless.

## Voice Identification

- **Voice: Objector** — asks how merit can arise where there is no enjoyer or receiver; later asks whether bodily/verbal offering becomes useless if merit comes from one’s own mind; finally proposes that a bad field should produce bad fruit.
- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — distinguishes the two kinds of merit, rejects other-benefit as the sole causal criterion, invokes the immeasurables and right-view cultivation, uses the killing analogy, and gives the seed/fruit reply.

## New Technical Terms

- **tyāgānvaya-puṇya** — merit connected with relinquishment itself.  
  **Status:** rare / locally introduced.  
  **Function:** causal distinction within giving.

- **paribhogānvaya-puṇya** — merit connected with the recipient’s enjoyment or use of the gift.  
  **Status:** rare / locally introduced.  
  **Function:** causal distinction within giving.

- **svacittaprabhava** — arising from one’s own mind.  
  **Status:** Bhāṣya formulation.  
  **Function:** explains merit without a living receiver.

- **bhakti** — devotion directed toward the Teacher or one possessed of qualities.  
  **Status:** previously encountered conceptually, important here.  
  **Function:** subjective cause of shrine-offering and its intensification through enactment.

- **parahitādhyāśaya** — underlying orientation toward the welfare of others.  
  **Status:** Bhāṣya-specific formulation in this passage.  
  **Function:** fixes the wholesome nature of the giving-seed even in a bad field.

## Doctrinal and Logical Determination

### 1. Recipient-benefit is not a necessary condition of merit

A stūpa does not consume the gift, yet relinquishment itself is karmically effective.

```text
actual recipient enjoyment
    is sufficient for one kind of merit
but not necessary for all merit
```

### 2. Merit can be generated from the agent’s own mental determination

The comparison with `maitrī`, the other `apramāṇas`, and `samyagdṛṣṭibhāvanā` establishes that wholesome causal efficacy may arise from the constitution of the agent’s own continuum without external reception.

This does not mean that the field is irrelevant. The Buddha as past `guṇavat` remains the intentional object of devotion.

### 3. Enactment intensifies intention

The bodily/verbal act is not redundant merely because the primary merit arises from the mind.

```text
bhakti alone
    <
bhakti embodied in giving/honoring activity
```

The same architecture holds negatively in the enemy-killing analogy.

### 4. Seed and field have different causal functions

The grape/neem analogy fixes the distinction:

```text
seed
    determines the basic qualitative tendency of the fruit

field
    conditions whether that tendency becomes large, small, or ineffective
```

A bad field may yield:

```text
alpa-phala
    little fruit

or

aphala
    no fruit
```

but it does not convert a wholesome giving-seed into an undesirable result merely by being a bad field.

### 5. Giving is neither purely subjective nor purely recipient-dependent

The full structure is:

```text
underlying wholesome orientation
    ↓
devotion / relinquishing volition
    ↓
body-speech enactment
    ↓
field conditions magnitude
    ↓
fruit preserves seed-quality
```

## Translation Consequences for the Kārikā

The first-pass translation is broadly sound, but the Bhāṣya sharpens two points.

1. `tyāgānvayaṃ puṇyam` is a technical contrast with `paribhogānvaya-puṇya`; it should be translated explicitly as **“merit connected with relinquishment”**, not merely generic merit from dedication.
2. `phalabījāviparyaya` means that the **fruit does not reverse the qualitative nature of the seed**. The field may reduce magnitude, even to zero, but does not invert wholesome into unwholesome.

A refined translation is:

> **At a shrine there is merit connected with relinquishment, as with loving-kindness and the other immeasurables, even though no one receives the gift. Even in a bad field the fruit remains desirable, because fruit does not reverse the nature of its seed.**

## Light Organon Contact

This passage makes an unusually precise distinction among three causal moments:

```text
agent-determination
field
fruit
```

The field is constitutive but not sovereign. It can condition the realization and magnitude of a causal determination without replacing the determination supplied by the seed.

The two kinds of merit also show that one practical act may have more than one causal channel:

```text
relinquishment-channel
recipient-enjoyment-channel
```

A stūpa-offering closes only the first channel, yet that is sufficient for genuine merit.

The strongest structural point is therefore:

```text
ethical quality originates in the determinate activity
and is mediated by the field,
not manufactured by the field.
```
