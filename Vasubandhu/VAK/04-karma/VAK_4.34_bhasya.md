# VAK_4.34_bhasya — False Speech, Intoxicants, and Protection of the Other Precepts

## Kārikā Anchor

```iast
mṛṣāvādaprasaṅgāc ca sarvaśikṣāvyatikrame |
pratikṣepaṇasāvadyān madyād evānyaguptaye ||
```

**Working translation**

> False speech is prohibited because it readily follows when any of the training rules is transgressed. Intoxicants alone are prohibited as a fault established by prohibition, because they leave the other [precepts] unguarded.

See: `VAK_4.34.md`.

## Source Cautions

The Kārikā cannot be understood correctly if its two halves are collapsed into a single argument.

The first half completes a question already opened immediately before the verse: why, among faults of speech, is abstention from **false speech** specifically included in the five lay training rules rather than abstention from divisive speech and the remaining verbal misconducts?

The second half begins a new question: why is a training rule established concerning an act that is not, on the Ābhidharmika account, naturally blameworthy (`prakṛtisāvadya`)? The answer concerns intoxicants as `pratikṣepaṇasāvadya`, blameworthy by explicit prohibition.

The source also preserves a real disagreement between **Vinayadharas** and **Ābhidharmikas** over whether intoxicant use is naturally blameworthy. This dispute should not be flattened into a single school position.

## Natural Argument

### 1. Why false speech rather than the other verbal faults?

Vasubandhu asks:

```text
Why is abstention from false speech
specifically a lay-restraint training rule,
rather than abstention from divisive speech and so forth?
```

He first says that the same three considerations used immediately before for sexual misconduct apply here as well:

```text
atigarhyatva
    exceptional blameworthiness

saukarya
    practicability

akriyāpti
    acquisition through non-performance
```

But false speech has an additional special reason:

```text
mṛṣāvādaprasaṅga
```

Whenever a person transgresses one of the training rules and is questioned about it, the thought readily occurs:

> “I did not do such a thing.”

Thus violation of **any** training rule can generate an occasion for false speech.

The false-speech precept therefore protects the integrity of the entire discipline at the level of acknowledgement and disclosure.

```text
transgression of another rule
    → questioning
    → temptation to deny the act
    → false speech
```

This is more precise than saying simply that lying is morally serious. Its inclusion has a structural relation to all the other training rules.

### 2. Why establish a rule concerning a `pratikṣepaṇasāvadya` act?

The text then changes questions.

Some faults are `prakṛtisāvadya`, naturally blameworthy. Others are blameworthy because they have been specifically prohibited: `pratikṣepaṇasāvadya`.

The question becomes:

```text
Why should one of the five lay training rules
concern something blameworthy by prohibition
rather than another naturally blameworthy act?
```

The answer is:

```text
madyād eva
    intoxicants specifically

anyaguptaye
    for guarding the others
```

A person who drinks intoxicants may leave the remaining limbs of restraint unguarded.

```text
madya
    → impairment / intoxication
    → other aṅgas become agupta
```

This confirms the functional placement of the intoxicant rule already seen at VAK 4.29 as the `apramādāṅga`.

### 3. Why classify intoxicant use as `pratikṣepaṇasāvadya`?

The Bhāṣya immediately asks how this classification is known.

The Ābhidharmika reasoning is subtle:

```text
prakṛtisāvadya action
    is performed only with a kliṣṭa mind
```

But intoxicating drink can, in principle, be consumed medicinally with an intention of remedy (`pratīkārabuddhi`) so long as one knows the quantity will not intoxicate.

Therefore:

```text
knowing it will intoxicate + drinking
    → kliṣṭa citta

knowing the quantity will not intoxicate + medicinal use
    → not necessarily kliṣṭa citta
```

From this the Ābhidharmika side argues that drinking is not naturally blameworthy in every possible instance.

### 4. Vinayadhara objection

The Vinayadharas disagree:

```text
prakṛtisāvadyam madyam
```

They cite disciplinary evidence, including statements attributed to the Buddha forbidding even a tiny quantity and the conduct of noble persons who refrain from it even in other lives.

The dispute is therefore not over whether intoxicants are dangerous. It concerns the technical classification of their fault:

```text
Vinayadhara:
    intoxicants are prakṛtisāvadya

Ābhidharmika:
    intoxicants are prajñaptisāvadya / pratikṣepaṇasāvadya
    and are prohibited because they create a basis for pramāda
```

The Ābhidharmika reply appeals to the role of intoxication in destroying recollection and creating a `pramādasthāna`, a basis of heedlessness.

## Voice Identification

**Voice: Vasubandhu** — frames the successive questions and organizes the dialectic.

**Voice: received Abhidharma / likely Vaibhāṣika framework** — explanation that false speech is specifically selected because every training-rule violation creates an occasion for it.

**Voice: Ābhidharmikas** — explicitly deny that intoxicant use is intrinsically `prakṛtisāvadya`; explain its prohibition through heedlessness and the protection of the remaining precepts.

**Voice: Vinayadharas** — explicitly maintain that intoxicants are naturally blameworthy and cite Vinaya evidence.

The passage should therefore be read as a genuine intra-Buddhist technical disagreement, not as a single uncontested doctrine.

## New Technical Terms

- **mṛṣāvādaprasaṅga** — occasion or liability for false speech arising from another transgression; **status:** Bhāṣya-explicit development of the Kārikā; **function:** explains why false speech is singled out among verbal faults.
- **śikṣātikrama** — transgression of a training rule; **status:** recurring disciplinary vocabulary; **function:** condition generating the occasion for lying.
- **samanuyujyamāna** — being questioned or called to account; **status:** Bhāṣya-specific here; **function:** mediates transgression into possible false denial.
- **pratikṣepaṇasāvadya** — blameworthy by prohibition/rejection; **status:** major technical distinction introduced here; **function:** classifies acts whose fault depends on disciplinary prohibition rather than intrinsic moral character.
- **prakṛtisāvadya** — naturally or intrinsically blameworthy; **status:** major technical distinction; **function:** contrast-class for `pratikṣepaṇasāvadya`.
- **pratīkārabuddhi** — intention or understanding directed toward remedy; **status:** Bhāṣya-specific here; **function:** medicinal-use counterexample in the classification dispute.
- **madanīya** — intoxicating, capable of producing intoxication; **status:** local technical usage; **function:** marks the point at which drinking is associated with a defiled intention.
- **pramādasthāna** — basis or occasion of heedlessness; **status:** important Abhidharma/Vinaya vocabulary; **function:** explains the distinctive practical danger of intoxicants.
- **anyagupti** — protection/guarding of the others; **status:** Kārikā term clarified by Bhāṣya; **function:** gives the systemic reason for the intoxicant rule.

## Doctrinal Determination

1. False speech is singled out among verbal faults partly because violation of any other precept may provoke a false denial when one is questioned.
2. The false-speech rule therefore has a protective relation to the integrity of the whole discipline.
3. Intoxicants are singled out because intoxication can leave the other training limbs unguarded.
4. The Ābhidharmika position distinguishes naturally blameworthy acts from acts blameworthy by explicit prohibition.
5. On this account, intoxicant use is not intrinsically defiled in every conceivable case; medicinal, non-intoxicating use supplies the counterexample.
6. Vinayadharas reject this classification and maintain that intoxicants are naturally blameworthy.
7. The shared practical concern is preservation of recollection and non-heedlessness so that the other restraints remain operative.

## Effect on Kārikā Translation

The earlier translation should be sharpened so that the two halves remain distinct.

A better working translation is:

> False speech [is singled out] because it can arise upon transgression of any training rule. Intoxicants alone are [prohibited as] blameworthy by prohibition, for the protection of the others.

This keeps `mṛṣāvādaprasaṅga` tied to **all training-rule transgression** and `anyaguptaye` tied specifically to **intoxicants**.

It also avoids translating `pratikṣepaṇasāvadya` merely as “repudiation is blameworthy,” which misses the technical classification established by the Bhāṣya.

## Logical Determination

VAK 4.34 shows that the five lay rules are selected not merely by arranging five independent moral prohibitions side by side.

Two of the rules have a **reflexive protective function** with respect to the system of restraint itself.

```text
false-speech restraint
    protects truthful acknowledgment
    when another restraint has failed

intoxicant restraint
    protects the conditions
    under which the other restraints can remain guarded
```

Thus the structure is not flat:

```text
direct restraints
    +
restraints protecting the integrity of restraint
```

The system contains determinations whose function is to preserve the continued efficacy of the system as a whole.

## Organon Contact Point

A restrained comparison is available here.

The practical whole does not merely contain rules directed outward toward isolated acts. It also contains determinations that preserve the **conditions under which rule-governed agency remains possible**.

```text
first-order practical determination:
    do not perform X

second-order practical determination:
    preserve the capacity
    by which first-order determinations remain operative
```

This is especially clear for intoxicants:

```text
smṛti / apramāda preserved
    → other restraints remain guardable
```

The point should remain within Vasubandhu's practical analysis for now; a larger comparison with reflective or self-preserving normative systems can wait until the Karma chapter is complete.
