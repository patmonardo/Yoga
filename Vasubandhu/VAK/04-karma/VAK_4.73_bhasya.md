# VAK_4.73 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

> prāṇātipātaḥ saṃcintya parasyābhrānti-māraṇam /
> adattādānam anya-sva-svīkriyā bala-cauryataḥ // 4.73 //

**Best translation:**  
Destruction of life is the deliberate, non-mistaken killing of another. Taking what is not given is the appropriation of another's property by force or theft.

**Kārikā analysis:** [`VAK_4.73.md`](./VAK_4.73.md)

### Opening question

> **[243|12–243|13]**  
> kiyatā punaḥ prāṇātipātaṃ svayaṃ kurvataḥ karmapatho bhavati kiyatā yāvanmithyādṛṣṭiḥ karmapathaḥ iti lakṣaṇaṃ vaktavyaṃ taducyate /

Vasubandhu now asks for the defining conditions by which each of the ten becomes a completed karmapatha.

### Killing

> **[243|14]**  
> prāṇātipātaḥ saṃcintya parasyābhrāntimāraṇam /
>
> **[243|15]**  
> yadi mārayiṣyāmyenamiti saṃjñāya paraṃ mārayati tameva ca mārayati nānyaṃ bhramitvā /
>
> **[243|16]**  
> iyatā prāṇātipāto bhavati /

Literal prose translation:

If, having formed the representation, “I shall kill this one,” he kills another, and kills precisely that one rather than someone else through confusion, to that extent there is destruction of life.

The two operative conditions are therefore explicit:

```text
saṃcintya
    deliberate lethal determination

abhrānti
    no mistake in the intended target
```

The karmapatha is not constituted by death alone. The intention must be directed toward a living other, and the actual victim must stand in the intended object-relation.

### The doubtful case

> **[243|16–243|17]**  
> yastarhi saṃdigdho mārayati kimayaṃ prāṇī na prāṇīti sa evānyo veti so 'pyavaśyametaṃ niścayaṃ labdhvā tatra praharati /
>
> **[243|17–243|18]**  
> yo 'stu so 'stviti kṛtamevānena tyāgacittaṃ bhavati /

The apparently doubtful killer does not escape the condition of determination merely because the object was initially uncertain. Before striking, he reaches a practical resolve. Even the thought “whoever it may be, let it be” has already formed the relinquishing or lethal orientation (`tyāgacitta`).

Thus uncertainty at an earlier moment does not negate the karmapatha if the act is finally performed under a determinate willingness to destroy life.

### How killing is possible if the aggregates are momentary

> **[243|18]**  
> kathaṃ kṣaṇikeṣu skandheṣu prāṇātipāto bhavati /
>
> **[243|19]**  
> prāṇo nāma vāyuḥ kāyacittasaṃniśrito vartate / tamabhipātayati /
>
> **[243|19–243|20]**  
> yathā pradīpaṃ nirodhayati ghaṇṭāsvanaṃ vā /
>
> **[243|20]**  
> jīvitendriyaṃ vā prāṇāstannirodhayati /
>
> **[243|20–243|21]**  
> yadyekasyāpi jīvitakṣaṇasyotpadyamānasyāntarāyaṃ karoti prāṇātipātāvadyena spṛśyate nānyathā /

Voice: Vasubandhu

The objection is generated by the doctrine of momentary aggregates: if there is no enduring living substance, what exactly is destroyed?

The Bhāṣya gives two formulations. `Prāṇa` may be spoken of as the vital wind supported by body and mind; or, more precisely for Abhidharma purposes, as the life faculty (`jīvitendriya`). Killing consists in interrupting its arising. Vasubandhu compares this with extinguishing a lamp or stopping the sound of a bell.

The exact criterion is striking:

```text
if one obstructs the arising
of even one life-moment
that would otherwise arise,
one incurs the fault of prāṇātipāta
```

This means destruction of life is formulated not as annihilation of a substance but as the wrongful interruption of a conditioned life-series.

### Whose life is it?

> **[243|22]**  
> kasya tajjīvitaṃ yastadabhāvānmṛto bhavati /
>
> **[243|22–243|23]**  
> kasyeti ṣaṣṭhīṃ pudgalavāde vicārayiṣyāmaḥ /

The Bhāṣya refuses to settle the apparent possessive “whose life?” here. It explicitly postpones analysis of the genitive to the later discussion of the person (`pudgala`).

This is an important discipline point: the ordinary grammar of possession is used provisionally without being allowed to smuggle in a permanent subject.

### Scriptural support

> **[243|23–243|26]**  
> uktaṃ tu bhagavatā ... tasmātsendriyaḥ kāyo jīvatītyucyate anindriyo mṛta iti /

The cited verse states that when lifespan, warmth, and knowledge leave the body, it lies discarded like unconscious wood. Vasubandhu concludes that a body possessing the faculties is called living, while one without them is called dead.

### Nirgrantha objection: unintended killing

> **[243|26–243|27]**  
> abuddhipūrvādapi prāṇivadhāt karturadharmo yathā 'gnisaṃyogāddāha iti nirgranthāḥ /

Voice: Nirgrantha position reported by Vasubandhu

The Nirgranthas hold that even an unintentional killing produces demerit for the doer, just as contact with fire produces burning.

Vasubandhu rejects this causal analogy by reductio.

> **[243|27–244|05]**  
> teṣāṃ paradāradarśane 'pyeṣa prasaṅgaḥ ... acetanānāṃ ca kāṣṭhādīnāṃ gṛhapāte prāṇivadhāt pāpaprasaṅgo na vā dṛṣṭāntamātrātsiddhiriti /

If mere causal connection were enough, absurd consequences would follow: morally relevant fault would arise from cases where one merely causes conditions under which another suffers or dies, and even insentient objects could become bearers of fault when they causally kill. A mere analogy with fire therefore proves nothing.

The doctrinal point is exact:

```text
physical causation alone
    ≠ karmic killing

intentional determination
    + correctly related object
    + interruption of life
    = prāṇātipāta
```

### Taking what is not given

> **[244|07]**  
> adattādānamanyasvasvīkriyā balacauryataḥ // 4.73 //
>
> **[244|08]**  
> abhrāntyeti vartate /
>
> **[244|08–244|09]**  
> yadi balādvā cauryeṇa vā parasvaṃ svīkaroti yatraiva ca balacauryābhiprāyeṇānyatra saṃjñāvibhramāt iyatā 'dattādānaṃ bhavati /

The Bhāṣya explicitly carries `abhrānti` forward from killing into theft.

Thus taking what is not given requires:

```text
another's possession
    +
appropriative intention
    +
force or stealth
    +
non-mistaken object relation
```

The center of the definition is `svīkriyā`: making another's possession one's own.

Theft is therefore not merely moving an object. It is a successful practical appropriation under a relation the agent understands as belonging to another.

### Special cases of property

> **[244|09–244|10]**  
> stūpādapaharato buddhāda dattādānam /
>
> **[244|10–244|11]**  
> sarvaṃ hi tadbhagavatā parinirvāṇakāle parigṛthītamiti /
>
> **[244|10–244|11]**  
> yeṣāṃ samrakṣyamityapare /

Taking from a stūpa is treated as taking what is not given from the Buddha, on one explanation because such property was taken into his possession at parinirvāṇa. Another view assigns it to those whose duty is to protect it.

> **[244|11–244|12]**  
> asvāmikaṃ ca nidhimuddharato viṣayasvāmikāt parivartakaṃ harataḥ kṛte karmaṇi sīmāprāptebhyaḥ akṛte sarvabuddhaśaikṣyebhyaḥ /

The Bhāṣya continues with special ownership cases involving ownerless treasure and objects connected with a territorial or communal field. These details show that `adattādāna` presupposes a determinate ownership relation; classification depends on whose possession is actually violated.

## Argument Reconstruction

VAK 4.73 marks a methodological shift. Earlier verses classified karmapathas by roots, phases, completion, and supports. Here Vasubandhu asks for their **constitutive conditions**.

For killing:

```text
living continuum
    +
deliberate lethal determination
    +
non-mistaken targeting
    +
causal interruption of a life-moment
    =
prāṇātipāta
```

For theft:

```text
another's possession
    +
knowledge of the relevant object relation
    +
appropriative intention
    +
force or stealth
    +
successful taking
    =
adattādāna
```

The common structure is relational rather than merely behavioral. The bodily event becomes a karmapatha only through intention, object-reference, and completion.

## Voice Identification

- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — framing question, definitions, momentariness analysis, postponement of the pudgala issue, theft analysis.
- **Voice: Nirgrantha position** — unintended killing produces demerit by mere causal connection, like burning from fire.
- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — reductio against the Nirgrantha causal analogy.
- **Voice: uncertain / alternative scholastic position** — the alternative account of ownership of stūpa property (`apare`).

## New Technical Terms

- **tyāgacitta** — a mind of relinquishment/destructive abandonment in relation to the victim.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only in this local unit.
  - **function:** explains why an initially doubtful killer can still arrive at determinate lethal intention.

- **jīvitendriya** — life faculty.
  - **status:** previously encountered in the Kośa, made technically operative here.
  - **function:** provides the Abhidharma account of what is interrupted in killing.

- **jīvitakṣaṇa** — a moment of life.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only formulation in this unit.
  - **function:** specifies killing under momentariness as obstruction of a life-moment that would otherwise arise.

- **pudgalavāda** — the discussion concerning the person.
  - **status:** anticipated here for later treatment.
  - **function:** postpones the metaphysical analysis of the possessive “whose life?”

- **abuddhipūrvaka** — not preceded by deliberate cognition/intention.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only in this local dispute.
  - **function:** marks the Nirgrantha claim concerning unintended killing.

- **svīkriyā** — making one's own; appropriation.
  - **status:** present in the Kārikā, explicated by the prose.
  - **function:** constitutive definition of taking what is not given.

## Doctrinal and Logical Determination

The Bhāṣya refuses two reductions:

```text
killing
    ≠ mere physical production of death

stealing
    ≠ mere physical displacement of an object
```

Both require a determinate relation between agent and object.

The killing analysis is especially important because it joins ethical intentionality to a non-substantialist ontology. There is no need for an enduring life-substance to be annihilated. The fault consists in intentionally preventing the continuation of a conditioned life-series.

```text
no permanent living substance
    does not imply
no meaningful destruction of life
```

The theft analysis parallels this relational logic. Property is not defined here metaphysically in the abstract; the karmapatha occurs when another's possession is knowingly and successfully appropriated under force or stealth.

The Nirgrantha controversy establishes an explicit principle:

```text
causal efficacy
    is not sufficient for
moral authorship
```

That is one of the strongest local statements yet about the distinction between mere causation and karma.

---

## Translation Consequences for the Kārikā

The existing translation is substantially confirmed, but `abhrānti` should be understood more strongly than generic “without error.” It means that the intended object and the actually affected object remain correctly linked.

Recommended stabilized rendering:

> **Destruction of life is the deliberate, non-mistaken killing of another; taking what is not given is the appropriation of another's possession by force or stealth.**

The Bhāṣya also makes clear that `abhrānti` carries into the second pāda. This should be retained in any later Super Kārikās pass even though the compact verse states it only in the first half.

## Light Organon Contact Point

The text gives a precise distinction among:

```text
causal event
intentional act
object relation
completed karmic determination
```

A rational account of action therefore cannot classify moral activity from surface events alone.

```text
Event
    becomes Action
through intention and object-reference

Action
    becomes completed karmapatha
through successful realization of its constitutive result
```

The point is genetically earned from Vasubandhu's argument itself: moral determination is a structured relation, not a predicate pasted onto an event after the fact.

VAK 4.73 establishes:

```text
prāṇātipāta
    deliberate + non-mistaken interruption of another life-series

adattādāna
    non-mistaken appropriation of another's possession
```

The decisive general principle is:

```text
mere causation
    ≠
karmic authorship
```
