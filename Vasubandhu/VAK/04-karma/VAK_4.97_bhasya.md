# VAK 4.97 — Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

> triṣu dvīpeṣv ānantaryaṃ śaṇḍhādīnāṃ tu neṣyate |  
> alpopakārālajjitvāc cheṣe gatiṣu pañcasu || 4.97 ||

**Best current translation:** Immediate-consequence karma is possible in the three continents, but is not accepted for śaṇḍha and related cases, because of diminished parental benefit and diminished moral inhibition. The remaining two obstructions occur across the five destinies.

**Kārikā analysis:** [VAK_4.97.md](./VAK_4.97.md)

**Source caution:** the e-text has evident typographic irregularities (`tetṣvapi`, `śaṇḍā dīnām`); they are preserved here rather than silently normalized.

## Natural Segmentation

The passage has four movements:

1. determine the locus in which ānantarya karma is possible;
2. explain why śaṇḍha and related embodied cases are excluded from the technical category;
3. clarify that the “remaining” members of the verse are the other two obstructions, not remaining grave acts;
4. begin an analysis of the intrinsic karmic form of the five ānantaryas, preparing VAK 4.98–4.99 on Saṅgha schism.

## Literal Prose Translation

> Among these obstructions, which should be understood in which destiny? To begin with, the ānantarya karmas occur exclusively in the three continents—not in Uttarakuru and not in the other destinies; still less in another realm. Even in those three continents they occur only for women and men.
>
> They are not accepted for śaṇḍha and the like. Why? The same reason was given in connection with the absence of non-restraint; and furthermore, because of little benefit and lack of moral inhibition. Respectively, this concerns the parents and those persons. Their mother and father are said to have rendered them comparatively little benefit, because they preside over an incomplete embodied condition and because affection is slight. Nor is there present in them, in relation to their parents, the strong moral inhibition such that by killing them they would incur an ānantarya.
>
> For the same reason it is not accepted among animals and pretas. The Venerable One says that it might occur among those of sharp intelligence, as with a well-bred horse. Even for a human being, if the mother and father are non-human, killing them would not constitute an ānantarya.
>
> Thus the karmic obstruction has been explained. “The remaining [two] in the five destinies.” What are the remaining two? The afflictive obstruction and resultant obstruction. Resultant obstruction occurs among humans as the Kuru condition and among the gods as the non-percipient beings.
>
> What is the intrinsic nature of the ānantarya karmas? Four are bodily karma and one is verbal karma; three are killing, one is false speech, and one is an undertaking toward killing. For Tathāgatas are by nature incapable of being killed by assault. Saṅgha schism is called an ānantarya by transferring the designation from effect to cause, or because the Saṅgha is split by means of it.

## Argument Reconstruction

### 1. Ānantarya requires a determinate human locus

The restriction is stronger than the bare Kārikā suggests:

```text
ānantarya
    only in three human continents
    not Uttarakuru
    not other destinies
    not another realm
    and within the three continents: only women and men
```

The category is therefore not a universal label attached simply to a physical event. Its technical constitution depends upon a qualified agent, a qualified relational field, and a world in which those relations possess the relevant karmic force.

### 2. Why parental killing is relationally determined

The Bhāṣya explains the exclusion of śaṇḍha and related cases through two considerations:

```text
alpopakāra
    diminished parental benefit

apatrāpya / lajja
    diminished moral inhibition before the parents
```

The first reason is assigned to the parents: their constitutive beneficence is held to be less because the embodied condition is described as incomplete and parental affection correspondingly reduced. The second concerns the agent: the relevant powerful inhibition or reverence toward the parents is said not to arise strongly enough for their killing to acquire the technical ānantarya status.

This is historically difficult material and should be recorded as Vasubandhu's doctrinal exposition rather than generalized into a philosophical anthropology.

### 3. Animal and preta cases reveal the rule is not merely biological

The same exclusion is extended to animals and pretas. Yet a reported voice (`bhadantaḥ`) allows a possible exception for exceptionally intelligent animals, using the example of a well-bred horse.

That reported exception is philosophically revealing even if doctrinally uncertain: the criterion is not sheer species membership. What matters is the presence of the cognitive and relational structure required for the act to bear this exceptional karmic determination.

The following case reinforces this point: even a human who kills non-human parents does not thereby incur the parental ānantarya. The field-relation itself must possess the required form.

### 4. “The remaining” means the other two obstructions

The Kārikā-pass translation was too loose here. Vasubandhu explicitly asks:

```text
ke punaḥ śeṣe?
what then are the remaining two?
```

and answers:

```text
kleśāvaraṇa
vipākāvaraṇa
```

Thus `śeṣe gatiṣu pañcasu` does **not** mean that other grave karmic acts occur throughout all five destinies. It closes the three-obstruction classification begun in 4.96.

### 5. Transition to the intrinsic form of the five ānantaryas

The Bhāṣya immediately asks `kiṃsvabhāvāny ānantaryāṇi`—what are the ānantaryas by intrinsic nature?

The answer prepares the Saṅgha-schism discussion:

```text
four bodily karmas
one verbal karma

three killings
one false speech
one undertaking toward killing
```

The last category concerns maliciously causing a Tathāgata to bleed: because a Tathāgata cannot actually be killed by assault, the act is classified as a preparatory undertaking toward killing.

Saṅgha schism requires a further distinction: the schism itself is not straightforwardly identical with the verbal karma that causes it. That problem opens VAK 4.98.

## Voice Identification

- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — asks where each obstruction occurs and states the restrictions on ānantarya.
- **Voice: doctrinal exposition / attribution uncertain** — gives the `alpopakāra` and `apatrāpya` explanation for the exclusion of śaṇḍha and related cases.
- **Voice: Bhadanta** — proposes that an ānantarya might occur among exceptionally intelligent animals, illustrated by a well-bred horse.
- **Voice: Vasubandhu** — clarifies `śeṣe` as the afflictive and resultant obstructions and begins the intrinsic classification of the five ānantaryas.

## New Technical Terms

- **apatrāpya** — moral inhibition, shame before another, or reluctance grounded in regard for others; here specifically the powerful inhibition before one's parents whose violation contributes to ānantarya status.
  - **status:** previously encountered as an ethical mental factor, but newly operative in the explanation of grave karma.
  - **function:** causal/relational condition.

- **alpopakāra** — comparatively little benefit or beneficence.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya explanatory term in this passage.
  - **function:** explains the differential gravity of the parental field.

- **paṭubuddhi** — sharp intelligence.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only qualification here.
  - **function:** reported exception testing the scope of the rule.

- **anupakramadharman** — not subject to successful lethal assault; said here of Tathāgatas.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya explanatory term.
  - **function:** explains why bloodshed against a Tathāgata is classified as a killing-preparation rather than completed killing.

- **hetau phalopacāra** — figurative transfer of the name of an effect to its cause.
  - **status:** important Bhāṣya hermeneutical/logical expression.
  - **function:** explains why the causal false speech may be designated by the name `saṃghabheda`.

## Doctrinal Determination

VAK 4.97 makes clear that an ānantarya is not defined by visible behavior alone.

Its constitution depends upon a whole:

```text
qualified agent
    + qualified karmic field
    + determinate relation of beneficence / reverence
    + appropriate world-locus
    + completed intentional activity
```

This does not mean that ordinary harmful conduct is harmless outside those conditions. It means only that the highly technical category `ānantarya` has stricter constitutive conditions.

The passage also completes the distribution of the three obstructions:

```text
karmāvaraṇa
    ānantarya karma
    restricted locus

kleśāvaraṇa
    possible through the five destinies

vipākāvaraṇa
    specific resultant states
    including Kuru humans and non-percipient gods
```

## Effect on the Kārikā

Two corrections are required to the first-pass interpretation.

First, `alpopakārālajjitvāt` should not be generalized into an abstract doctrine of “moral responsibility.” Vasubandhu gives a very specific traditional explanation involving diminished parental benefit and diminished `apatrāpya` in certain embodied cases.

Second, and more importantly:

```text
śeṣe
    = the remaining two obstructions
    = kleśāvaraṇa + vipākāvaraṇa
```

not “remaining grave acts.”

A revised philosophical translation is therefore:

> Ānantarya karma is possible among humans of the three continents, but is not technically constituted in śaṇḍha and related cases, because the required parental beneficence and moral inhibition are held to be diminished. The other two obstructions—afflictive and resultant—extend through the five destinies according to their respective forms.

## Logical Determination

The passage gives a useful distinction between an event-description and its complete karmic determination:

```text
PhysicalEvent(x)
    ≠
AnantaryaKarma(x)
```

For the latter, additional relational predicates are required:

```text
QualifiedAgent(x)
QualifiedField(x)
RelevantBeneficenceRelation(x)
RelevantMoralInhibition(x)
QualifiedLocus(x)
```

The gravity of karma is therefore relationally mediated rather than reducible to an externally described motion.

At the same time the category remains objective within the Kośa framework: these relations do not make the act “subjective”; they constitute what kind of karma the act actually is.

## Light Organon Contact

The strongest contact point is not a moral analogy but a logical one:

```text
bare act
    ↓ through determinate relations
complete karmic determination
```

A determination acquires its full practical meaning only within a concrete relational field. This is continuous with the chapter's larger movement from `cetanā` through manifest and unmanifest action into karmically effective actuality.

The text also guards against abstract universalization: a predicate such as `ānantarya` cannot simply be attached wherever an externally similar event appears. Its truth depends on the mediated whole that constitutes the act.
