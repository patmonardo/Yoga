# VAK_4.39_bhasya — Does a Grave Downfall Destroy Prātimokṣa Restraint?

## 1. Kārikā Anchor

```iast
patanīyena cety eke saddharmāntardhito 'pare |
dhanarṇavat tu kāśmīrair āpannasyeṣyate dvayam ||
```

**Working translation**

> Some say that [prātimokṣa restraint is lost] also through an offense entailing downfall; others, through the disappearance of the true Dharma. The Kāśmīras, however, maintain that one who has incurred such an offense possesses both [restraint and misconduct], as one may possess both wealth and debt.

Kārikā analysis: [VAK_4.39.md](./VAK_4.39.md)

## 3. Source Cautions

The source contains several difficult or visibly corrupt readings, including `saddhamantirdhito`, `kādmīrair`, and compressed Vinaya quotations. They are preserved rather than silently normalized.

The debate is rapid and not every anonymous reply can be assigned with absolute certainty. Where the speaker is not explicit, the analysis identifies the argumentative side rather than forcing a personal attribution.

---

## 4. Natural Argument

### A. Two additional theories of loss

The verse first records two positions beyond the four causes accepted in VAK 4.38.

Some maintain:

```text
one of the four patanīya / pārājika offenses
    → loss of bhikṣu or śrāmaṇera restraint
```

Others maintain:

```text
disappearance of the true Dharma
    → loss of prātimokṣa restraint
```

The rationale offered for the latter is that when the true Dharma has disappeared, the disciplinary trainings, boundaries, and formal ecclesiastical acts cease to operate.

### B. Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣika: grave offense does not cancel restraint

The Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣikas reject the first position.

A monk who incurs a root grave offense does not thereby abandon the whole monastic restraint. Their reason is structural:

```text
disturbance of one part
    ≠
loss of the entire restraint
```

They go further: even after an offense there is no complete cutting-off of śīla.

Instead the offender possesses two determinations:

```text
śīla
    +
dauḥśīlya
```

The analogy is financial:

```text
wealth
    +
debt
```

The presence of debt does not annihilate the wealth. Likewise a grave unwholesome determination need not annihilate the previously acquired restraint.

After disclosure of the offense, the analogy is extended to repayment of debt: the person is again characterized through śīla rather than through the outstanding fault.

### C. Scriptural objection: the Buddha says the offender falls from bhikṣu-status

An objection cites the Buddha to the effect that such a person becomes an `aśramaṇa`, an `aśākyaputrīya`, and falls away from bhikṣu-status.

The Kāśmīra reply is that the statement concerns `paramārthabhikṣutva`, bhikṣuhood in the highest or ultimate sense.

Vasubandhu's response is unusually sharp:

```text
idam abhisāhasaṃ vartate
```

This is an overbold interpretive maneuver.

Why? Because a statement presented as `nītārtha`, definitive in meaning, is being forcibly redirected to another sense.

Vasubandhu also notes the practical danger: such reinterpretation supplies conditions for misconduct and for many afflictions.

### D. Vinaya definition blocks the `paramārtha-bhikṣu` escape

The debate then appeals to a Vinaya classification of four senses of `bhikṣu`.

For the present disciplinary question, however, `bhikṣu` means specifically one who has received full ordination by the formal act with motion as the fourth (`jñapticaturthakarmopasaṃpanna`).

The person under discussion was not previously a `paramārthabhikṣu` in the special liberated sense. Therefore the claim that he later ceases only to be a `paramārthabhikṣu` does not answer the Vinaya statement about loss of bhikṣu-status.

### E. The palm-tree analogy answers the “one part cannot destroy the whole” argument

The Kāśmīra argument had been:

```text
one-part disturbance
    cannot destroy
whole restraint
```

The counterargument invokes the canonical image of a palm tree whose head has been cut off and which therefore cannot sprout, grow, or increase.

The relevant structure is:

```text
not every part is merely one interchangeable part

some part is mūlabhūta
    root-constituting
```

Thus:

```text
cutting a root-constituting part
    → remainder incapable of further growth
```

A root grave offense breaches the boundary constituting bhikṣu-status and, through intense shamelessness (`tīvra-anapatrāpya`), cuts the root of restraint.

On this argument, complete abandonment of the restraint becomes reasonable:

```text
maulī āpatti
    → saṃvaramūlaccheda
    → kṛtsnasaṃvaratyāga
```

### F. Social and disciplinary exclusion confirms the gravity of the break

The text reinforces the point through Vinaya consequences: the offender is excluded from communal food, lodging, and the shared life of monks, and scriptural language calls for removing the one who is `abhikṣuṃ bhikṣuvādinam`—not a monk though claiming to be one.

The opposing side nevertheless continues to defend some residual `bhikṣubhāva`.

A further scriptural classification includes a `mārgadūṣin`, one who corrupts the Path, among four kinds of śramaṇa. The reply is that such a designation may remain merely by outward form (`ākṛtimātrāvaśeṣatva`), compared with burnt wood, dried matter, rotten seed, or a dead being: a name or appearance can remain when the effective capacity has been lost.

The details of the final exchange are difficult and compressed, but the central distinction is clear:

```text
nominal / residual designation
    ≠
capacity for renewed restraint
```

The inability to be reordained is explained by the continuum having been ruined by intense shamelessness and therefore being incapable of restraint (`saṃvarābhavya`).

### G. Disappearance of the true Dharma prevents new acquisition, not possession already obtained

The Bhāṣya finally resolves the second additional theory.

When the true Dharma disappears:

```text
Vinaya acts no longer exist
    → no new acquisition of restraint
```

But:

```text
restraint already acquired
    → is not thereby abandoned
```

So disappearance of the Dharma is an **acquisition-blocking condition**, not a retroactive loss-condition.

This is a decisive correction to the second view recorded in the Kārikā.

## 5. Voice Identification

**Voice: some teachers (`eke`)**  
A grave downfall offense causes loss of bhikṣu or śrāmaṇera restraint.

**Voice: other teachers (`apare`)**  
Disappearance of the true Dharma causes loss of restraint.

**Voice: Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣikas**  
A grave offender continues to possess restraint together with misconduct, like wealth together with debt.

**Voice: Vasubandhu / critical interlocutor**  
The attempt to reinterpret the Buddha's definitive statement as referring only to `paramārthabhikṣutva` is called an `abhisāhasa`; the palm-tree analogy is used to show that destruction of a root-constituting part can destroy the whole restraint.

**Voice: uncertain in the final exchange**  
Several short objections and replies concerning residual bhikṣu-status, the four śramaṇas, and reordination shift rapidly and should not be over-attributed.

## 6. New Technical Terms

- **patanīya / pārājika** — a downfall or defeat offense of root gravity.
  - **status:** important Vinaya term newly central in this passage.
  - **function:** candidate cause of complete loss of monastic restraint.

- **maulī āpatti** — root or fundamental offense.
  - **status:** newly explicit.
  - **function:** offense said to breach the constitutive boundary of bhikṣu-status.

- **dauḥśīlya** — misconduct; contrary ethical determination.
  - **status:** previously encountered in ethical contexts.
  - **function:** Kāśmīra counterpart to still-existing `śīla` after grave offense.

- **āviṣkaraṇa** — disclosure or manifestation of an offense.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya procedural vocabulary.
  - **function:** compared to repayment of debt in the Kāśmīra model.

- **paramārthabhikṣutva** — bhikṣuhood in the highest or ultimate sense.
  - **status:** newly explicit.
  - **function:** Kāśmīra interpretive device rejected as insufficient for the Vinaya passage at issue.

- **nītārtha** — definitive in meaning.
  - **status:** important hermeneutical term.
  - **function:** grounds Vasubandhu's criticism of forced reinterpretation.

- **abhisāhasa** — overbold or unwarranted interpretive maneuver.
  - **status:** striking Bhāṣya-only polemical term.
  - **function:** Vasubandhu's criticism of the `paramārthabhikṣu` reading.

- **mūlabhūta** — root-constituting; fundamental to the whole.
  - **status:** newly important logical term.
  - **function:** explains how destruction of one part can destroy the capacity of the remainder.

- **anapatrāpya** — shamelessness / absence of moral concern.
  - **status:** technical mental-factor vocabulary.
  - **function:** intense form is said to cut the root of restraint.

- **saṃvarābhavya** — incapacity for restraint.
  - **status:** Bhāṣya-only formation in this local argument.
  - **function:** explains why the defeated offender cannot simply be ordained again.

## 7. Doctrinal Determination

VAK 4.39 records, tests, and partially rejects competing theories of loss.

```text
Theory A:
    grave pārājika
        → restraint lost

Theory B:
    disappearance of true Dharma
        → restraint lost

Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣika:
    grave offense
        → restraint remains
        + dauḥśīlya acquired
```

The Bhāṣya then substantially qualifies the Kāśmīra position.

The decisive counterargument is not merely that a grave offense contradicts restraint. It is that a `maulī` offense can destroy a **root-constituting condition** of the whole disciplinary form.

```text
ordinary local breach
    may leave restraint standing

root-level defeat
    may cut the root
    of the restraint itself
```

Likewise, disappearance of the true Dharma is distinguished from loss of an already possessed restraint:

```text
saddharmāntardhāna
    → no new Vinaya acquisition

but not

saddharmāntardhāna
    → automatic loss of already acquired restraint
```

## 8. Effect on Kārikā Translation

The Kārikā translation remains substantially sound, but the Bhāṣya requires two cautions.

First, the wealth-and-debt analogy is explicitly the **Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣika position**, not Vasubandhu's final unqualified conclusion.

Second, `saddharmāntardhitaḥ` should not be read as an accepted loss-condition without qualification. The prose concludes that disappearance of the true Dharma prevents **new acquisition** because Vinaya acts no longer occur, but does not destroy restraint already acquired.

A refined translation is therefore:

> Some say that [prātimokṣa restraint is lost] also through a downfall offense; others say through the disappearance of the true Dharma. The Kāśmīras, however, hold that one who has incurred the offense possesses both [restraint and misconduct], as one may possess both wealth and debt.

The verse itself reports positions; the Bhāṣya adjudicates among them.

## 9. Logical Determination

The deepest issue is the difference between **contradiction within a form** and **destruction of a constitutive ground**.

The Kāśmīra model says:

```text
restraint
    +
contrary offense

can coexist
```

The counterargument says that this is true only so long as the contrary event does not destroy a `mūlabhūta` condition of the restraint.

Thus:

```text
local contradiction
    ≠ necessarily
abolition of the whole

but

root-destruction
    → loss of capacity of the remainder
```

The palm-tree analogy makes this precise:

```text
one part
    may be quantitatively partial

and yet

qualitatively constitutive
    of the whole's continuing capacity
```

This is more exact than either a simple cancellation model or a simple coexistence model.

The final discussion of disappearance of the Dharma adds another distinction:

```text
condition for acquisition
    ≠
condition for continued possession
```

A system can lose the institutional means of generating a new status without thereby cancelling statuses already generated.

---

## 10. Organon Contact Point

The restrained Organon contact is the distinction among **opposition, root-destruction, and persistence**.

```text
contrary determination
    may coexist with prior determination

unless

the contrary determination destroys
that from which the prior form remains generatively possible
```

The crucial notion is therefore not mere logical inconsistency but **genetic dependence**.

The palm-tree analogy gives an unusually clear practical form of this principle:

```text
cutting an accidental branch
    leaves the organism capable of growth

cutting the constitutive head/root
    removes the capacity of the remainder
```

This remains internal to Vasubandhu's argument and does not require importing a larger metaphysical architecture.

## 12. Commit Note

VAK 4.39 opens a major dispute over whether grave contradiction merely coexists with restraint or destroys its root. The Kāśmīra Vaibhāṣika wealth/debt model is explicitly recorded, but Vasubandhu strongly contests attempts to preserve bhikṣu-restraint after a root downfall by appealing to definitive Vinaya language and the palm-tree analogy. The passage ends by distinguishing disappearance of the true Dharma as preventing new acquisition rather than cancelling restraint already acquired.
