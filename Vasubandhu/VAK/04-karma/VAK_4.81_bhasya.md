# VAK 4.81 Bhāṣya

## Kārikā

**yugapad yāvad aṣṭābhir aśubhaiḥ saha vartate |**  
**cetanā daśabhir yāvac chubhair naikāṣṭapañcabhiḥ || 4.81 ||**

Best current translation:

> Intention may occur simultaneously with as many as eight unwholesome karmapathas. With wholesome karmapathas it may occur with as many as ten, but not with exactly one, five, or eight.

See: [VAK_4.81.md](./VAK_4.81.md)

---

## Bhāṣya Source Unit

The Bhāṣya opens by saying that the discussion of karmapathas is still continuing and asks:

> With how many karmapathas may one `cetanā` arise and occur simultaneously?

For the unwholesome side it then explains the possibilities one by one:

- with one;
- with two;
- with three;
- with four;
- and analogously with five, six, and seven;
- with eight as the maximum.

For the wholesome side it first states the general rule that intention may occur with as many as ten wholesome karmapathas, and then gives an exception rule:

> not with one, eight, or five.

The prose then lists the possible wholesome totals: two, three, four, six, seven, nine, and ten, together with the specific contexts in which they occur.

---

## Literal Prose Translation

“This discussion still concerns karmapathas. Here it must be stated: with how many karmapathas does intention arise and occur simultaneously?

With unwholesome karmapathas it occurs simultaneously with as many as eight.

First, it occurs with one. [This happens] without another [mental karmapatha], when covetousness and the like become manifest by themselves; or, for one whose mind is not afflicted by these, when through the corresponding preparation one among the material karmapathas reaches completion.

It occurs with two: when one whose mind is hostile kills a living being; or when one possessed by covetousness commits taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, or disordered speech.

It occurs with three: when one whose mind is hostile simultaneously kills another’s living being and takes [the other’s property]. But then, would it not follow that taking what is not given does not reach completion through greed alone? That rule is to be understood when the act is completed by one whose mind is not otherwise [determined]. Or, when one possessed by covetousness and the like causes two material karmapathas to reach completion through the corresponding preparation, there are precisely three.

It occurs with four: when one whose intention is to divide [others] utters false speech or harsh speech. Here one is mental and three are verbal. Or, when one under covetousness and the like causes three other material karmapathas to reach completion through the corresponding preparation.

In this way the cases with five, six, and seven are to be worked out.

It occurs with eight when, after having made preparation regarding six [courses], one personally commits sexual misconduct and they reach completion simultaneously.

Thus much for the unwholesome.

With wholesome karmapathas intention occurs with as many as ten. Having stated this as the general rule, [the Kārikā] makes an exception: not with one, eight, or five.

It does not occur with one, eight, or five karmapathas.

It occurs with two in the five wholesome sense-knowledges, and also in an immaterial attainment in the knowledges of exhaustion and non-arising.

It occurs with three in mental knowledge associated with right view.

It occurs with four when one with an unwholesome or indeterminate mind undertakes the lay or novice restraint.

It occurs with six when, in the five wholesome sense-knowledges, that undertaking [of restraint] is present.

It occurs with seven in wholesome mental knowledge when that undertaking is present; and also when one with an unwholesome or indeterminate mind undertakes the monastic restraint.

It occurs with nine in the five wholesome sense-knowledges when that undertaking is present; and in mental knowledge associated with the knowledges of exhaustion and non-arising, and in that same [mental knowledge] when included in dhyāna.

It occurs with ten elsewhere in wholesome mental knowledge, and also in the undertaking of monastic restraint.

Every intention accompanying dhyāna-restraint or non-outflow restraint [occurs with the corresponding wholesome courses], apart from the knowledges of exhaustion and non-arising.

But apart from restraint, it could occur even with one [wholesome course], when one whose mind is otherwise undertakes abstention from a single factor; and it could also occur with five or eight in wholesome mental knowledge when two-factor or five-factor abstention is undertaken simultaneously.”

## Argument Reconstruction

### 1. The problem is simultaneity, not possession

The question is not:

```text
How many karmapathas can a person possess?
```

but:

```text
With how many karmapathas can one cetanā
arise and occur at the same time?
```

The topic is therefore event-level co-presence:

```text
one cetanā-event
    +
several simultaneously operative karmapathas
```

This must be kept distinct from `samanvāgama`, enduring endowment with a karmapatha.

### 2. Unwholesome combinations range from one to eight

The Bhāṣya does not merely state an upper bound. It explicitly constructs the series:

```text
1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8
```

The examples show how mental and material courses combine.

A simple case:

```text
vyāpāda
    +
prāṇātipāta
    = 2
```

A more complex case:

```text
divisive intention
    +
false speech
    +
divisive speech
    +
harsh speech
    = 4
```

The maximum eight is obtained when six courses have been prepared and sexual misconduct is personally completed, producing simultaneous completion of the full relevant set.

### 3. The earlier completion-root rule is not contradicted

The Bhāṣya notices an apparent problem:

If theft can occur together with hostile mind, how can VAK 4.70 have said that taking what is not given is completed through greed?

The reply is precise:

> `ananyacittasya tatsamāptau sa niyamo jñeyaḥ`

The earlier rule applies where the act is completed by a mind not otherwise determined.

Thus:

```text
characteristic completion-root
    ≠
all possible co-present mental determinants
```

The system distinguishes:

```text
formal niṣṭhā-rule
from
complex simultaneous conjunction
```

This is a significant refinement of 4.70.

### 4. Wholesome conjunctions obey a different combinatorial structure

The wholesome side is presented through:

```text
utsarga
    general rule:
    up to ten

apavāda
    exception:
    not 1, 5, 8
```

Within the main restraint-based scheme, the allowed totals are:

```text
2
3
4
6
7
9
10
```

The exclusions are therefore structural, not arbitrary.

### 5. But even the exclusions are context-dependent

The prose then qualifies the verse further.

Outside the specific restraint-combination scheme:

- one wholesome karmapatha can occur through undertaking one-factor abstention;
- five or eight can occur through simultaneous undertaking of two-factor or five-factor abstention.

Therefore the Kārikā’s `naikāṣṭapañcabhiḥ` must not be universalized beyond the context under discussion.

This is one of the most important Bhāṣya corrections to the first-pass reading.

## Voice Identification

**Voice: Vasubandhu / expository Bhāṣya** — frames the question and supplies the case analysis.

**Voice: Objector** — implicit at the objection:

> Would it not then follow that taking what is not given is not completed through greed alone?

**Voice: Vasubandhu / Vaibhāṣika resolution** — restricts the earlier completion-root rule to the uncomplicated completion case.

No major external-school polemic occurs in this unit.

## New Technical Terms

- **yugapat** — simultaneously, in one event-level conjunction.  
  **Status:** present in Kārikā; now operationalized in Bhāṣya.  
  **Function:** defines co-presence of one `cetanā` with several karmapathas.

- **utsarga** — general rule.  
  **Status:** Bhāṣya-only in this unit.  
  **Function:** states the default wholesome maximum of ten.

- **apavāda** — exception to a general rule.  
  **Status:** Bhāṣya-only in this unit.  
  **Function:** restricts the default rule by excluding certain totals in the specific scheme.

- **saṃmukhībhāva** — becoming manifest/present.  
  **Status:** previously encountered in Abhidharma contexts.  
  **Function:** marks actual manifestation as opposed to mere endowment.

- **saṃvara-samādāna** — undertaking a restraint.  
  **Status:** Bhāṣya technical expression.  
  **Function:** explains many wholesome numerical configurations.

- **ekaṅga-virati** — abstention from one factor.  
  **Status:** Bhāṣya-only here.  
  **Function:** shows that one wholesome karmapatha can occur outside the verse’s main restraint-combination context.

## Doctrinal Determination

### One intention can organize many determinations

The central result is:

```text
one cetanā
    ≠
one karmapatha
```

A single practical intention can unify several karmic determinations at once.

### The combinations are constrained, not arbitrary

The Bhāṣya never treats karmapathas as freely combinable independent units.

Their co-occurrence depends on:

- which mental course is manifest;
- which bodily/verbal courses are being completed;
- whether the act is in preparation or completion;
- whether restraint is being undertaken;
- which type of knowledge is occurring;
- whether the context is ordinary, meditative, or non-outflow.

Thus cardinality is an expression of structure.

### The wholesome and unwholesome sides are asymmetric

Unwholesome:

```text
possible totals in the principal enumeration:
1–8
```

Wholesome:

```text
principal restraint-based enumeration:
2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10
```

Yet the prose later permits 1, 5, and 8 in other contexts.

Therefore the verse is not giving a context-free arithmetic law.

## Logical Determination

VAK 4.81 is a doctrine of **constrained compossibility**.

The correct form is not:

```text
Karmapatha A exists.
Karmapatha B exists.
Therefore A and B can co-occur.
```

Rather:

```text
A can co-occur with B
only under determinate conditions
of root, phase, object, restraint,
and completion.
```

The Bhāṣya therefore distinguishes:

```text
individual possibility
from
joint possibility
```

This is a genuinely logical result.

The earlier root-classification is preserved through contextual restriction:

```text
formal completion rule
    remains valid

while

complex event composition
    permits additional co-present determinants
```

So a determination can be formally classified one way while participating in a richer concrete event.

## Translation Consequences

The first-pass Kārikā statement:

> “wholesome totals of one, five, and eight are excluded”

needs qualification.

A better Bhāṣya-informed formulation is:

> In the principal wholesome conjunction scheme under discussion, intention occurs with up to ten wholesome karmapathas, but not with exactly one, five, or eight; however, the prose explicitly allows those totals in other abstention contexts.

Likewise, the eightfold unwholesome maximum should be understood as a maximum of **simultaneously manifest karmapathas under one intention**, not total karmic endowment.

---

## Light Organon Contact

The useful logical form here is simply:

```text
possible determination
    ≠
possible conjunction
```

The Bhāṣya is specifying a space of jointly realizable determinations.

That is close to a logic of compossibility:

```text
individual predicates
    require compatibility conditions
before forming one concrete event
```

The stronger Organon lesson is methodological rather than analogical:

```text
general rule
    → exception
    → contextual qualification
```

The concept is therefore not adequately understood until the Bhāṣya has mediated the apparent universal claim through its concrete conditions.

## Carry-Forward

VAK 4.81 establishes:

1. `cetanā` may coexist with several karmapathas at once.
2. Unwholesome conjunctions are explicitly generated from one through eight.
3. The maximum eight concerns simultaneity, not possession.
4. Earlier completion-root rules remain valid through contextual restriction.
5. Wholesome conjunctions are governed by an `utsarga/apavāda` structure.
6. The verse’s exclusions of one, five, and eight are context-bound, not absolute.
7. The next verse shifts from **how many courses may coexist** to **which karmapathas occur or are possessed in the different destinies**.
