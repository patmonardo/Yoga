# The Ordinary Logic of Samyama

## A report on Yoga Sutra III.1-22, Hegel, and Vyasa

## Purpose

This report records a constructive reading of the first twenty-two Sutras of Yoga III alongside Hegel's *Doctrine of the Concept* and Vyasa's *Yoga Bhasya*.

The central thesis is:

> Yoga Sutra III.1-22 presents an Ordinary Logic of Samyama. It begins with the elementary act of cognition, develops the subjective and objective transformations of Citta, and culminates in the Idea as the unity of cognition and its objectivity.

This is not a historical claim that Patanjali anticipated Hegel or that Hegel secretly practiced classical Yoga. It is a systematic Varttika: Patanjali controls the sequence, Hegel articulates its immanent logical movement, and Vyasa is evaluated as a commentator upon that same sequence.

The governing correspondences are:

```text
Kapila-Kant       articulation of the cognitive instrument
Patanjali-Hegel   immanent transformation of the instrument into the Idea
Sankara-Fichte    genetic recognition of Absolute Knowing as nondual Artha
Vasubandhu-Kosa   Practical Reason and the analysis of cultivated actuality
```

The present report concerns the Patanjali-Hegel relation and Vyasa's interpretation of it.

## Method: commentary without rearrangement

The Hegelian text is not cut apart and rearranged to manufacture similarities. The order of Hegel's *Doctrine of the Concept* is preserved:

```text
Concept
Judgment
Syllogism

Mechanism
Chemism
Teleology

Life
Cognition
Absolute Idea
```

The Yoga Sutras are placed in their own order within that uninterrupted movement. The translation of each Sutra is the active hinge: it must remain answerable to the Sanskrit while making its logical office visible.

This method distinguishes three levels:

1. Patanjali's Sanskrit and its lexical possibilities.
2. Vyasa's historical commentary.
3. The constructive Hegelian Varttika.

The third level does not pretend to be the first or second. Its validity depends upon whether it explains the ordered necessity of the Sutras better than an isolated paraphrase does.

## The complete architecture

```text
YS III.1-3    Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi
              The originary cognitive act

YS III.4-8    Samyama and Prajna-Aloka
              The act becomes a conscious method

YS III.9-12   Citta-Parinama
              Subjectivity:
              Concept, Judgment, Syllogism

YS III.13-15  Bhuta-Indriya Parinama
              Objectivity:
              Mechanism, Chemism, Teleology

YS III.16     Knowledge of Parinama
              Life

YS III.17-20  Sabda, Artha, Pratyaya, Samskara,
              Para-Citta, and Alambana
              Cognition

YS III.21-22  The conditions of manifestation
              Absolute Idea
```

The structure can be compressed into one formula:

```text
Subjectivity : Objectivity -> Idea
     Citta    :   Caitta    -> self-comprehending Samyama
```

Here `Caitta` is used constructively for the determinate content or objective side correlated with Citta. This is broader than the standard Buddhist use of *caitta* for mental factors and should always be stated explicitly.

## I. The originary cognitive act: III.1-8

### III.1: Dharana

`desa-bandha cittasya dharana`

Dharana binds Citta to a `desa`, but this locus need not be merely a physical coordinate. It is a determinate place within the conceptual map of Citta. A bodily point, chakra, sound, image, or principle may index that locus, but the act is cognitive localization:

> Dharana gives Artha a determinate position within Citta.

### III.2: Dhyana

`tatra pratyaya-eka-tanata dhyanam`

Dhyana is the continuity of one Pratyaya within that locus. The determination is no longer a momentary placement; it becomes a sustained cognitive current.

### III.3: Samadhi

`tad eva artha-matra-nirbhasam svarupa-sunyam iva samadhih`

In Samadhi, Artha-matra shines while the merely subjective form appears empty. This is not the destruction of cognition. It is the adequacy of subjective form to objective content.

### III.4-8: Samyama

Dharana, Dhyana, and Samadhi together are Samyama. Mastery produces `prajna-aloka`, the illumination of Prajna. Samyama is applied across successive grounds, is more inward than the preceding limbs, and is nevertheless external relative to Nirbija Samadhi.

Hegel's initial Concept supplies the general form: cognition establishes a determination, sustains it, and becomes adequate to its subject matter. Patanjali supplies the actual discipline through which this form becomes a transformation of Citta.

## II. The first triad: Citta-Parinama, III.9-12

The subjective triad is:

```text
Nirodha-Parinama
Samadhi-Parinama
Ekagrata-Parinama
```

### III.9-10: Nirodha-Parinama and the Concept

`vyutthana-nirodha-samskarayor abhibhava-pradurbhavau`

`nirodha-ksana-citta-anvayo nirodha-parinamah`

Nirodha-Parinama is the overcoming and manifestation of the Samskaras of Vyutthana and Nirodha, together with Citta's continuity through the Nirodha moment.

The decisive term is `citta-anvaya`. Citta does not disappear in Nirodha. It continues through the transformation. Vyutthana is therefore not simply killed; it is sublated: negated as dispersion, preserved as content, and raised into a governing unity.

Hegel's Universal Concept provides the logical form. The Universal remains itself within the differences it produces. `Citta-anvaya` is the self-linkage that preserves one Citta through opposing Samskara tendencies.

III.10 gives the result:

`tasya prasanta-vahita samskarat`

From its Samskara comes a tranquil flow. The result is not blank cessation but `prasanta-vahita`: universal simplicity flowing through its determinations without being fragmented by them.

#### Vyasa on III.9-10

Vyasa usefully distinguishes Samskara from Pratyaya. The stopping of a present Pratyaya does not annihilate its latent Samskara. He explains that Vyutthana Samskaras diminish while Nirodha Samskaras are deposited.

His account becomes weaker when Citta is characterized chiefly as `samskara-sesa`, a Citta with Samskara remaining. This prepares a quietist interpretation in which Yoga approaches residual latency rather than a positive self-ordering of cognition. He correctly identifies the terms but does not yet comprehend their Concept.

### III.11: Samadhi-Parinama and Judgment

`sarva-arthata-ekagratayoh ksaya-udayau cittasya samadhi-parinamah`

Samadhi-Parinama is the decline of Sarva-Arthata and the arising of Ekagrata.

`Sarva-Arthata` is not mere sensory distraction. It is the dispersion of Meaning across mutually external loci. Each Artha solicits Citta independently. Ekagrata is not staring at one impoverished point; it is the objective unity under which the manifold becomes one cognition.

```text
Sarva-Arthata                   Ekagrata

A   B   C   D                       One
|   |   |   |                      / | \
separate solicitations            A  B  C
```

Kant's objective unity of apperception clarifies the Samkhya instrument here. Ahamkara is not only egoistic self-assertion; it is the determinative function of Judgment, the subsumption of the particular under the universal.

Hegel goes further. Judgment is the Concept's own act of positing determinate concepts. The many Arthas are not foreign contents pressed together by an external yogi. They are differentiations of one Concept whose unity becomes explicit.

#### Vyasa on III.11

Vyasa says that Sarva-Arthata and Ekagrata are both Dharmas of Citta; one disappears, the other appears, and Citta persists as their Dharmin. This is accurate but philosophically thin. It records a change of properties without explaining why the decline of dispersed Artha necessarily becomes the objective unity called Samadhi.

### III.12: Ekagrata-Parinama and Syllogism

`tatah punah santoditau tulya-pratyayau cittasya ekagrata-parinamah`

The subsided and arisen Pratyayas are `tulya`, equal or equivalent. Their equality cannot be contained in either isolated moment. A mediating unity must relate the past Pratyaya to the newly arisen one.

```text
subsided Pratyaya -- Universal Artha -- arisen Pratyaya
```

Kant-Samkhya identifies Buddhi as Reason: the determination of the particular from the universal. Each new Pratyaya is generated according to one governing principle.

Hegel identifies this structure as Syllogism. The Universal is the middle term connecting distinct extremes. Ekagrata is therefore not mechanical repetition of the same mental image; it is the self-reproduction of one Artha across distinct cognitive moments.

#### Vyasa on III.12

Vyasa says that one Pratyaya subsides, a similar Pratyaya arises, and Samadhi-Citta continues through them until there is a fall from Samadhi. He describes the succession but does not explain its inference. Resemblance restates `tulya`; it does not explain the mediating ground of equality.

## III. The second triad: objective Parinama, III.13-15

The objective triad is:

```text
Dharma-Parinama
Laksana-Parinama
Avastha-Parinama
```

The two triads form a dyad of models:

| Citta: subjective transformation | Caitta: objective/content transformation |
|---|---|
| Nirodha-Parinama | Dharma-Parinama |
| Samadhi-Parinama | Laksana-Parinama |
| Ekagrata-Parinama | Avastha-Parinama |

The exact pairings remain open to refinement. The governing discovery is that Subjectivity and Objectivity exhibit corresponding triple structures.

### III.13: Mechanism

`etena bhutendriyesu dharma-laksana-avastha-parinama vyakhyatah`

The crucial word is `etena`: by this. The transformations demonstrated in Citta also explain transformation in Bhuta and Indriya.

```text
Citta-Parinama
      |
      | etena
      v
Bhuta-Indriya Parinama
```

This is Hegel's transition from Subjectivity into Objectivity. The completed Syllogism steps forth as an objective system. At first its members appear externally connected: eye and form, ear and sound, organ and object. This is Mechanism.

#### Vyasa on III.13

Here Vyasa becomes substantially stronger. He distinguishes:

- Dharma-Parinama: one determination subsides while another manifests;
- Laksana-Parinama: a Dharma passes through future, present, and past characterization;
- Avastha-Parinama: the relative strength or condition of a Dharma changes.

He adds that no Guna-Vritti remains without transformation for even one moment, that the three transformations are ultimately one Parinama, and that Dharma is the articulated form of the Dharmin itself. This is a genuine philosophy of transformation, not a lexical paraphrase.

### III.14: Chemism and the Dharmin

`santa-udita-avyapadesya-dharma-anupati dharmi`

The Dharmin continues through Dharmas that are:

- `santa`: subsided or past;
- `udita`: arisen and presently active;
- `avyapadesya`: not yet designable as a present manifestation.

The Dharmin is not an inert substrate behind its properties. It is the unity immanent within its differentiated powers.

Hegel's Chemism is the correct objective form because relation and determinateness now belong to the object's own nature. The present state contains an intrinsic tension toward unrealized determinations.

#### Vyasa on III.14

Vyasa defines Dharma as the power of the Dharmin delimited by a particular capacity:

`yogyata-avacchinna dharminah saktir eva dharmah`

He argues that manifestation is conditioned by place, time, form, and cause. The Dharmin contains more capacities than its present state displays.

Most importantly, he invokes recognition:

`vastu-pratyabhijnanac ca sthito anvayi dharmi`

Through recognition of the object, the enduring and continuous Dharmin is established. Memory, Karma, and recognition across altered states would be unintelligible if there were only disconnected momentary Dharmas.

This is Vyasa's strongest contribution to the sequence and an unexpected foundation for a Pratyabhijna reading of Parinama.

### III.15: Teleology and Krama

`krama-anyatvam parinama-anyatve hetuh`

Difference of sequence is the cause of difference of transformation.

Krama is not empty succession. The same field of powers produces different actualities when its moments are ordered differently.

```text
same Sakti-field + different Krama = different Parinama
```

Hegel's Teleology makes explicit what is only implicit in the word `krama`. When a result determines the significance and order of its preceding moments, sequence becomes purposive. The final Artha is active throughout the process as its governing principle.

Samkhya provides the concrete instrument. The external Indriyas are gates; Manas, Ahamkara, and Buddhi are gatekeepers; Purusha-Artha supplies the end for which the system operates.

#### Vyasa on III.15

Vyasa illustrates Krama through clay becoming powder, lump, pot, shards, and particles. He applies sequence to Dharma, Laksana, and Avastha and observes that a Dharma can itself function as Dharmin relative to another Dharma. Ultimately, where Dharma and Dharmin are treated as non-different, Krama appears as one continuous process.

Vyasa provides an excellent genetic chronology. Hegel supplies the missing question: why this order rather than another? Teleology makes the immanent result the ground of the ordered process.

## IV. The Idea of Life: III.16

`parinama-traya-samyamad atita-anagata-jnanam`

From Samyama on the three transformations arises knowledge of past and future.

The philosophical meaning is genetic knowledge. The past is preserved as the completed process by which the present became actual. The future is present as Sakti, the determinate range of transformations implicit in present actuality.

```text
present actuality
= preserved genesis
+ active self-relation
+ directed possibility
```

Hegel places this Sutra under Life. A living Dharmin generates its own determinations, preserves itself through them, and contains both its history and its development within its present organization.

Samyama now takes Parinama itself as its locus. Knowing turns upon its own formative activity. This is the threshold at which the Science of Knowing becomes known.

#### Vyasa on III.16

Vyasa briefly says that direct Samyama on the three transformations produces knowledge of past and future. He does not explain how temporal totality is immanent in the present or why knowledge of transformation produces genetic knowledge. After his strong III.13-15 discussion, this is a surprisingly minimal gloss.

## V. The Idea of Cognition: III.17-20

These four Sutras complete a compact Citta-Caitta analysis:

```text
III.17  structure of a cognitive event
III.18  genetic continuity through Samskara
III.19  another Citta disclosed through Pratyaya
III.20  Pratyaya distinguished from its Alambana
```

### III.17: Sabda, Artha, and Pratyaya

`sabda-artha-pratyayanam itaretara-adhyasat sankarah`

`tat-pravibhaga-samyamat sarva-bhuta-ruta-jnanam`

Sabda, Artha, and Pratyaya are ordinarily confused through mutual Adhyasa:

```text
Sabda      articulated sign
Artha      intended object or Meaning
Pratyaya   determinate cognitive presentation
```

Samyama performs `pravibhaga`, precise differentiation without fragmentation. Nonduality cannot mean an immediate mixture in which every distinction is ignored; that would be `sankara`. A higher unity requires that each moment first become determinate.

#### Vyasa on III.17

Vyasa offers a sophisticated philosophy of language. The ear receives successive sounds, but no isolated phoneme is the complete word. Buddhi gathers the vanished and present sounds into one unified word-cognition. The final sound activates a synthesis informed by memory and linguistic Samskara.

This analysis is directly important for OM-Artha:

```text
A subsides
U arises
M completes the sequence
      |
      v
OM appears as one Buddhi-Artha
```

The phonetic Krama is temporal and divisible; the word as grasped in Buddhi appears as one Meaning. Vyasa also clearly distinguishes the external Artha as Alambana from both Sabda and Pratyaya. This is another of his strongest comments.

### III.18-20: Samskara, Para-Citta, and Alambana

III.18 makes Samskara directly cognizable and thereby discloses prior formation. III.19 shows another Citta through its Pratyaya. III.20 immediately restricts the claim: the Pratyaya does not automatically reveal its Alambana, because that support was not the object of Samyama.

This distinction is essential:

```text
Pratyaya is evidence of Citta,
but Pratyaya is not identical with its object.
```

Hegel's Idea of Cognition supplies the systematic frame. The Concept is liberated from immediate Life and confronts itself in objective form. Subjective cognition and objective content must be distinguished before their unity can be comprehended.

## VI. The Absolute Idea: III.21-22

### III.21: the relational generation of appearance

`kaya-rupa-samyamat tad-grahya-sakti-stambhe`

`caksuh-prakasa-asamprayoge antardhanam`

Visibility requires a complete relation:

```text
Kaya-Rupa       determinate bodily form
Grahya-Sakti    its power of being apprehended
Prakasa         illuminating medium
Caksus          receptive instrument
Samprayoga      effective conjunction
Appearance      the realized result
```

The body is not annihilated when its appearance is suspended. Its capacity to enter a particular perceptual synthesis is arrested. Even when the paranormal claim is bracketed, Patanjali supplies a rigorous model of manifestation: appearance belongs neither to the object alone nor to the subject alone, but to their realized relation.

Hegel's Absolute Idea is the identity of theoretical and practical cognition. Theoretical cognition knows the conditions of appearance; practical cognition intervenes in their realization. In Samyama, knowing the relation and determining the relation become one act.

#### Vyasa on III.21

Vyasa follows the operational chain accurately: Samyama on bodily form arrests its apprehensibility, prevents the conjunction of eye and illumination, and produces invisibility. He does not develop the general theory of appearance implicit in the Sutra.

### III.22: generalization across the sensible field

`etena sabda-adi-antardhanam uktam`

Again `etena` is decisive: by this same principle the disappearance of Sabda and the other sensible qualities is explained.

The model is universal:

```text
objective power
+ sense-specific medium
+ cognitive instrument
+ effective conjunction
= determinate appearance
```

The Ordinary Logic ends where it began. III.1 established a determinate cognitive locus; III.21-22 explain how a determinate object becomes manifest or fails to become manifest within such a locus.

### Textual note

The Hegel Varttika follows a recension that counts `etena sabda-adi-antardhanam uktam` as III.22. Some editions treat it as part of the Bhasya to III.21 and number the Karma Sutra as III.22. The twenty-two-Sutra architecture in this report follows the recension used by the project files.

## Vyasa: final assessment

Vyasa is neither dismissed nor treated as automatically authoritative. His commentary varies greatly in philosophical force.

### Strongest contributions

- distinguishing Samskara from present Pratyaya at III.9;
- the threefold Parinama analysis at III.13;
- Dharma as the determinate Sakti of the Dharmin at III.14;
- recognition, memory, and Karma as evidence of an enduring Dharmin;
- Krama and the relativity of Dharma-Dharmin at III.15;
- the synthesis of phonemes into one word-cognition at III.17;
- the distinction among Sabda, Artha, Pratyaya, and Alambana.

### Weakest contributions

- reducing III.11 to the disappearance of one Citta-Dharma and appearance of another;
- describing similar Pratyayas at III.12 without explaining their universal mediation;
- stating the result of III.16 without deriving genetic knowledge;
- treating III.21 chiefly as an operational Siddhi rather than a universal account of manifestation.

The fair conclusion is:

> Vyasa often preserves the ontology and phenomenology required by Patanjali, but Hegel more consistently reconstructs the necessity connecting one determination to the next.

## Why this is the Ordinary Logic

The first twenty-two Sutras do not merely list meditation techniques and powers. They derive the form of knowing:

```text
Conceptual locus
-> sustained Pratyaya
-> shining of Artha
-> Samyama
-> subjective Parinama
-> objective Parinama
-> Life
-> Cognition
-> generated Appearance
```

This is called Ordinary Logic to distinguish it from the later Transcendental Logic of Vivekaja and Taraka Jnana.

After III.22, much of Yoga III concerns determinate applications to domains of Nature and Spirit: Karma, Maitri, bodily organization, force, light, hearing, celestial structures, elements, and cognitive powers. These are not philosophically unimportant; they are the application of the logical instrument already derived.

The final movement of Yoga III requires a different commentator:

```text
Ordinary Logic       Patanjali-Hegel
Applied sciences     domains of Nature and Spirit
Transcendental Logic Vivekaja/Taraka Jnana
Genetic completion   Sankara-Fichte with Samkhya
```

Hegel carries Samyama through the Idea. Fichte is required where Knowing must derive and recognize the genesis of its own appearance. Samkhya supplies the articulated instrument, and Sankara supplies the nondual recognition of the whole movement as Atman's Artha.

## Final thesis

The result of the first twenty-two Sutras can be stated succinctly:

> Samyama is the Concept comprehending its subjective and objective Parinamas as one Idea.

Or in the project's central formula:

```text
Citta : Caitta -> Idea
```

Citta is not killed. It becomes capable of preserving itself through its transformations, recognizing the Artha transmitted across successive Pratyayas, and comprehending the conditions through which its world appears.

The Ordinary Logic of Yoga is therefore a Science of Citta becoming alive to its own Idea.
