# The Power Does Not Come from Outside

## A report on Yoga Sutra IV.3 and Fichte

## Purpose

This report tests a constructive reading of Yoga Sutra IV.3 alongside the
sixteenth lecture of Fichte's 1804 *Science of Knowing*.

The experiment depends upon the translation. The translation must remain
answerable to the Sanskrit, yet it must also make the Sutra's philosophical
claim plain enough for Fichte's text to appear as a genuine commentary upon
it. The test is not whether Yoga and Fichte use the same vocabulary. They do
not. The test is whether Fichte articulates the same relation of causality:
an external occasion does not manufacture an effect whose power lies outside
it; it removes an obstruction so that an immanent power can exhibit itself.

This is a systematic Varttika, not a historical claim that Patanjali knew
transcendental philosophy or that Fichte was explaining Yoga.

## The Sutra

`nimittam aprayojakam prakrtinam varana-bhedas tu tatah ksetrikavat`

### A close translation

> The occasion does not impel the powers of nature; from it comes only the
> breaking of their barrier, as when a farmer opens a channel.

### A simpler translation

> An outside cause does not produce nature's transformation. It only removes
> what blocks it, like a farmer letting water into a field.

The first version is preferable for the commentary. "Occasion" preserves the
limited office of `nimitta` better than the unrestricted word "cause."
"Impel" renders the force denied by `aprayojaka`: the occasion does not set
the natural powers to work or supply their efficacy. `Prakrtinam` is plural,
so "powers of nature" leaves room for nature's several productive tendencies.
`Varana-bheda` is literally a breaking or opening of a barrier. The farmer
does not create the water and does not give it the power to flow. The farmer
opens the bank; the water moves by its own power.

The distinction can be stated in one line:

```text
external occasion -- removes the barrier --> immanent power acts
```

The Sutra therefore rejects a picture in which transformation is imposed upon
an inert nature by an independently productive agent.

## Fichte's commentary

Fichte begins from an insight into self-enclosed living being. If nothing can
stand outside being, then the ground of being's construction cannot be placed
outside it. Being, insofar as it is constructed at all, must construct itself.

This conclusion is directed against a tempting account of idealism:

```text
the knowing subject constructs being
therefore
being depends upon the knowing subject
```

For Fichte, this leaves an unexplained gap between the subject performing the
construction and the being constructed. It quietly presupposes the external,
objectified existence that it claims to explain. The philosopher then appears
to stand outside being and to make being valid by constructing it.

Fichte replaces this picture with a more radical one:

```text
being cannot get outside itself
therefore
if being is constructed, being constructs itself
```

"We" certainly perform the construction, but not as agents independent of
being. We perform it insofar as we coincide with living being. The real ground
of the construction remains wholly immanent to being; it cannot be located in
our external act of reflection.

Read as a Varttika on IV.3, the philosophical subject is the `nimitta`. Our
act of construction is an occasion for insight, but it is `aprayojaka`: it
does not produce or impel the living power that it knows. The power belongs to
being itself. Philosophical reflection removes a barrier—the projection of a
gap between knowing and being—and thereby allows being's self-construction to
become evident.

The Fichtean farmer does not manufacture truth. The farmer opens the channel
through which truth shows its own life.

## The correspondence

| Yoga Sutra IV.3 | Fichtean Varttika |
|---|---|
| `nimitta`, the occasion | the reflective act performed by "we" |
| `aprayojaka`, not impelling | reflection does not produce or ground being |
| `prakrtinam`, of nature's powers | living being's immanent self-construction |
| `varana-bheda`, breaking the barrier | abstraction from objectification and the projected gap |
| `ksetrikavat`, like a farmer | the philosopher opens access but supplies neither being nor its power |

This is not merely a resemblance between two images. The sequence of
relations is the same:

1. A transformation appears to be attributable to an agent.
2. The agent is denied the office of productive ground.
3. The genuine power is relocated within that which transforms.
4. The agent's office is redefined as removing an obstruction.

Fichte's argument can therefore be heard as answering the question left
compressed in the Sutra: if the occasion does not produce the transformation,
where does the effective power reside? His answer is that no power can enter
living being from beyond it. The ground must be being's own immanent life.

## What the reading changes

The ordinary translation can sound like advice about indirect action: do not
force a process; create favorable conditions and let it happen. That sense is
present, but Fichte discloses a stronger claim. The limitation of the external
cause is not merely practical modesty. It follows from the nature of reality.
If the ground of transformation came from outside, the transforming nature
would not truly possess the transformation as its own.

The Sutra thus becomes an elementary doctrine of essence. Immediate
appearance says, "the farmer caused the field to be irrigated." Reflection
distinguishes two offices concealed in that statement:

```text
the farmer opens the barrier
the water supplies the motion
```

The apparent cause is only the condition. The essential ground is the power
that persists through the transformation and realizes itself when its
restriction is removed.

Fichte intensifies this distinction by applying it to knowing itself. The
philosopher's construction cannot be the external cause of truth. A true
construction is possible only if knowing is already the self-exhibition of
living being. Method clears the channel; it does not create the light.

## Does the comparison hallucinate?

The comparison would become a hallucination if it made any of the following
claims:

- that `prakrti` simply means Fichte's absolute being;
- that Patanjali teaches transcendental idealism;
- that Fichte's "we" is literally the farmer intended by the Sutra;
- that both texts have the same account of liberation, nature, or selfhood;
- that lexical similarity proves historical or doctrinal identity.

None of those claims is needed. The correspondence is narrower and more
defensible: both passages deny productive independence to what first appears
to be the external agent and locate the real power of transformation within
the process itself.

There is also a real difference. In the Yoga setting, the Sutra concerns the
transformation of `prakrti` and limits the causal role of an occasion such as
merit or practice. In Fichte, the argument concerns the validity of knowing
and the apparent construction of being by consciousness. Yoga gives a model
of immanent natural efficacy; Fichte gives a genetic account of why knowing
must not claim to create the being it discloses.

The difference is productive. It prevents equivalence while allowing
commentary. Fichte does not translate the Sutra; he unfolds one of its possible
logical consequences.

## Provisional result

The experiment succeeds at IV.3. Its strongest formulation is:

> Transformation is not produced by an external act. The external act only
> opens the barrier; the power that transforms is already immanent in that
> which becomes.

Fichte adds:

> Knowing is true only when its own act is understood in this way. Reflection
> is not the creator of being but the opening in which being's self-construction
> becomes visible.

This gives Dharma Pada a precise starting point as a Doctrine of Essence.
Essence is not a second world hidden behind appearance. It is the immanent
power whose activity becomes intelligible when the merely external cause is
reduced to its proper office.
