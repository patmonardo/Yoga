# One Thing, Different Paths of Citta

## A report on Yoga Sūtra IV.15, Vyāsa, and Fichte

## The Sūtra

`vastu-sāmye citta-bhedāt tayor vibhaktaḥ panthāḥ`

### Simple translation

> Although the Thing is the same, Cittas differ; therefore the paths of the
> Thing and its cognition are distinct.

This translation makes explicit the two terms indicated by `tayoḥ`, “of the
two.” Vyāsa identifies them as `vastu` and `jñāna`: the Thing known and the
cognition that knows it.

- `vastu-sāmye`: while the Thing remains the same;
- `citta-bhedāt`: because there is difference among Cittas;
- `tayoḥ`: of those two—the Thing and its cognition;
- `vibhaktaḥ panthāḥ`: the path is divided or distinct.

The point is not merely that people have different opinions:

> The Thing has its path of transformation, while its appearance in a Citta
> has a cognitively conditioned path.

```text
                   one Thing
                       |
        --------------------------------
        |              |               |
     Citta 1         Citta 2          Citta 3
        |              |               |
   appearance A    appearance B     appearance C
```

The plurality of appearances does not divide the Thing into private objects.

## Why this follows IV.14

IV.14 first secured the Thing:

> The reality of the Thing consists in the unity of its transformation.

Only after `vastu-tattva` has been established can Yoga distinguish the path
of the Thing from the paths of the Cittas that know it.

```text
IV.14                              IV.15

one Guṇa-transformation            many conditioned Cittas
constitutes the Thing        →     encounter that same Thing
```

If the plurality of Cittas came first, each appearance might be dismissed as
the private construction of its own Citta. IV.14 blocks that result before
IV.15 raises the cognitive problem.

## Vyāsa's Bhāṣya

Vyāsa asks how we know that the object is not merely a mental construction.
His answer is direct.

One Thing becomes the common support of many Cittas. It is constructed
neither by one Citta nor by many Cittas acting together. It is
`svapratiṣṭha`—established in itself.

Yet cognitions of this same Thing differ according to Citta's conditions:

| Condition of Citta | Cognition of the same Thing |
|---|---|
| dharma | pleasant cognition |
| adharma | painful cognition |
| avidyā | confused cognition |
| right seeing | equanimous cognition |

This yields Vyāsa's decisive question:

> By which of these Cittas was the Thing constructed?

It cannot have been constructed by one, because the other Cittas are affected
by the same Thing. Nor can one Citta properly be modified by an object
imagined only by another. The Thing and cognition therefore differ as the
grasped and the grasping, and their paths contain no confusion.

Vyāsa adds the Sāṃkhya specification: the Thing is tri-Guṇa and changeful. It
enters into relation with different Cittas according to their conditioning
and becomes the cause of correspondingly formed cognitions.

## Two paths, not two isolated worlds

Vyāsa prevents two opposed reductions:

```text
naive realism
the Citta contributes nothing to Appearance

private idealism
the Thing is nothing but one Citta's construction
```

The Sūtra instead maintains two real paths:

```text
path of the Thing                 path of cognition

its Guṇa-transformation          the conditioned formation
as one Vastu                     of a particular Citta
              \                 /
               \               /
                one appearance
```

Appearance occurs through their relation, but relation does not erase their
difference.

## Fichte's Bhāṣya

Fichte returns to the proposition:

> The Light is a `from`.

At IV.14 he emphasized the disjunction within this `from`. Here he asks us to
attend more deeply to its essential qualitative oneness.

In its pure qualitative unity, the `from` is genesis. Because Light is
identical with the `from` and permeates its essence, Light in its second
power—its Appearance—is itself genesis.

Fichte gives the result in one difficult proposition:

> Genesis and seeing converge completely and unconditionally.

This does not mean that an empirical Citta creates the self-established Thing
by looking at it. It means that appearance in knowing is not a dead copy
added after the genesis. Seeing is itself the living genesis by which a
determinate appearance becomes manifest.

```text
not:

finished Thing  →  passive copy inside Citta

instead:

Thing's objective path  +  Citta's conditioned path
                         ↓
          genesis of a determinate appearance
                         =
                 an actual seeing
```

## The example of awakening

Fichte says that this convergence can be intuited in every transition from
lassitude to energy.

In lassitude, knowing may be present only as an assumed or habitual form. In
the transition to energy, insight becomes alive: knowing does not watch its
own genesis from elsewhere; the coming-to-light of the determination is the
seeing itself.

His preferred example is the inquiry into the premise that knowing is a
`from`. When asked to justify it, we recall that we ourselves knew within
that presupposition and were the knowing. The premise is validated through
the living actuality of seeing, not by an external proof added to it.

This is why the words are easy but the insight difficult. “Genesis and
seeing converge” must be enacted in energetic seeing rather than retained as
a proposition about seeing.

## Yoga and Fichte

| Yoga IV.15 | Fichte |
|---|---|
| the same Thing | the qualitatively one Light |
| difference among Cittas | different acts and determinations of Appearance |
| Thing and cognition have distinct paths | disjunction remains within the `from` |
| determinate appearance arises in their relation | genesis and seeing converge in Appearance |

There is a productive tension between them.

Yoga insists upon the distinction between the self-established Thing and its
cognition. Fichte insists that the actual seeing of Appearance cannot be
separated from its genesis. Together they yield a stronger statement:

> The Thing is not created by a private Citta, but its determinate appearance
> in that Citta exists only as an actual genesis of seeing.

## Citta-Dharma

Every Citta has a determinate history of Saṃskāra, Vāsanā, karma, and insight.
Those conditions shape how the same Thing becomes known.

```text
same Vastu
    |
    +-- Citta conditioned by attachment  → desirable appearance
    +-- Citta conditioned by aversion    → painful appearance
    +-- Citta conditioned by avidyā      → confused appearance
    +-- Citta disciplined by right sight → equanimous appearance
```

Yet the Thing's path is not reducible to any of these appearances. Because
its transformation is one, it can remain the same Thing across their
difference and can correct them. If there were only private appearances,
there would be no common standard by which confused cognition could become
right seeing.

This is the beginning of objective knowledge in the Yoga System:

> Mastery of Citta does not manufacture truth. It discriminates Citta's
> conditioned path from the path of the Thing so their relation can be known
> adequately.

## Provisional result

IV.15 gives Appearance two paths.

The Thing is one through its Guṇa-transformation. Cittas are many through
their distinct conditioning. A determinate appearance arises where these
paths meet, but neither path can be collapsed into the other.

Vyāsa states the objective safeguard:

> The common Thing is established in itself, not imagined by one or many
> Cittas.

Fichte states the genetic safeguard:

> In Appearance, seeing is not a passive duplicate of genesis; genesis and
> seeing converge.

Together they let us translate the Sūtra plainly:

> Although the Thing is the same, Cittas differ; therefore the paths of the
> Thing and its cognition are distinct.

IV.16 can now ask the necessary next question: if the Thing is independent of
one Citta, what would happen to it when that Citta did not cognize it?
