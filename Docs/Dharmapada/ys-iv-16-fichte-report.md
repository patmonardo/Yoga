# The Thing Does Not Depend on One Citta

## A report on Yoga Sūtra IV.16, Vyāsa, and Fichte

## The Sūtra

`na caika-citta-tantraṃ vastu tad-apramāṇakaṃ tadā kiṃ syāt`

### Simple translation

> The Thing does not depend upon one Citta—for when that Citta supplied no
> cognition of it, what would the Thing be?

`Eka-citta-tantra` means dependent upon, governed by, or subject to one
Citta. `Apramāṇaka` can mean not cognized, unverified, or unsupported by a
means of knowledge. The concluding `tadā kiṃ syāt` asks: what would it then
be?

The question is a reductio. Suppose a Thing existed only as the cognition of
one Citta:

```text
Citta cognizes       →  Thing exists
Citta does not       →  what could the Thing be?
```

If the second line annihilated the Thing, the object would disappear whenever
that Citta became distracted, slept, entered cessation, or simply looked
away.

The simple claim is:

> The absence of cognition in one Citta is not the absence of the Thing.

## Why this follows IV.15

IV.15 distinguished two paths:

```text
the path of the Thing     ≠     the path of its cognition
```

IV.16 tests whether that distinction is serious. If the Thing depended upon
one Citta, it would have no path of its own. Its existence would begin and
end with that Citta's awareness.

```text
IV.15        Thing and cognition have distinct paths
                              ↓
IV.16        therefore the Thing is not governed by one Citta
```

Objectivity here does not mean that cognition has no role in Appearance. It
means that the cognitive relation cannot be mistaken for the existence of
the Thing it makes known.

## Vyāsa's Bhāṣya

Vyāsa presses the counterfactual forcefully. If the Thing were dependent on
one Citta, then when that Citta was distracted or restrained, the Thing would
be untouched by cognition, unavailable to any means of knowledge, and not
possessed of an apprehended nature. What, then, would it be?

When the Citta later entered into relation with it again, where would the
Thing come from?

Vyāsa adds a wonderfully concrete objection. Parts of an object that are not
currently presented would not exist. If its back were not seen, it would
have no back; consequently even the front could not be apprehended as the
front of a complete object.

```text
what one Citta presently sees       the whole Thing

          front              ≠      front + unseen back
```

The Thing must therefore be independent and common, while Cittas operate
independently for different persons. Cognition occurs through the relation
of Thing and Citta.

Vyāsa finally assigns this cognition to the experience of Puruṣa. We need
not accept that final externalization in order to preserve his decisive
analysis:

> The Thing and Citta are independently determinate; knowledge occurs through
> their relation.

## What IV.16 does and does not establish

The Sūtra establishes independence from **one Citta**. It does not yet offer
a theory of a Thing wholly unrelated to all cognition.

```text
established:

Thing ≠ private production of this Citta


not yet established:

Thing = an unknowable object outside every possible relation to knowing
```

The language is exact. `Na caika-citta-tantram`: the Thing is not subject to
one Citta. Yoga protects common objectivity without needing to construct an
absolutely cognitionless world.

## Fichte's Bhāṣya

Fichte considers the emergence of a new awareness during energetic
reflection. It appears to be a new production: before the act, this awareness
was not there; through reflection, it becomes manifest.

He then asks us to abstract from every content of that awareness:

```text
determinate consciousness
          |
          | abstract from all content
          v
pure knowing / seeing / Light
```

What remains is not another content but the qualitative actuality of Light.
At the same time, this abstraction is itself an act of genesis or freedom.

Fichte's important result is that the two appearances are indivisible:

```text
appearance of pure Light
              ↕
appearance of the genesis that abstracts
```

Pure Light cannot appear without the abstraction from content, and the
abstraction cannot appear without arriving at pure Light. Each permeates the
other. Light therefore appears as permeating genesis—or as producing itself.

## Energy and manifestness

Fichte gives the same result more practically. We are instructed to reflect
energetically, and a new consciousness emerges.

The new consciousness does not occur without the energy; the energy does not
remain an external action to which consciousness is later added. They open
together indivisibly.

```text
energy of reflection       manifestness of insight
          \                    /
           \                  /
            one living event
```

We may initially divide the genesis:

- the energy seems to come from `me`;
- the manifestness seems to come from reason itself.

Fichte says this division has no validity in itself. Once it is abstracted
away, there remains an absolute, self-producing knowing that cannot occur
without genesis.

## Yoga and Fichte

The apparent tension is useful:

| Yoga IV.16 | Fichte |
|---|---|
| the Thing does not depend upon one Citta | a determinate consciousness arises with an energetic genesis |
| the unseen object is not thereby nothing | no appearance of pure Light occurs apart from abstraction |
| Thing and Citta enter a cognitive relation | energy and manifestness open together |
| objectivity survives private non-cognition | actual seeing is inseparable from its genesis |

Fichte is not claiming that my empirical Citta creates the Thing. His object
of analysis is the coming-to-presence of knowing. Yoga supplies the objective
limit: the Thing is not produced or destroyed by one Citta's cognitive act.

Together they give a precise account:

> The Thing does not depend upon my cognition, but its becoming manifest for
> me is inseparable from the genesis of my seeing.

## Practical Reason and the Kośa

IV.16 gives practical discipline an objective basis.

The Kośa analyzes how conditioned Cittas encounter All This. Practice alters
the Citta's dispositions, attention, affect, and action. It does not pretend
that suffering disappears merely because one Citta refuses to look at it.

```text
closing my eyes to suffering       ≠       suffering absent

transforming the path of Citta     =       changing how suffering is
                                            known and responded to
```

This is why the Immeasurables belong to Practical Reason. They train the
relation of Citta to a common world:

- Maitrī transforms hostility in the encounter with another.
- Karuṇā makes suffering practically present rather than avoidable by
  indifference.
- Muditā transforms the appropriation of another's flourishing.
- Upekṣā keeps cognition equal without making it insensible.

The Thing—or the suffering being—does not depend upon my Citta. But whether
it appears through attachment, aversion, confusion, or adequate practical
knowledge depends upon the path of my Citta.

Thus objectivity increases rather than diminishes practical responsibility.

## Citta-mastery

IV.16 blocks a false account of mastery:

> Mastery of Citta is not mastery by making the world disappear whenever
> attention is withdrawn.

It is mastery of the cognitive relation. Citta learns to discriminate:

```text
what belongs to the Thing's path
                  from
what its own conditioning contributes to Appearance
```

This discrimination is necessary for Kriyā-Jñāna. Knowledge of creation
requires respect for the independent determination of the created Thing;
otherwise “mastery” becomes private fantasy.

## Provisional result

IV.15 established that the same Thing can appear differently because Cittas
differ. IV.16 prevents that difference from swallowing the Thing:

> The absence of cognition in one Citta is not the absence of the Thing.

Vyāsa supplies the realist test: the unseen back of the object does not cease
to exist when only its front is presented.

Fichte supplies the genetic account of manifestation: when a new seeing does
arise, energy, genesis, and manifestness open together as one living event.

The result is both theoretical and practical:

> The world is not the private product of Citta; nevertheless Citta is
> responsible for the path through which that world becomes manifest in
> knowledge and action.

IV.17 can now determine the positive condition: an object is known or unknown
according to whether it colors, or enters into relation with, Citta.
